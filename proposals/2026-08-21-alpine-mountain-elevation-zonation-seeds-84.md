# Ouros Alpine Mountain & Elevation Zonation Seeds — Pass 84

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research patterns. No external plot or character is transplanted.

## 1. The Treeline That Split in Two

Two surveys of the same mountain disagree about treeline. Both are correct: opposite aspects now support different upper limits after years of changing snow persistence and disturbance.

## 2. The Last Patch of Summer Snow

A late snowbed that normally persists into summer disappears early. The first problem is determining whether this is a one-year anomaly or a real change in mountain state.

## 3. The Hut Above the Clouds

A small shelter becomes a repeated social node for patrols, researchers, rivals and lost travelers. Its importance grows through use rather than because it was built as a major institution.

## 4. Gogoat Route Without Gogoat

A herd historically observed along one slope stops using the route. The path remains intact. Players need to distinguish route change, forage change, disturbance, seasonal movement and bad assumptions about herd territory.

## 5. Three Paths to the Same Pass

A formal trail, an old service track and an informal wildlife route all reach the same saddle. Each has different knowledge, maintenance and access state.

## 6. The Wrong Side of the Mountain

A forecast is regionally accurate but a lee slope remains sheltered while the windward route becomes unusable. Travelers who treat the mountain as one weather cell make poor choices.

## 7. Avalugg on the Old Road

A large wild Avalugg repeatedly uses a snow-covered service road. Its route use is documented, but nobody assumes the Pokémon is blocking the road deliberately or should be captured.

## 8. Summit Without a View

A summit expedition succeeds physically but arrives inside cloud. The players still obtain valuable route, flora and instrument observations even though the expected panorama never appears.

## 9. The Broken Altimeter Archive

Historical expedition records use several inconsistent elevation measurements. The discrepancy becomes a research problem instead of proof that the mountain physically changed height.

## 10. The Shelter Everyone Forgot

An old patrol shelter remains usable but vanished from modern maps after a route reroute. Rediscovering it raises questions about maintenance, safety and whether it should return to public maps.

## 11. The Flowering Belt

An alpine flower blooms in a narrow elevation band for only a short period. The event attracts researchers and visitors, creating temporary route pressure without creating spawn bonuses.

## 12. The Wind-Shaped Grove

A patch of low, twisted trees survives where ordinary trees cannot. Old photographs show the patch slowly changing over decades, creating a natural long-term monitoring site.

## 13. The Cable Station That Lost Its Route

A cable station still functions mechanically, but the trail that once connected its upper terminal to the summit has become unreliable. Infrastructure and route access diverge.

## 14. The Pass That Became a Border in People's Minds

Two valleys treat the same saddle as a cultural boundary even though no formal jurisdiction was ever authored there. Public memory and geography have to remain separate.

## 15. The Mountain Spring Above the Map

A small spring appears in recent field reports above the elevation where older maps place reliable water. The discovery may matter to travel and ecology, but its long-term reliability is unknown.

## 16. The Empty Alpine Meadow

A meadow usually associated with a wild collective is empty during a survey. Absence becomes a research lead, not automatic evidence of decline.

## 17. Rest Station Number Four

A staging site gains an informal reputation because generations of Trainers leave field notes, repaired gear and observations there. Its archive becomes more valuable than its building.

## 18. The Route Marker Under the Snow

A route marker appears to have disappeared. Spring melt reveals it was never removed; snow redistribution buried it several meters from the visible winter path.

## 19. The Summit Relay's Quiet Year

A relay continues functioning but records far fewer contacts than expected. The cause could be route closures, changed travel patterns, technical logging or simply fewer expeditions.

## 20. The Wrong Herd Leader

Visitors repeatedly identify the largest Gogoat as the herd leader. Long-term observation suggests leadership changes and is not reliably inferred from size.

## 21. The Pass After the Fire

A lower montane wildfire changes the approach route while the alpine zone remains physically untouched. Years later, altered vegetation below treeline still changes how expeditions reach the upper mountain.

## 22. The New Treeline Trail

An informal path develops where visitors follow a shifting vegetation edge. The path becomes ecologically sensitive before any institution officially recognizes it.

## 23. The Mountain Seen From Two Towns

Two settlements tell different stories about the same peak because they use opposite slopes, different routes and different landmarks. Neither version needs to be false.

## 24. The Snowbed Fossil Find

A late-melting snowbed retreats enough to expose geological material. Geology and custody systems decide what the discovery means; the mountain layer only records where and when it was exposed.

## 25. The Ridge Where Sound Travels Too Far

A normally isolated staging site starts receiving sounds from a new facility in the valley. Soundscape and mountain topography together create a new observation problem.

## 26. The Weather Station Below the Weather

A summit station and a lower station report very different conditions during the same hour. Both instruments are functioning. The mountain's vertical structure matters.

## 27. The Route Open Only in Memory

Older residents still describe a traditional ascent that is now broken into several inaccessible segments. Cartography retains it historically without pretending it remains traversable.

## 28. A Herd Above Treeline

A wild herd is observed unusually high for one season. The system records the event without immediately redefining the species' permanent habitat.

## 29. The Mountain Festival That Never Reaches the Summit

A regional festival celebrates the mountain but takes place entirely in lower staging areas. Cultural importance does not require summit access.

## 30. The Summit That Changed Name

A summit acquires a new public name after a major expedition. The old name remains in archives, older maps and local speech. Renaming never rewrites historical records.

# Long Arc A — Five Years on Cloudstep Massif

Year 1 establishes vertical zones, routes, shelters and baseline wildlife observations.

Year 2 brings an unusual snow year and conflicting treeline observations.

Year 3 a lower-slope disturbance reroutes visitors and creates pressure on a different aspect.

Year 4 a research station is upgraded while an older hut loses regular staffing.

Year 5 repeated observations show some changes were temporary while others became persistent.

The arc's payoff is a mountain that genuinely accumulates history rather than a one-time summit quest.

# Long Arc B — The Two Valleys Expedition Network

Two settlements on opposite sides of one massif develop different route knowledge, staging infrastructure, scientific specialties and cultural interpretations of the mountain.

Players gradually connect the networks through shared surveys, rescue operations and map corrections. The ending can be cooperation, partial interoperability or continued distinct traditions.

# Long Arc C — The Moving Treeline Atlas

A multi-year atlas project compares photography, vegetation plots, snow records, fire history and route observations across several slopes.

Early interpretations conflict. Later evidence may show multiple causes operating simultaneously.

The arc deliberately allows null results and uncertainty. The final atlas is versioned knowledge, not a perfect model of the mountain.

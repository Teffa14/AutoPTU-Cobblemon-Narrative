# Ouros Worldbuilding Candidates — Pass 87 — Open Ocean, Oceanography & Pelagic Ecology

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros material derived from high-level research patterns. No external plot, character, dialogue or distinctive setting is copied.

## 1. The Front That Moved Overnight

A research vessel returns to yesterday's coordinates and finds no sign of the thermal front it mapped. The instruments are working. The front moved. The quest becomes locating the feature again, comparing methods and deciding which public map needs correction.

## 2. Wailord Watch Without Wailord

A long-running sightseeing route has a week with no Wailord sightings. Operators, tourists and researchers all interpret the absence differently. The event can still succeed as a safe tour while Science investigates whether the timing, route or population changed.

## 3. The Cold Ribbon

A narrow band of unexpectedly cold surface water appears offshore during a warm season. Mantyke behavior differs inside and outside it. The observation is real; the causal explanation remains open.

## 4. The Lost Mooring

A sensor buoy breaks loose during ordinary weather. Its last twelve hours of data look unusual. Players must recover it, reconstruct its drift and decide which readings are trustworthy after the station stopped holding position.

## 5. Three Samples, Three Depths

Surface plankton is sparse, the mid-depth sample is dense and the deeper sample is different again. A satellite-like visual survey would have missed most of the story.

## 6. The Productive Empty Sea

Ocean measurements suggest high productivity, yet fishing reports are poor. The explanation may involve timing, target species, depth, movement, observation bias or an incorrect assumption about how productivity should propagate upward.

## 7. The Wrong Side of the Front

Two fishing crews report contradictory catch conditions on the same day. Both are accurate because they worked opposite sides of a moving ocean front.

## 8. Full Moon Surface Watch

Jellicent are expected near the surface during a lunar window, but this year's observations are inconsistent. Astronomy provides timing; Open Ocean provides surface state; Wildlife provides actual observations. None writes the others automatically.

## 9. The Plankton Tow That Changed the Map

A routine transect finds a larval cohort far from the assumed nursery. Researchers open a connectivity study linking offshore currents with a reef and estuary, but the origin remains a hypothesis until more evidence appears.

## 10. Driftwood from Somewhere Else

A recognizable crafted object is recovered far offshore. Its discovery position, origin, loss event and current transport become separate records. It may be evidence for an old wreck, a recent storm or something much less dramatic.

## 11. The Warm Pool

A temporary patch of warmer water appears around a research route. Pelagic Pokémon observations shift toward its edge. The feature later disappears without producing a crisis or Legendary encounter.

## 12. The Current Everyone Knows

Sailors use a named current in everyday route planning. Academic charts describe it differently because they measure at another scale. The disagreement becomes an opportunity to reconcile practical and instrument knowledge rather than decide one side is ignorant.

## 13. The Silent Plankton Station

A station that normally records a seasonal bloom goes offline. The region must choose whether to issue a cautious notice, send a vessel immediately or wait for independent observations.

## 14. A Bloom That Is Not Harmful

Green water near a fishery causes public concern. Sampling confirms a bloom but finds no evidence that it is the harmful type people feared. The correction still has to travel through media, markets and public memory.

## 15. A Warning Before the Water Turns Green

Instruments and samples indicate a developing bloom before any obvious visual sign appears. Fishers, clinic staff and tourism operators receive different versions of the same advisory according to their needs.

## 16. The Old Transect

A retired scientist's repeated sampling line crosses a current system that has shifted over decades. The old dataset becomes valuable because the methods were consistent even though the ocean never was.

## 17. The Ferry in the Eddy

A ferry's schedule remains reliable, but a recurring eddy changes fuel/time assumptions enough to matter operationally. The travel service adapts without requiring a dangerous storm.

## 18. Remoraid Without Mantine

A school of Remoraid appears where researchers expected Mantine associations. The absence prevents the system from assuming a permanent partnership structure.

## 19. The Nursery That May Be Offshore

Juvenile observations near an estuary no longer line up with the assumed spawning area. A multi-season project tests whether an offshore front is transporting early-life stages toward the nursery.

## 20. The Deep Signal

A sensor repeatedly detects biological activity below the depth sampled by routine surveys. The first quest is not to fight whatever is there, but to improve observation coverage safely.

## 21. Surface Calm, Strong Current

The sea looks calm from shore while a strong current regime is active below or farther offshore. Visual weather and ocean movement remain separate systems.

## 22. The Tourist Front

A moving productivity feature becomes famous because Wailord and other pelagic Pokémon are often seen nearby. Visitor vessels begin following the same feature as research and fishing fleets, creating crowding without any fixed attraction site.

## 23. The Research Vessel That Arrived Too Late

By the time the vessel reaches yesterday's hotspot, the front has weakened. The failed sampling day still produces useful negative evidence and informs future scheduling.

## 24. The Two Models

Two oceanographic teams predict different front positions. Neither is dishonest. Their models use different assumptions and update windows. A field expedition can compare both against observations.

## 25. The Offshore Cold Year

One season produces unusually cold offshore observations without a corresponding cold spell on land. Fisheries, tourism and Mantyke surveys respond differently.

## 26. The Current Carries the Rumor

Debris from a well-known event washes up in a distant region. Public discussion treats its arrival as proof that something happened nearby, while Cartography and Oceanography show that the material could have traveled for weeks.

## 27. The Broken Chain of Samples

A set of plankton samples is scientifically interesting but has incomplete custody/provenance after a rushed emergency transfer. The data may still be useful, but the uncertainty must stay attached.

## 28. The Offshore Refuge

During a coastal disturbance, several pelagic collectives concentrate in an area that is normally unremarkable. The region later investigates whether the place was a true refuge, a temporary coincidence or an observation artifact.

## 29. The Front Beyond the Map

An old public chart ends just before a recurring productive front. Fishers and local guides know the area well, while official maps treat it as mostly empty open water. A new survey can connect local knowledge with versioned institutional data.

## 30. Ten Years of Water

A regional archive preserves buoy records, plankton samples, ferry logs, sightings, fishery data and research transects across a decade. The value comes from comparing years rather than generating a single definitive ocean state.

# Longer arc A — The Moving Blue Line

Year 1: researchers map a recurring offshore front used by fishers and wildlife-watch operators.

Year 2: the front forms later than expected. Public maps lag behind the observations.

Year 3: a new mooring network improves coverage but reveals more variability rather than a simpler pattern.

Year 4: larval samples suggest a connection between the front and a nearby reef/estuary nursery system.

Year 5: tourism grows around the feature. Conservation and Maritime begin reviewing vessel concentration rather than the front itself.

Years 6–8: warm and cold years shift the front differently. Old datasets become more valuable.

Later: the region understands the front better without ever reducing it to a static line or guaranteed wildlife spawn.

# Longer arc B — The Upwelling Years

A coastal/open-ocean region experiences recurring seasonal upwelling.

Early arcs focus on repeated temperature and plankton surveys.

Later, Fisheries records better catches in some years and poor results in others despite apparently similar upwelling conditions.

Researchers discover that timing, stratification, smaller currents and observation coverage matter.

A strong tourism industry eventually grows around pelagic wildlife sightings, forcing the region to coordinate Science, Maritime, Conservation and Public Space without turning the ocean feature into a theme park.

# Longer arc C — From Reef to Open Sea and Back

A restoration program at a reef struggles to explain variable recruitment.

Plankton surveys identify possible offshore transport pathways.

An estuary study finds another potential nursery connection.

Years of observations gradually build a regional connectivity graph linking currents, fronts, larvae, reefs and estuaries.

Some hypotheses fail. Others survive repeated tests.

The arc's payoff is a better world model, not a single boss or revelation.

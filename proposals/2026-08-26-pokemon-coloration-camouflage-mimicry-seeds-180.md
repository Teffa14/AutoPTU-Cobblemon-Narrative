# Ouros Seeds — Coloration, Camouflage & Mimicry — Pass 180

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANON
Date: 2026-08-26

These concepts extend the Pass 180 coloration layer. They do not establish species behavior, mechanics or canon by themselves.

## 30 worldbuilding and quest candidates

1. The Pokémon Was in Every Photograph

A long-running camera series is re-reviewed after someone notices that the same Kecleon-shaped distortion appears in frames previously catalogued as empty background. The story becomes a correction to detection history, not a retroactive population increase.

2. The Wall Changed, Not the Pokémon

A warehouse district is repainted. A local population that formerly blended with brick now becomes conspicuous. Public reports of a sudden population boom rise even though telemetry and sightings suggest the same individuals were present before.

3. The Sudowoodo That Fooled the Survey

A vegetation survey repeatedly logs the same apparent sapling. A later inspection identifies a persistent Sudowoodo. The original survey remains valid as an observation record but the classification is corrected.

4. Three Backgrounds, One Kecleon

Researchers document one known Kecleon on bark, wet stone and painted concrete. Its hue changes differently in each setting. The study remains open because concealment, physiological condition and social context all correlate with color.

5. The Famous Warning Pattern

A brightly marked local Pokémon is widely described as toxic. Toxicology finds no supporting evidence. The pattern may still function socially or defensively, but public memory lags behind the science.

6. The Pattern Appears Only at Dusk

A visible flank marking seems seasonal until Timekeeping and Lightscapes show that observers were sampling only one twilight window. The phenotype was present throughout the season.

7. The New Form Headline

A photograph of an unusually colored Pokémon goes viral. Taxonomy holds the classification at `UNRESOLVED`; later observations support context-dependent appearance rather than a distinct Form.

8. The Old Guide Was Right

A century-old field guide describes a color pattern no modern observer sees. Climate, vegetation and land-use history suggest the habitat background changed enough that selection or behavior may now favor another appearance pattern. No evolutionary mechanism is assumed without evidence.

9. The Background-Matching Nursery

Juveniles use a patchy nursery area where apparent detectability changes dramatically by substrate. Wild Nesting and Visual Records track site use while Coloration records appearance-context matching.

10. The Bright Individual Survives

A conspicuous individual persists for years despite assumptions that camouflage must always be advantageous. The case becomes a standing reminder that visibility alone does not determine fitness.

11. The Rock That Walked Away

A trail closure begins after visitors repeatedly disturb what they think is a decorative stone formation. The “rock” is a masquerading Pokémon using the same rest site.

12. The Public Art Problem

A mural accidentally reproduces the visual pattern used by a local Pokémon population. Visitors begin misidentifying wall markings as sightings and actual individuals as part of the mural.

13. The Photographer Effect

A rare-looking color state appears disproportionately in professional photos because photographers seek one specific substrate and time of day. Community Science later exposes the sampling bias.

14. The False Target

A species has a conspicuous rear-body marking that observers repeatedly interpret as an eye. Researchers record predator/approach behavior without assuming a mechanical targeting penalty.

15. The Two Populations Look Different

Two valleys report different “local forms.” Standardized imaging reveals much of the difference came from lighting, substrate and seasonal covering state. Taxonomy still keeps a residual difference open for study.

16. The Mimicry Everyone Explains Differently

A Pokémon resembles a common manufactured object. Merchants, historians and biologists maintain three incompatible origin stories. None is promoted to world truth.

17. The Clean Room Color Shift

A research station notices repeatable color changes in one Pokémon after entering a controlled interior. Care, Lightscapes and Coloration jointly test whether lighting, stress, temperature or another factor is responsible.

18. The Burn Scar Mismatch

After wildfire, a population that blended with the old understory becomes easier to detect on charred ground. Recovery monitoring records how detectability changes again as vegetation returns.

19. Snow Came Late

A population with a recurring pale winter appearance becomes conspicuous during an unusually delayed snow season. Climate and Seasonal Coverings own the timing; Coloration owns the visibility mismatch.

20. The Signal Nobody Notices

Humans focus on a dramatic color patch while repeated observations suggest another subtle pattern is the one associated with social responses. The famous feature may be irrelevant to the behavior people assumed it explained.

21. The Urban Counterfeit

A business starts selling decorations based on a local Pokémon’s pattern. The objects create false-positive visual reports across the district.

22. The Species That Uses Trash as Background

Urban Wildlife documents individuals resting among discarded materials whose colors match their bodies. Waste/Sanitation changes collection practices, unintentionally altering detectability and site use.

23. The Unreliable Silhouette

Fog and backlighting make several species look nearly identical. A sequence of confident but conflicting visual identifications becomes a training case for institutions studying evidence quality.

24. The Masquerade That Became a Landmark

A persistent Pokémon spends years at the same roadside site while residents treat it as part of the scenery. When it eventually stops appearing, people initially believe the landmark itself was removed.

25. The Former Partner in Plain Sight

A released former partner is possibly photographed near an old route. Distinctive coloration supports an identity hypothesis but does not prove it. Pokémon Agency keeps the case unresolved until stronger evidence appears.

26. The Pattern Changes After Injury

A known individual develops a temporary color difference during recovery. Care records health state; Coloration records correlation. The layer never diagnoses the injury from color alone.

27. The Museum Reconstruction Was Too Bright

A museum model of a historical population uses colors inferred from faded images. New archival material prompts a revised reconstruction without changing the original photographs.

28. The Decoy Background

A Pokémon repeatedly arranges leaves or debris around a resting site. Cognition records the object manipulation; Coloration records the resulting visual effect; Social Learning remains open unless transmission is observed.

29. The Pattern Survived the City

An urban population retains a forest-associated pattern for decades after habitat conversion. Researchers debate whether behavior, development, ancestry or sampling explains the persistence.

30. Nothing Happened at Observation Point Six

A year of standardized visual surveys finds no unusual appearance shifts. Years later, that ordinary dataset becomes the best baseline for evaluating a genuinely changed pattern.

## Longer arcs

### Five Backgrounds of Meridian Kecleon

Year 1 establishes a known individual in a wooded district.

Year 2 roadwork changes substrate and lighting.

Year 3 observers report a supposed new color state.

Year 4 standardized trials show that detection and identification vary strongly by background.

Year 5 a health-related color correlation appears, forcing Care and Coloration to separate concealment from condition.

The arc can remain scientifically mixed rather than ending with one universal explanation.

### The Pattern Atlas

A regional field institute spends decades assembling appearance records from photographs, museums, community science and living observations. Early plates mix lighting artifacts, seasonal states and true biological differences. Each edition becomes more careful about provenance. The atlas itself becomes historical evidence of how the region learned to see Pokémon.

### The Mimic That Outlived Its Model

A Pokémon population resembles a once-common human-made object or structure. The original object gradually disappears from everyday life, but the resemblance remains. Younger residents start believing the Pokémon inspired the object rather than the reverse. Archives, Public Memory and Taxonomy preserve the unresolved chronology.

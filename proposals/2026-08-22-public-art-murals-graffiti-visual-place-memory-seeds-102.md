# Ouros Public Art, Murals, Graffiti & Visual Place-Memory Seeds — Pass 102

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Every name, place, actor, artwork and history below is original candidate material for Ouros and may be revised or rejected.

These candidates require review against established Ouros canon, PTU/Caelo rules and implementation readiness before promotion.

## 1. The Wall Beneath the Wall

A restoration crew cleaning a station mural finds a much older painted layer beneath it. The older work is authentic, but nobody yet knows whether it was official, community-made or simply painted before the current station existed.

Hooks: archives, architecture, rail history, material provenance, public debate.

## 2. The Pattern That Keeps Moving

Researchers document the same distinctive Grafaiai-like pattern on trees farther and farther from an old forest edge over several seasons. The hypothesis that one individual is responsible remains unresolved until photography, material analysis and field signs are compared.

Hooks: Pokémon identity, tracking, forest ecology, photography.

## 3. The Mural That Became a Landmark

Residents give directions using a painted wall long before any map records it. A redevelopment project keeps the building but proposes covering the wall with insulation, forcing the city to decide whether a practical navigational landmark deserves preservation.

Hooks: cartography, public space, architecture, civic governance.

## 4. Three Signatures

A famous collaborative mural has three signatures, but oral histories name five contributors. One signature may have been added during a later restoration rather than at original creation.

Hooks: authorship, archives, photography, public memory.

## 5. The Railway Paint Years

A rail retaining wall accumulates layers of authorized murals, unsanctioned tags, maintenance paint, warning stripes and festival decorations across twenty years. A new platform project needs a record of what should be documented before demolition.

Hooks: rail, museums, photography, civic works.

## 6. The Wrong Historic Scene

An old civic mural is unquestionably authentic but depicts a local disaster in a way contradicted by newer archival evidence. The question is whether to correct the wall, add context or preserve it as evidence of how the event was once remembered.

Hooks: public memory, history, museums, media.

## 7. The Blank Square Project

A newly rebuilt plaza has a large blank wall. Several legitimate proposals compete: a memorial work, a children's project, a rotating temporary wall, a wild-Pokémon observation panel and no artwork at all.

Hooks: governance, public space, accessibility, community participation.

## 8. The Artist Who Refuses the Commission

A respected local artist declines a high-profile sponsor-funded mural because the proposed subject does not match the neighborhood's history. The refusal becomes more famous than the project itself.

Hooks: finance, sponsorship, media, artist career.

## 9. Smeargle Practice Alley

A workshop uses washable panels for art practice alongside trained Smeargle. Over time, one panel becomes historically important because it preserves years of student experiments and Pokémon marks.

Hooks: education, downtime, mentorship, museums.

## 10. The Overpaint Nobody Authorized

A beloved wall changes overnight. Witnesses disagree whether it was vandalism, a continuation by the original artist, an emergency maintenance coat or an authorized revision whose paperwork never reached the public office.

Hooks: Cases, permissions, communications, evidence.

## 11. The Festival Mural That Never Comes Down

A work created for a one-week festival becomes so embedded in local identity that teardown is repeatedly postponed. Years later nobody agrees whether it is still temporary.

Hooks: festivals, public memory, maintenance, tourism.

## 12. The Toxic Color Question

A Grafaiai's mark changes color across the year. Researchers test diet, season, habitat and individual identity hypotheses without treating the paint as a combat hazard.

Hooks: ecology, food systems, science, Pokémon agency.

## 13. The Mural Route Boom

A travel article links several public artworks into a walking route. The route succeeds commercially, then produces crowding, litter, noise and pressure around a quiet residential courtyard.

Hooks: tourism, waste, public space, housing.

## 14. The Children's Version

A school paints a simplified version of a well-known local legend. Years later visitors mistake the school mural for the oldest surviving depiction and repeat its simplified details as history.

Hooks: education, myth, media, misinformation.

## 15. The Wall With No Artist

A striking old geometric work has no known author. Multiple living artists are incorrectly credited by tourism material because their later styles look similar.

Hooks: attribution uncertainty, archives, tourism, correction.

## 16. The Restoration Test Patch

Conservators apply several small test treatments to a damaged wall. One patch unexpectedly reveals an older pigment layer, changing the entire restoration plan.

Hooks: materials, science, conservation, decision-making.

## 17. The Harbor Signal Painting

A large painted symbol once helped workers identify a warehouse from boats before modern navigation aids. It is now fading, but sailors and residents disagree over whether its practical purpose ended.

Hooks: maritime, geomagnetism/navigation history, archives, public art.

## 18. The Quiet Protest Wall

A public wall accumulates small unsanctioned statements over months. The marks express several incompatible positions, and the system preserves each layer without declaring the wall a unified political actor or assigning beliefs to everyone nearby.

Hooks: public space, media, governance, provenance.

## 19. The Former Partner's Mark

A released Smeargle-like former partner is suspected of contributing marks to an outdoor wall years later. Even if identity is confirmed, the observation does not imply the Pokémon wants to return to its previous Trainer.

Hooks: Pokémon agency, photography, tracking, release history.

## 20. The Flood Line Mural

After a flood, residents deliberately preserve a visible waterline and later incorporate it into an artwork documenting recovery. A later repaint risks removing both the artwork and a useful physical record of the event.

Hooks: freshwater, crisis recovery, public memory, material evidence.

## 21. The Underpass Gallery

A once-unpleasant pedestrian underpass gradually becomes a rotating community-art space. Better use increases foot traffic, which later changes lighting, maintenance schedules and wildlife presence.

Hooks: urban heat/light, road ecology, public space, tourism.

## 22. The Painting on the Old Gym

A retired Gym building carries a mural of a former challenge era. The institution moved years ago, but challengers still visit the wall and sometimes confuse its imagery with current rules.

Hooks: battle institutions, public memory, adaptive reuse.

## 23. The Mural That Predates the Neighborhood

Demolition records reveal that a mural currently considered neighborhood art was moved panel-by-panel from an older district. The work belongs to the current community now, but its provenance complicates a centennial celebration.

Hooks: demography, adaptive reuse, archives, festivals.

## 24. The Paint That Wasn't Paint

A bright mark on a rock face is first cataloged as human pigment, then as Pokémon residue, then as a mineral staining process. Each correction preserves the earlier interpretation history.

Hooks: geology, science, Pokémon marks, visual evidence.

## 25. The Community Map Wall

Residents maintain a large hand-painted neighborhood map showing shortcuts, closed paths, informal names and seasonal warnings. It is extremely useful but not authoritative cartography.

Hooks: cartography, public space, travel, local knowledge.

## 26. The Memorial Addition

A long-standing public artwork receives a small new panel after a later disaster. Some residents see this as continuity; others see it as changing the meaning of the original work.

Hooks: memorials, crisis recovery, governance, public memory.

## 27. The Missing Panel

One detachable panel from a multi-part artwork disappears during building repairs. The panel may have been stored, discarded, stolen, loaned or moved without records. Treat it as a custody/provenance case rather than assuming theft.

Hooks: Cases, custody, material culture, construction.

## 28. The Wildlife Wall

A wall beside a conservation site is repainted every year with species observed during the previous season. One year a familiar species is missing from the artwork because it was not detected, prompting public alarm despite incomplete survey effort.

Hooks: conservation, field surveys, public communication.

## 29. The Unfinished Masterwork

A famous artist leaves a large work unfinished for ordinary reasons. After years, local mythology treats the missing section as a deliberate mystery. Surviving project notes suggest otherwise but remain incomplete.

Hooks: public memory, archives, myth formation.

## 30. One Surface, Five Generations

A single wall survives a school, warehouse, clinic, market and community center. Each period leaves some visible trace. The wall becomes a compact physical chronology of the district without needing any supernatural property.

Hooks: adaptive reuse, demography, workplaces, material history.

# Long Arc A — The Meridian Walls

Year 1: a city documents major shared surfaces before redevelopment.

Year 2: several works are restored; one is removed after structural review; one is moved; one is deliberately allowed to remain temporary.

Year 3: tourism concentrates around a subset of works and changes public-space use.

Year 4: new Pokémon-made markings appear on trees and retaining walls at the urban edge, creating an ecological research program rather than a vandalism panic.

Year 5: an archive exhibition compares original photographs, current surfaces and resident oral histories. The city discovers that no single “visual identity” ever existed; it was layered and changing.

Possible endpoints include a rotating mural program, protected heritage walls, community-managed surfaces or a decision to allow continual overpainting.

# Long Arc B — The Wall of Five Stories

A single large retaining wall contains five physically distinct layers:

- a utility marking from before the district existed;
- an early community painting;
- a later sponsored mural;
- an unsanctioned overpaint;
- a partial restoration.

Across several years, different institutions investigate materials, authorship and meaning. None of the layers is treated as automatic historical truth. Players can help document, stabilize, reveal, cover or reinterpret portions while Chronicle preserves every revision.

The arc is designed to produce conflicts of stewardship rather than a final boss.

# Long Arc C — Patterns in the Trees

Season 1: distinctive painted patterns are recorded in a forest near a settlement.

Season 2: additional marks appear along a changing ecological edge.

Season 3: diet/material analysis suggests variation in pigment source.

Season 4: photography and field signs support, but do not conclusively prove, that one persistent Grafaiai has authored many of the marks.

Season 5: redevelopment and visitor attention create pressure around the marked trees.

Season 6: the central question shifts from “who made these?” to “how should the place be managed now that people value the marks?”

Possible resolutions include protected observation areas, no special protection, temporary access limits, or leaving the phenomenon undocumented publicly to reduce pressure.

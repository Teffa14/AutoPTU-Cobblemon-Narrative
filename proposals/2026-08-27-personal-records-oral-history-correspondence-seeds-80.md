## The Notebook That Changes Its Mind

A field worker kept a notebook over many years.

Early entries describe a recurring Pokémon behavior one way. Later notes explicitly question that interpretation after better observations became available.

A modern researcher has been citing only the earliest pages because a copied excerpt stopped there.

Gameplay:

- locate the original notebook;
- compare dates and observation conditions;
- discover the author’s own later correction;
- update the institutional citation without deleting the historical mistake.

Persistence:

Future exhibits/reports can show how understanding changed over time.

## Three Letters, One Missing Reply

An archive holds three letters from one participant in a long correspondence but none from the recipient.

Possible explanations remain open:

- replies existed and were lost;
- replies remain private elsewhere;
- the recipient answered in person;
- there was no reply;
- the surviving bundle came only from one side.

The investigation can establish chronology without inventing the missing message.

## The Interview Everyone Quotes Differently

A respected older resident gave one recorded interview years ago.

Three later publications quote or summarize it differently.

The player can compare:

- the original recording if access exists;
- a verbatim transcript;
- an abridged public version;
- later citations;
- the resident’s own follow-up clarification.

The story is about derivation, not detecting a liar.

## The Page Found After the Decision

A missing journal page is recovered months after an important public project was approved.

The page contains information that would have mattered during the original debate.

It cannot undo the past.

Possible current consequences:

- review the project’s next phase;
- reinterpret why an earlier stakeholder objected;
- update a memorial/exhibit;
- reopen a research question;
- decide that the new information changes nothing operationally.

This seed enforces discovery-time provenance.

## The Interview Subject Says “Not Yet”

A community history project asks several residents to record memories of a major event.

One participant agrees to speak but restricts public access for now.

The useful state is neither “quest failed” nor “secret unlocked.”

Possible follow-up:

- store the session under restriction;
- revisit when the speaker changes their access instruction through an explicit event;
- use other sources meanwhile;
- keep the public exhibit incomplete rather than violating the restriction.

Exact consent/access rules remain a canon question.

## Two Journals, Same Week

Two people recorded the same week from different locations.

The entries appear contradictory until the player notices that they describe different time windows or different sections of the route.

Outcome:

Both sources remain valid within narrower scopes.

This is a reusable mystery pattern where contradiction creates precision instead of culprit-hunting.

## The Field Note in the Wrong File

A loose note was incorporated into the wrong institutional folder years ago.

The note itself may be genuine; its current context is wrong.

The player can reconstruct:

- handwriting/author provenance;
- date;
- expedition schedule;
- cited location;
- neighboring records;
- later reports that inherited the filing error.

Long-term consequence:

The institution corrects the catalogue while preserving the old classification history.

## A Family Story Gains a Date

A family/local story has been told for generations but lacks a reliable date.

A newly discovered personal ledger or letter independently mentions the same ordinary event alongside a dated delivery, festival, weather observation or workplace shift.

The new source narrows the time window without proving every detail of the oral tradition.

This seed can deepen family history without assigning ancestry to PCs or inventing inheritance.

## The Research Report Left the Rough Notes Behind

A respected institutional report has circulated for years.

The author’s original field notebook later enters an archive.

The rough notes reveal:

- observations omitted for space;
- tentative ideas correctly excluded from the report;
- an uncertainty that later citations forgot;
- a mundane failed method that explains why the final study used another approach.

The notebook does not automatically discredit the report. It restores context.

## The Translation That Became the Famous Version

A personal letter or diary survives only through a widely circulated translation while the original is difficult to access.

A later translator notices one phrase has several plausible readings.

Gameplay focuses on provenance:

- compare original and translation;
- identify which public claims depend on the ambiguous phrase;
- preserve both readings until evidence narrows them;
- update exhibits/publications without declaring the earlier translator fraudulent.

No language system or translation check is invented here.

## The Notebook Returned with New Margins

A personal notebook is loaned or shared and comes back with annotations from another reader.

The annotations are historically valuable but must remain visibly separate from the author’s original entries.

Possible conflict:

A later scholar mistakenly cites a margin note as the original author’s opinion.

Resolution can correct attribution while preserving the annotation as its own source.

## The Resident Remembers the Route Before the Road

An oral-history speaker remembers how people crossed an area before modern infrastructure existed.

Cartography/Public Works has old maps that only partially match the description.

The player can walk the remembered route and compare:

- surviving path traces;
- old maps;
- later modifications;
- repeated place names;
- ecological or property changes.

The speaker’s account becomes one evidence stream, not a GPS trace.

## The Personal Record That Becomes Public Slowly

Long-term arc.

Phase 1: private source.

A notebook, correspondence bundle or interview exists only under restricted access.

Phase 2: selective sharing.

A narrow claim is shared with one researcher or family/community group.

Phase 3: institutional citation.

A report or exhibit cites the source without exposing the complete private material.

Phase 4: new context.

Another source challenges or strengthens one part of the account.

Phase 5: revised access.

Through an explicit authorized event, more or less of the record becomes public.

Phase 6: public memory.

The community now remembers a simplified version. The archive retains the source history and access transitions.

Phase 7: later re-reading.

Years later, a new question makes an overlooked passage relevant.

The same source produces several waves of play without needing a new dungeon.

## Mystery — Seven Entries, Six Days

A diary series contains seven dated entries across what appears to be six calendar days.

Possible explanations:

- one date was copied incorrectly;
- an entry was written after midnight about the prior day;
- two entries describe morning/evening separately;
- a later compiler inserted an undated fragment at the wrong point;
- the local calendar notation was interpreted incorrectly;
- one entry is a later reconstruction.

The player compares handwriting/format, subject times, locations and related records.

The outcome can remain probabilistic. No forgery is required.

## Mystery — Four Interviews, Two Original Stories

Four residents tell apparently independent versions of an old incident.

Source comparison shows that two residents learned their account from the same older relative, while a third heard a published version derived from the same interview archive.

The fourth account is genuinely independent.

This reduces apparent corroboration without making anyone dishonest.

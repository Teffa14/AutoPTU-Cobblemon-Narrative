# Ouros Proposals — Organization / Faction Identity & Lineage Seeds — Pass 146

Status: NON-CANON. These are original Ouros-ready seeds derived from the Pass 146 research architecture. They do not establish any named faction, institution, law, organization type, or region as canon.

## Design aim

Use organizational continuity to make the world remember ordinary institutional history. The useful tension comes from time, provenance, disagreement, asynchronous records, inherited spaces, former members, and competing interpretations. A villain is optional.

The seeds below deliberately avoid importing external Pokémon plots, characters, uniforms, factions, dialogue, or distinctive scenes.

## Short quest and world-state seeds

### The Branch That Never Closed

A central organization records a rural branch as closed years ago. The local hall still opens twice a month. Three older members insist the branch merely became inactive and later resumed. A later treasurer started a new ledger without copying the earlier closure notice.

Possible truths:

- the branch remained the same organization through an inactive interval;
- former members created a new local organization under the same name;
- the central office's closure notice was never effective locally;
- evidence is insufficient and the historical relationship remains ambiguous.

Player work can involve gathering meeting minutes, notices, old membership records, site history, and testimony. Combat is unnecessary.

### Two Groups Use the Same Crest

Two neighboring organizations display nearly identical versions of a historic crest. Residents assume they are branches of one body. Their own records show no current parent relationship.

One may descend from the original association while the other inherited only a shared tradition. Both may also be legitimate descendants of a split.

The quest should reward precise language rather than forcing a winner.

### The Successor Nobody Agrees On

A dissolved community organization left behind a workshop, a small archive, unfinished service commitments, and strong local goodwill. Two descendants claim continuity for different reasons.

One inherited most former members. The other inherited the records and workshop lease. Neither fact alone settles succession.

Useful consequences:

- different NPCs recognize different descendants;
- vendors may have outdated counterparties;
- an old public notice may use the predecessor's name;
- a recurring service may continue even while the lineage question remains disputed.

### The Association Moved; the Workshop Stayed

A trade association relocates its meetings. The workshop that once hosted it keeps the old sign because the workshop itself still uses the historical name informally.

A courier, visitor, or new member arrives at the old site expecting the association.

This can intersect Place Reference, Courier, Public Notices, Workplaces, and organization lineage without any system overriding another.

### The Club Split Before the Festival

A local club divides into two groups several weeks before a public festival. The festival program was printed earlier and lists only the old club name.

The venue, organizer, both descendants, and longtime attendees now use different language.

The players can help update practical arrangements while leaving the historical succession question unresolved.

### Three Notices, Two Organizations

Three public notices appear to come from the same body because they use similar names and the same hall address. One notice predates a split. Two are post-split documents from separate descendants.

The useful puzzle is chronological, not cryptographic.

### The Former Member Still Has the Jacket

A respected former member still wears an old organization jacket while volunteering around town. New residents assume the person currently represents the group.

The NPC is not deceiving anyone. Their affiliation ended years ago, but the garment remained personally meaningful.

This seed reinforces `SYMBOL_PRESENT != CURRENT_MEMBERSHIP` and gives an ordinary social consequence to historical affiliation.

### The Merger Was Announced, Then Delayed

Two organizations publicly announce a merger. Signs are ordered, stationery is printed, and a joint event is scheduled. The effective transition is delayed because practical handoffs are incomplete.

For several weeks, the world contains:

- a future combined name;
- two still-active organizations;
- joint public events;
- separate staff and records;
- old contracts;
- confused residents.

No fraud is required.

### The Name Came Back Twenty Years Later

A new youth group adopts the name of a beloved organization that dissolved a generation earlier. Some founders are descendants of former members, but there is no continuous membership, archive, or governance chain.

Older residents disagree about whether this is a revival.

The system should support `NAME_REUSE`, `REACTIVATION_CLAIMED`, or `LINEAGE_RELATED_DISTINCT` depending on evidence and canon decisions.

### The Old Committee Still Meets

A formal committee was replaced by a new structure, but several former committee members still gather socially and continue one harmless volunteer activity.

Outsiders call them "the old committee." They no longer hold the old authority.

This is a useful test for separating social continuity from institutional continuity.

### The Chapter With No Parent

A remote chapter keeps operating after the parent organization dissolves. For years it continues using the inherited name and methods because nobody sees a reason to change them.

Later, a new region-wide organization forms and invites the chapter to affiliate.

The chapter's history now contains three periods: branch, independent survivor, new affiliation.

### The Shared Hall, Separate Ledgers

Two organizations use the same hall on different days. A volunteer accidentally files records from one group into the other's cabinet because both use the building name as a shorthand address.

The resulting mystery is resolved by provenance, not ownership assumptions.

### The Coalition Banner

Several independent organizations create a coalition for one seasonal project. They agree on a joint banner and public name for the project.

After the project ends, residents continue referring to the coalition as though it were a permanent parent organization.

This seed protects `COALITION_WITH != SUBORDINATE_TO`.

### The Branch Became Independent Quietly

A branch gradually stopped receiving instructions, stopped sending reports, and began making all local decisions itself. No single dramatic meeting marks the change.

Historians later disagree about the effective date of independence.

The lineage event can use a time interval rather than a fabricated exact timestamp.

### The Sign Was Correct in Both Years

A building photograph from Year A shows one organization name. A later photograph shows another. A local caption database flags one as an error because the system only stores a current name.

The player discovers that both captions were correct for their dates.

### The Spokesperson for Yesterday's Organization

A public spokesperson gives an interview shortly before a merger becomes effective. The interview airs afterward.

Viewers hear the old title even though the new organization now exists.

The record remains historically correct because speech time and publication time differ.

### The Archive Was Divided on Purpose

After a split, two descendants each receive parts of a predecessor archive according to subject and local relevance. Decades later, researchers assume one archive must be incomplete because they expect a single successor.

The actual answer is distributed provenance.

### The Name Nobody Owns

Several unrelated local groups use the same generic name in different towns. A regional directory later conflates them.

Correcting the directory should not require inventing a central organization.

## Mystery structures

### Five Times the Organization Was “the Same”

Five records use language implying continuity:

1. same visible name;
2. same leader;
3. same meeting hall;
4. same archive;
5. explicit predecessor/successor record.

Only the fifth directly asserts lineage, and even that assertion may be disputed. The mystery teaches players to separate evidence types.

Possible ending states:

- one continuous organization;
- several distinct but related organizations;
- one continuous organization plus a later name reuse;
- `ACCEPTED_AMBIGUITY`.

### Four Seals, Three Branches

An archive contains four seal designs associated with three historical branches. One branch changed seal after independence. Another reused the parent seal after affiliation ended. A third never had its own seal.

The puzzle should never resolve identity from the image alone.

### Who Inherited the Archive?

A predecessor dissolved. One successor received the building. Another received staff. A third was recognized by most former members. The archive stayed temporarily with a former secretary.

A later transfer request asks who "owns" the archive.

The correct procedural answer may be that lineage evidence cannot decide custody. The Archive/Custody owner must resolve that question separately.

### The Revival That Might Be a New Group

A historical name reappears with some descendants of old members, a recovered banner, and a new mission. No continuous records survive across the inactive period.

Clues support cultural lineage strongly and institutional continuity weakly.

The case may close with both facts recorded separately.

### Three Founding Dates

An organization publicly celebrates one founding year. Its earliest surviving records date from a later year. A predecessor association existed earlier under another name.

All three dates may describe different things:

- earliest predecessor activity;
- formal organization event;
- surviving documentary evidence.

No date must be declared a lie.

### The Branch That Exists Only in Other People's Records

A central archive, vendors, and public directories all reference a branch. No branch-local records survive, and current residents do not remember it.

Possible explanations include a short-lived branch, an administrative placeholder, a renamed local group, or a records error.

Use `UNKNOWN` rather than generating a branch history without evidence.

## NPC and faction archetypes

### The Continuity Keeper

A longtime volunteer remembers names, mergers, old meeting places, and former leaders. Their memory is valuable but should remain testimony with normal limits.

### The Practical Successor

A current organizer has inherited the predecessor's unfinished work and community expectations but does not care about claiming historical legitimacy.

### The Heritage Claimant

An organization deliberately presents itself as a revival or continuation. Its claim may be sincere, partially supported, disputed, or later confirmed.

### The Reluctant Archivist

A former officer still has boxes of records because no successor ever completed a formal handoff. The NPC wants the records preserved but does not want to decide the lineage dispute.

### The Branch Veteran

A person remembers when the local chapter answered to a distant parent and when that relationship faded. Their chronology can clarify independence without giving them universal authority.

### The Coalition Coordinator

An NPC represents a temporary joint project. They can speak for the coalition scope while lacking authority over each member organization's unrelated affairs.

## Exploration spaces

### The Layered Meeting Hall

Environmental storytelling can show decades of organizational use through patched sign mounts, old paint outlines, storage labels, stacked chairs, archived event posters, and rooms renamed at different times.

Minecraft presentation can display these details only after world state has selected them. Block placement never decides lineage.

### The Split Archive Room

A former shared archive contains duplicated folders, two numbering systems introduced after a split, and a shelf left under the predecessor's name because neither descendant wanted to relabel historical records.

This space supports non-combat investigation and later record-handoff quests.

### The Successor Depot

Equipment markings show several organizational eras. Some assets transferred. Others were loaned. A few belong to an unrelated group sharing the site.

The environment should invite questions without making paint colors authoritative.

## Long arc — A Town Learns Who Speaks for Whom

Status: NON-CANON long-form arc template.

### Phase 1 — Normal overlap

Several local organizations already coexist. They share volunteers, buildings, events, and practical relationships. Players learn these relationships through ordinary jobs rather than exposition.

### Phase 2 — Administrative friction

A rename, leadership transition, branch independence, or coalition creates small discrepancies:

- old signs;
- outdated directories;
- deliveries sent under former names;
- NPCs using different labels;
- overlapping membership lists;
- contracts or notices still referencing predecessors.

Nothing catastrophic occurs.

### Phase 3 — A real split or consolidation

One organization divides, affiliates, merges, or dissolves. Different assets and projects follow different paths. Some members move. Some do not. Public language lags behind reality.

Players encounter consequences through jobs, social relationships, archives, events, and world services.

### Phase 4 — Competing memory

Residents develop different narratives about what happened. One descendant values historical continuity. Another wants a clean break. Former members keep old symbols. A shared hall preserves traces of both.

The generator stores claims by source instead of choosing a universal public story.

### Phase 5 — Practical settlement

Most operational questions eventually settle: who runs which project, where meetings occur, which records transferred, and how public services interact with the descendants.

Historical identity can remain partially disputed.

### Phase 6 — Long memory

Months or years later, the town still contains old signage, inherited rituals, recurring NPC loyalties, archive gaps, jokes, grievances, and cooperation patterns. The world feels historical because the data was not reset when the organization changed.

## Encounter concepts

### Branch Archive Withdrawal Corridor

Narrative premise: people need a safe physical route to remove records from a contested site. Combat does not decide who owns the records or which organization is the legitimate successor.

Full version requires verified targeting and base movement, plus PARTIAL complete movement, lifecycle, stateful damage, statuses, move-specific behavior, abilities, items, Trainer Features/perks; BLOCKING terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions if doorway control is reactive; VERIFIED AI legal-action infrastructure; BLOCKING AI tactical policy and adapter/playback.

Reduced READY version: all archivists, records, custody semantics, and neutral actors leave BattleSpec. The archive becomes inaccessible scenery. Explicit combatants fight on a static corridor. Success returns only `IMMEDIATE_ARCHIVE_WITHDRAWAL_ROUTE_CLEAR`.

### Assembly Hall Split Perimeter

Narrative premise: descendants of one predecessor hold separate meetings while an external physical threat reaches the shared hall.

Full version may need timed phases, reactions at exits, interception, forced movement, status interactions, and tactical AI, so its rich form remains dependent on PARTIAL/BLOCKING families.

Reduced READY version: meetings evacuate before initiative. Combat occurs on a static exterior map. Success returns only `IMMEDIATE_ASSEMBLY_HALL_PERIMETER_CLEAR`.

### Shared Depot Handoff Chokepoint

Narrative premise: assets are being handed between organizations during a transition while hostile actors threaten the approach.

Full version would require escort/object-carry semantics, reaction ordering, forced movement, dynamic access control, and tactical AI if those elements occur inside combat.

Reduced READY version: staff and controlled assets exit BattleSpec. Vehicles/equipment remain static scenery. Success returns only `IMMEDIATE_DEPOT_HANDOFF_APPROACH_CLEAR`. Ouros performs custody and organizational handoff afterward.

## Consequence templates

Battle-safe immediate consequences:

- `IMMEDIATE_ARCHIVE_WITHDRAWAL_ROUTE_CLEAR`
- `IMMEDIATE_ASSEMBLY_HALL_PERIMETER_CLEAR`
- `IMMEDIATE_DEPOT_HANDOFF_APPROACH_CLEAR`
- `IMMEDIATE_MEETING_EXIT_CLEAR`
- `IMMEDIATE_SHARED_SITE_APPROACH_CLEAR`

World-only consequences that battle must never assert:

- organization identity resolved;
- successor confirmed;
- merger effective;
- leadership recognized;
- membership changed;
- authority transferred;
- archive custody transferred;
- contract inherited;
- debt inherited;
- facility ownership transferred;
- coalition formed or ended;
- branch independence established;
- dissolution confirmed.

## Canon questions deliberately left open

- Which concrete organizations exist in Ouros?
- Which are formal, informal, civic, commercial, religious, scientific, sporting, criminal, volunteer, or otherwise categorized locally?
- Does any region maintain an organization registry?
- What gives an organization public or legal standing, if such a concept exists?
- What titles, charters, constitutions, seals, membership practices, or succession conventions exist?
- Which groups use branches, chapters, federations, coalitions, committees, or councils?
- Which historical splits, mergers, dissolved groups, revived names, and disputed successors belong to canon?
- Which relationships are publicly known and which remain private or disputed?
- How long do records survive, and who maintains them?

Pass 146 supplies reusable structures without answering any of these questions silently.

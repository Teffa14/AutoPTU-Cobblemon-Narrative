# Ouros Memorial, Absence & Succession Seeds — Pass 62

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANON.

Nothing here establishes a real Ouros cemetery, funeral custom, inheritance rule, religion, afterlife claim, institution, settlement or historical death. Names are placeholders until continuity review.

## Design intent

These seeds make confirmed loss, unresolved absence and remembrance playable without turning death into a default battle consequence or converting memorial spaces into haunted dungeons by default.

The strongest recurring pattern is return: the same place, marker, route, role or routine should look different after new evidence, maintenance, correction or time.

## Seed — The Name That Is Still Missing

A small memorial marker contains a first name and date but no reliable surname or institutional affiliation.

Several residents believe they know who the person was, but their accounts share one old source. An archive index, work roster and transport record narrow the possibilities without producing certainty immediately.

Possible resolution states:

- identity confirmed and marker revised through an approved process;
- identity narrowed but still uncertain;
- public story proven to combine two people;
- marker intentionally kept minimal at the original commissioner's request.

No version requires combat.

## Seed — Empty Seat at Dawn

A familiar worker stops appearing during a routine early shift.

The service layer records the operational absence. Coworkers offer different explanations. The residence layer may show no current occupancy change. Communications may show unanswered contact attempts. Travel state may later confirm ordinary relocation or a delayed return.

The hook exists because the routine changed, not because the generator assumes tragedy.

If the actor later returns, the absence record remains part of history rather than being deleted.

## Seed — The Bell Without a Ceremony

A community maintains a memorial bell used by some visitors, but there is no single scheduled ceremony and no requirement that everyone participate.

One year a public event organizer assumes a formal anniversary exists and begins planning around it. Local records show that the practice has always been personal and irregular.

The conflict becomes one of public programming, privacy and historical accuracy rather than supernatural stakes.

## Seed — The Memorial Moved Once Already

A marker stands in a strange location relative to the current road.

Survey records reveal that the path changed years ago and the marker was moved once under an old works project. A new accessibility project now proposes another relocation.

The playable question is not “is moving it disrespectful?” as a universal rule. The group must identify current stewards, past authorization, visitor needs, physical risk and the approved local process.

## Seed — Returned Belongings, No Owner Assumed

A sealed container of ordinary personal objects returns from an expedition after its original holder is canonically confirmed deceased.

Courier state can confirm delivery. Material provenance can confirm the objects. The receiving institution can hold temporary custody.

The story does not decide who inherits anything. It creates a `memorial_object_handoff` and routes ownership questions to canon/legal rules if those rules exist.

Possible gameplay:

- identify which items were institution property;
- separate archival material from personal effects;
- preserve a field notebook without publishing private pages;
- wait for an authorized transfer decision.

## Seed — The Partner Keeps Taking the Old Route

A Pokémon with a canonically documented long-term partnership continues to appear along an old daily route after its Trainer is no longer present.

The system records only observable behavior: route, time, stops and responses.

NPCs may interpret the routine differently. Those interpretations remain claims.

The scenario must not infer new ownership, grief, supernatural awareness or willingness to join another Trainer.

A player can help maintain safe access, observe changes or connect caretakers with the appropriate welfare system.

## Seed — Anniversary Without Agreement

Several groups want to remember the same past event differently.

One prefers a public ceremony. Another prefers a quiet marker. A third argues that the accepted account omits a participant.

Public Memory owns the competing historical narratives. Pass 62 tracks the site, participation and any marker revision. The story can end with multiple practices coexisting rather than forcing one official interpretation.

## Seed — Wildflowers Between Markers

A section of an old memorial ground develops a distinctive seasonal plant community.

Visitors disagree about whether the growth should be cleared, protected or left alone. Conservation evidence shows that some wild Pokémon now use the edge habitat.

The site becomes a stewardship problem linking memorial access, ecology and maintenance.

No flower or Pokémon receives supernatural meaning unless an approved tradition or canon fact provides it.

## Seed — The Wrong Date on the Stone

A researcher notices that an inscription date conflicts with a contemporaneous public schedule.

The first assumption is a carving error. Later evidence shows the marker may use a different local dating convention, refer to a different event, or have been replaced from memory after the original was damaged.

The investigation is designed to teach the player that a contradiction is a question, not proof of fraud.

## Seed — The Role Nobody Automatically Gets

A respected local role becomes vacant after its holder is canonically confirmed gone from active life through retirement, relocation or death.

Several NPCs assume a successor based on custom, seniority or family connection, but the institution record does not yet establish the rule.

Pass 62 emits a role-continuity handoff. Staffing/governance systems decide the outcome.

This prevents the narrative generator from inventing hereditary or elective systems to close a plot quickly.

## Seed — A Marker for Someone Who Returned

An actor was missing long enough that friends created a non-funerary remembrance marker. The actor later returns alive.

The marker does not disappear automatically.

Possible consequences:

- it becomes a record of the search period;
- wording is revised;
- it moves to an archive;
- the returning actor requests privacy;
- the community leaves it unchanged as a historical artifact.

This is a direct test of the distinction between remembrance and confirmed death.

## Seed — The Closed Path, Not the Closed Place

Weather damage blocks the main approach to a memorial site while the site itself remains physically stable.

Travel and Facility Maintenance own the route repair. Pass 62 tracks visitor access and temporary alternatives.

A small scene can involve guiding a visitor cohort to an approved accessible route, updating signage and documenting the closure without creating a crisis battle.

## Mystery — Four Markers, Five Records

Four visible memorial markers correspond to five archival entries with overlapping names.

The investigation combines:

- marker revision history;
- archived rosters;
- old map versions;
- a relocation record;
- testimony from a person who only remembers the later layout.

Possible truth structures:

- two names were once on one shared marker;
- one marker was removed for repair and never reinstalled;
- one record refers to a memorial elsewhere;
- an archive transcription duplicated a person;
- the visible markers and archive are both partially correct.

The mystery should resolve provenance, not create a ghost identity reveal.

## Long arc — A Place Learns How to Remember

Phase 1: baseline.

Players first visit a modest memorial site during ordinary operation. They learn access routes, stewards, visitor patterns and the condition of several markers.

Phase 2: disruption.

A path closure, damaged inscription, identity question or nearby public-works project creates a concrete maintenance problem.

Phase 3: disagreement.

Different groups want different responses. Their preferences are tied to documented history, access needs, privacy or stewardship rather than generic “traditional versus modern” stereotypes.

Phase 4: investigation.

Players compare records, physical condition, testimony and prior work orders. Some popular assumptions narrow or change.

Phase 5: action.

An authorized repair, revision, route change or preservation decision occurs. Not every historical disagreement is resolved.

Phase 6: return.

Months later the site reflects the outcome. Visitors use the new route. A corrected marker exists beside a record of its earlier text. A stewardship practice persists. A disputed claim remains in Public Memory with better provenance.

The place has accumulated history without requiring a new dungeon or a supernatural escalation.

## Encounter — Boundary-Nest Disturbance

Premise:

Wild Pokémon establish a seasonal nesting area in vegetation along the boundary of a memorial site. Some visitors blame them for marker damage that may predate the nesting.

Full intended version:

- moving visitor cohorts;
- protected fragile zones;
- retreat/containment goals;
- interception;
- forced movement;
- terrain-sensitive routing;
- objective-aware AI;
- persistent Minecraft site changes.

Dependencies:

The same permanent capability map applies. Complete movement, environmental tactical systems, AI tactical policy and adapter/playback are BLOCKING where the full version depends on them.

Reduced executable version:

Close the boundary section to visitors. Record marker and vegetation condition before any battle. If conflict occurs, move tactical resolution to a static safe area. Determine responsibility for earlier damage from observations, maintenance history and ecology evidence rather than from which Pokémon is defeated.

## Noncombat encounter — Inscription Review

Premise:

A marker inscription conflicts with two archival sources and one local testimony chain.

Scene loop:

1. photograph/read the current marker;
2. retrieve revision history;
3. compare source dates and provenance;
4. identify which claims are independent;
5. ask the authorized steward what correction process exists;
6. submit a correction packet or retain uncertainty.

This scene can run before missing combat systems and can create durable world state immediately.

## Canon questions left open

- Which Ouros settlements or cultures use public memorial sites at all?
- Are human and Pokémon memorial practices shared or separate in different regions?
- Which institutions maintain records and markers?
- Who can authorize inscription changes?
- What privacy rules apply to memorial records?
- Are there approved funeral, burial, cremation, scattering, shrine, bell, garden or household practices?
- What rules govern belongings after confirmed death?
- What rules govern Pokémon custody or partnership continuity?
- How are vacant institutional roles filled?
- Which supernatural beliefs exist, and which are canonically true versus culturally believed?

No answer is assumed by this pass.

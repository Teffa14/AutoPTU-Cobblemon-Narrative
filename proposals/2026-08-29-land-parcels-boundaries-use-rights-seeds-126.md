# Ouros Narrative Seeds — Land Parcels, Boundaries, Use Rights & Records — Pass 126

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. These seeds demonstrate the continuity extension. They do not establish Ouros property law, institutions, ownership, survey standards, access rights or adjudication.
Date: 2026-08-29

## Design rule

Each seed begins from evidence already present in world state. Physical geometry, occupation, fences, maps, addresses, stewardship and repeated use remain separate from any authored land interest. When a question requires authority, hand it to the existing canon owner instead of resolving it by narration or combat.

## The Fence Was Built for Mareep

A ranch fence appears several metres away from a line on an old administrative map. Locals assume one of them must be wrong.

Archived maintenance notes show that the fence was installed to keep a managed Pokémon group away from a wet patch. It was never described as a boundary marker. A later survey can now proceed without turning a practical husbandry feature into evidence of theft or encroachment.

Useful systems: Ranching, Cartography, Archives, land continuity.

## The Address Survived the Land Change

Two records use the same old address while a modern map shows separate land units. Residents argue that the database duplicated a house.

The building kept its historic address after a later reconfiguration of surrounding land records. The address is a stable social label; the land-unit IDs tell a different chronological story.

Useful systems: Homes/Housing, Public Notices, Archives, Cartography.

## The Marker Is Genuine and Still Misleading

A carved stone at a field edge is authentic and old. Its inscription matches a historical survey archive. It nevertheless fails to match the current geometry.

The stone belonged to an earlier configuration that was superseded. The mystery ends by identifying which edition it represents rather than proving that someone moved it.

## The Shortcut Everyone Knows

A footpath between a settlement and a market has been used for decades. A new public map omits it, creating rumors that someone secretly closed the route.

Travel records confirm repeated use. The land record contains no authored access-right entry either way. The practical route and its formal status remain separate. A local institution may later decide what to do if canon gives it that mandate.

The path can continue to matter socially while the legal question stays open.

## The River Moved, the Record Waited

A flood shifts a shallow channel. A fence repair follows the new bank because that is operationally convenient. An older map still shows the previous line.

The physical river, repaired fence and recorded boundary now form three separate geometries. The story becomes a chronology problem. Which one controls a land interest remains unanswered until a regional rule exists.

This seed can connect Stormwater/Flood, Cartography, Ranching and Civic Governance without creating a generic river-boundary doctrine.

## Two Maps, Two Jobs

A tourism map and an infrastructure plan show different edges around the same public facility. Players suspect concealment.

The tourism edition simplifies the site for navigation. The infrastructure plan tracks service corridors. Neither document was intended to define land ownership. A third record may eventually provide the relevant scope.

This is a low-stakes provenance mystery suitable for early-game investigation.

## The Road Crew Maintains What It Does Not Own

Residents infer that the road operator owns a strip of land because its workers repeatedly maintain drainage and vegetation there.

Records only establish a maintenance responsibility. Ownership remains a separate claim. The distinction matters later when another project needs permission beyond routine maintenance.

Useful systems: Roads, Civic Governance, Facility Maintenance, land continuity.

## The Ranch Changed Hands, the Herd Did Not Notice

A managed ranch acquires a new canon-authored operator or holder. The persistent land ID, paddocks, paths, herd routines, staff relationships and local name continue.

Pokémon behavior changes slowly or not at all. The story emphasizes that administrative change and ecological/social continuity occur on different clocks.

No species behavior is inferred automatically; individual routines require observation records.

## The Old Gate Faces Nothing

An ornate gate remains on a lane after the main entrance moved during reconstruction. Tourists keep treating it as the property entrance because old photographs show it prominently.

Current access policy points elsewhere. The gate survives as a landmark and public-memory object. It can later anchor a quiet neighborhood scene or misdirect a courier without becoming a legal boundary.

## The Public Work Finished Before the Map

A small bridge approach or utility corridor is complete and in service. The current public parcel map still shows the previous configuration.

The discrepancy is administrative lag, not evidence that construction occurred without authority. Project records contain the relevant approval references; the map edition awaits its next revision.

This seed reinforces `CONSTRUCTION_COMPLETE != RECORD_UPDATE_COMPLETE`.

## The Pokémon Crosses Here Every Dawn

A repeatedly observed wild individual crosses a wall gap at the same time each day. People begin calling the gap “its gate.”

The observation can reveal a broken enclosure, habitat connection or useful trail. It creates no human ownership, access or boundary fact. Wildlife Monitoring and Interspecies Ecology may use the behavioral record for their own purposes.

## The Reserve Sign and the Survey Post

A protected-area sign stands near an old survey post, but the two do not line up exactly. Visitors interpret the gap as a loophole.

Conservation's operational boundary is authoritative for current visitor restrictions if canon says so. The older land record has a different purpose and date. The story teaches players that overlapping spatial systems can legitimately use different lines.

## The House Is on One Unit, the Garden on Another

A long-standing residence visually appears to be a single property. Historical records show that the structure and garden reference different land units, with a canon-authored permission connecting their use.

The household experiences one home. The record preserves two spatial identities. This can matter during later repair or public works without invalidating anyone's residence.

## Five Lines Around One Field

The player finds five representations of a field edge:
- a ranch fence;
- a historic overview map;
- a recent survey measurement;
- a public walking map;
- a conservation operational boundary.

All five can be real and useful. The task is to identify purpose, date and source before asking which line answers the actual question.

This is a reusable tutorial for the entire Evidence Graph.

## Mystery — Four Stones, Three Surveys

Four old markers survive around a hillside. Three survey editions reference overlapping but non-identical configurations.

Resolution path:
- identify marker dates;
- link each marker to the survey edition that actually cites it;
- compare later correction records;
- distinguish a retired marker from a moved marker;
- leave any ownership dispute to a canon-authored authority.

Possible ending: every stone is genuine; the land record changed twice.

## Mystery — The Path That Is Missing From Every Official Map

Residents, delivery workers and several Pokémon routines all use a narrow path. No current official map displays it.

Possible evidence:
- old photographs;
- route traces;
- maintenance records;
- personal correspondence;
- field observations;
- an archived map edition that labels the area differently.

The investigation can establish physical existence and historical use. It cannot invent an access right. A satisfying local resolution may simply be a corrected navigation map plus a referral for the unresolved formal question.

## Mystery — One Farm, Three Names

A ranch appears under three names across decades. Players initially assume three properties were consolidated.

The evidence may instead show one persistent land unit, two operator changes and a later branding change. Or it may show a true reconfiguration if canon supports such an event. The generator must choose only from evidence-backed states.

## Exploration — The Boundary Walk Above the Old Canal

An old canal maintenance path, retired survey posts and several current fences form a walkable historical corridor above a settlement.

Current reduced implementation:
- the area is physically stable and reviewed;
- exploration uses static Minecraft geometry;
- old/current map overlays and marker provenance drive the puzzle;
- no exact legal conclusion is required;
- no active machinery, flooding or unstable bank enters combat.

Discoveries can update map knowledge, marker provenance and public-memory entries.

## Exploration — The Orchard Behind Two Addresses

A quiet orchard can only be reached through a lane whose modern address differs from the one in an archived field notebook. The orchard itself has remained in place while buildings and labels changed around it.

Players reconstruct the access history through addresses, photographs, map editions and testimony. The reward can be knowledge, a restored wayfinding annotation or contact with a local NPC rather than ownership of the land.

## NPC candidate — The Field Survey Technician

Status: NON-CANON.

A technically careful mapper who distinguishes observed measurements from decisions made by other institutions. Their narrative value comes from refusing to overclaim.

Recurring functions:
- explain why two geometries differ;
- request access to a site through the proper owner system;
- flag uncertainty;
- preserve retired markers instead of destroying them;
- become a witness to long-term landscape change.

No automatic legal authority.

## NPC candidate — The Registry Archivist

Status: NON-CANON.

An archivist who knows identifiers, aliases and revision histories better than current geography. They can locate old records but cannot verify a field marker by looking at a file alone.

Their partnership with a survey technician creates useful two-person investigations: one knows records, the other knows current geometry.

## NPC candidate — The Ranch Steward With the Wrong Map

Status: NON-CANON.

A competent ranch steward inherited a perfectly reasonable map that later proved approximate. Their operations are sound even though one record is stale. This avoids making every discrepancy the result of negligence.

## Faction dynamic — The Corridor Coalition

Status: NON-CANON.

Several organizations depend on one narrow strip of land for different reasons: road maintenance, utility access, ranch movement, conservation monitoring and a local walking route. None should be collapsed into one owner or faction.

The coalition can disagree over schedules, access windows and maintenance while still cooperating. Civic Governance may coordinate a proposal if canon supplies the mandate.

## Long arc — A Valley Learns Where Its Lines Came From

Phase 1: Ordinary life.
Ranches, homes, parks, roads and paths function. Fences and signs are part of the scenery. No parcel exposition is required.

Phase 2: A practical project.
A small road, drainage, conservation or service project needs a precise corridor. Existing maps disagree.

Phase 3: Field work.
Survey records, historical maps, addresses, photographs and local knowledge reveal several different reasons for the discrepancies. One old fence was practical rather than cadastral. One marker is retired. One public map was simplified. A genuine unresolved claim may remain.

Phase 4: Handoffs.
Questions go to the correct owners. Cartography corrects map representations. Public Works revises its project geometry. Conservation updates an operational boundary if needed. Any contested right goes only to an authored deciding process.

Phase 5: Visible legacy.
New markers or signs appear if approved. Old ones may remain as historical objects. NPC routines shift around the revised project. A familiar Pokémon continues using an old gap. The valley remembers the investigation.

Phase 6: Later callback.
Years later another project reuses the persistent IDs and revision history. Players who know the area can recognize why an apparently strange line exists.

## Outcome guardrails

Acceptable tactical writebacks:
- immediate approach secured;
- hostile/wild threat withdrew when supported by battle/world resolution;
- work site can be re-entered for later assessment;
- a static route can be rechecked.

Forbidden automatic writebacks:
- ownership proven;
- boundary accepted;
- survey certified;
- access right granted;
- claim invalidated;
- registry corrected;
- conservation/public-work authority created;
- Pokémon ownership changed.

Those outcomes require the systems and canon sources that actually own them.

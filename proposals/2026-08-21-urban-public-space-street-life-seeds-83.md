# Ouros Urban Public Space & Street Life Seeds — Pass 83

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros concepts derived from high-level research patterns. No external plot or character is transplanted.

## 1. The Bench Everyone Uses

A bench beside a station exit appears in years of photographs with different regulars. When redevelopment requires moving it, several unrelated NPCs reveal different reasons the spot matters to them. The decision can preserve, relocate or replace the bench while keeping its Chronicle history.

## 2. Morning Square, Evening Market

A plaza functions as a commuter shortcut before work and a small market at night. A new setup layout blocks the path that residents have used for years. The problem becomes scheduling, access and design rather than choosing whether commuters or vendors are “right.”

## 3. The Missing Fountain Regular

Researchers have repeatedly observed the same persistent wild Pokémon drinking from one fountain at dusk. It stops appearing. Possible explanations include a route change, construction, water quality, disturbance, injury, capture or simple observation gaps; none becomes truth until supported.

## 4. Station Forecourt Split

Two transport disruptions redirect passenger flows into the same forecourt. A space that is normally comfortable becomes congested without any permanent population increase. Players can help establish temporary routing and later contribute evidence to a redesign proposal.

## 5. The Shortcut Through the Green

An informal path gradually forms across a community green because it connects housing with a clinic. The same strip also crosses a recurring Pokémon-use corridor. A future project can formalize, reroute or close the shortcut after observing both uses.

## 6. Café Edge Without a Café

A café closes after decades, but people continue using the shaded tables and meeting outside the empty storefront. The public-space use survives its original business. A later tenant must decide whether to preserve the edge arrangement or redesign it.

## 7. The Park After School

A quiet park becomes busy for ninety minutes every school day. A wild collective has also begun crossing during that window after another route was obstructed. The solution may involve timing, landscaping, education or reopening the older corridor.

## 8. Last Tram, First Performance

A street performer regularly starts just after the final heavy commuter wave. A service schedule change suddenly makes the performance overlap with a crowded transfer period. The story can evolve through negotiation and observation rather than a ban or contest.

## 9. The Plaza Photograph

A photograph makes one corner of a plaza unexpectedly popular. Visitors repeatedly reproduce the same angle, obstructing a service entrance and changing where a wild Pokémon group forages. The original photographer never intended to create a destination.

## 10. The Empty Steps

Public steps outside an archive have been a social gathering place for years. They suddenly remain empty despite normal weather and opening hours. Investigation may reveal schedule changes, construction elsewhere, a new gathering point or a rumor that altered behavior.

## 11. Rain Under the Arcade

An arcade passage becomes a refuge whenever afternoon rain arrives. Vendors, commuters, students and wild Pokémon all use the dry edge differently. A renovation that improves drainage elsewhere unexpectedly changes the passage’s role.

## 12. Quiet Corner Becomes Famous

A small garden known mainly to residents is mentioned in a popular travel guide. Visitor numbers rise, but the effects are uneven: a nearby café benefits, a narrow entrance becomes difficult to use and nighttime Pokémon observations decline. No one consequence defines the entire outcome.

## 13. The Noticeboard Nobody Owns

For years, residents have pinned jobs, lost-item notices and club invitations to an unofficial board beside a transit stop. A modernization project removes it because it has no recognized operator. Players can help determine whether the function should be formalized somewhere else.

## 14. Wild Zone Spillover

A managed urban habitat becomes successful enough that some Pokémon start using adjacent ordinary streets and courtyards. The result can be research, coexistence design and public concern without assuming that the habitat “failed.”

## 15. The Night Market Cleanup

A successful festival leaves an unusual waste profile that changes scavenger activity for several days. Cleanup finishes visually, but wildlife observations remain different. Food, Waste and Wild Collective systems can determine whether the connection is real.

## 16. The Corner That Became a Club

Several Trainers begin meeting informally in the same public corner. Over months it gains a noticeboard, regular time and recognized members. Only later do they consider becoming a formal club with premises; the social history predates the institution.

## 17. The Club That Lost Its Corner

A long-running group loses access to its usual gathering place during repairs. Members split between two temporary locations and develop different habits. When the original site reopens, not everyone returns.

## 18. Waterfront Without the View

New temporary structures block a famous waterfront sightline during a long infrastructure project. Tourism declines at one spot but increases along another promenade. Street vendors and photographers adapt before the physical project is finished.

## 19. The Tree in the Middle

One mature tree shapes shade, seating, photographs and repeated Pokémon use in a small square. A health assessment later requires removing it. The story focuses on how the space changes afterward rather than inventing a magical solution that saves every old state.

## 20. First Night of the New Lights

A park receives new lighting intended to improve evening access. Human use rises immediately; nocturnal Pokémon observations change over the following weeks. Light Ecology and Science must establish whether the patterns are causal before management changes again.

## 21. The Route That Crosses a Festival

A recurring festival expands across a route that normally carries deliveries and emergency access. The event can proceed, but only if its time program and access plan account for those functions. This seed can create institutional learning across annual editions.

## 22. Two Maps of the Same Plaza

A tourism map shows attractions; an accessibility map shows gradients, entrances and rest points. Both are accurate yet tell different stories about the same place. Players can discover missing information by comparing them rather than deciding which map is “true.”

## 23. Former Partner at the Fountain

A released former partner Pokémon is observed near a public fountain months later. The observation becomes Chronicle evidence of location only. It grants no command authority and does not prove the Pokémon came looking for its former Trainer.

## 24. The Pop-Up That Stayed

A temporary food stall installed during a recovery event remains popular after the crisis. Its operator seeks a longer-term arrangement. The question passes through public-space use, commerce and civic permissions rather than automatic settlement growth.

## 25. Three Uses for One Court

A paved courtyard is used by children in the afternoon, a training group in early evening and nearby restaurant tables at night. A single permanent redesign would benefit one use while constraining another. Players can help test reversible time-based changes first.

## 26. The Day the Pigeons Changed Sides

Urban bird Pokémon that normally gather along one roofline suddenly move to the opposite side of a street. New signage, façade work, light, food availability, sound or weather could all matter. The hook is observational, not a guaranteed quest crisis.

## 27. The Plaza During Recovery

After a regional emergency, a central square becomes a relief coordination point. As normal life returns, some temporary structures disappear while one service remains because residents found it useful. The physical and social after-state becomes part of the settlement’s history.

## 28. The Market Without Battles

A local market has a strong cultural norm against casual Trainer battles during opening hours because stalls and visitors fill the space. This is an authored institutional norm, not an automatic mechanical prohibition. Challengers use a nearby legal battle area instead.

## 29. The Forgotten Passage

A narrow pedestrian passage once linked two busy streets, but a newer route made it nearly irrelevant. Years later, another closure makes the old passage important again. Archive maps, physical condition and current access must all be checked before reopening it.

## 30. A Square Learns a New Name

Players resolve several unrelated events around the same small square over multiple years. Residents gradually adopt an informal nickname based on one remembered feature. The project can record alias usage before any institution decides whether the name becomes official.

# Longer arc A — Seven Years in One Square

Year one establishes normal morning, evening and seasonal use. Year two brings a temporary festival redesign. Year three adds a transport reroute. Year four changes one frontage. Year five introduces a recurring Pokémon-use pattern. Year six includes a public-works proposal based on accumulated evidence. Year seven shows the square after the intervention, preserving old memories and uses that survived.

The arc demonstrates that place identity can emerge from many small state changes rather than one main quest.

# Longer arc B — The Green Line

An abandoned service corridor becomes an informal walking route. Residents add temporary uses, researchers discover ecological value along one segment and a transport project proposes formal conversion. Over several years the corridor can become a linear park, mixed route, habitat corridor or partially closed infrastructure reserve depending on actual decisions.

No single ending is automatically optimal.

# Longer arc C — A City Between Battles

A set of recurring public spaces—station steps, small park, market square, waterfront and café edge—becomes the continuity fabric between larger adventures. NPC careers change, clubs form, relationships produce explicit events, Pokémon use shifts, festivals recur and old incidents become public memory.

The arc deliberately tests whether Ouros can make “ordinary life between quests” feel persistent without filling every visit with a new objective.

# Encounter contract 1 — Station Forecourt Rush

FULL intent: moving civilian cohorts, withdrawal routes, corridor protection, interception and AI that understands exit access.

Required blocking families: complete movement including push/pull/knockback/interception/forced movement; terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions if protected lanes are tactical zones; AI tactical policy; Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback. Lifecycle, damage, statuses, Moves, Abilities, Items and Trainer Features remain PARTIAL whenever used.

REDUCED version: divert commuters before the battle, close one forecourt segment and freeze a static arena containing only combatants and fixed blockers. Post-battle withdrawal and crowd return are authoritative world-state transitions.

# Encounter contract 2 — Park Edge Wildlife Conflict

FULL intent: wild actors crossing or withdrawing, spectators clearing the edge and players protecting a route without KO being mandatory.

Critical dependencies: complete movement BLOCKING; terrain/zones/reactions BLOCKING; tactical AI BLOCKING; adapter/playback BLOCKING.

REDUCED version: clear the public edge first. If conflict remains, use a static battle space. The ecological crossing and later behavior stay outside the grid.

# Encounter contract 3 — Night Market Chokepoint

FULL intent: protected exit lanes, moving visitors, interactive stall geometry and possible disengagement objectives.

Critical dependencies: complete movement BLOCKING; terrain/zones/reactions BLOCKING; tactical AI BLOCKING; adapter/playback BLOCKING. Item/Feature/Move-specific behavior remains PARTIAL when a concept requires it.

REDUCED version: market staff secure goods and visitors before battle. Stalls become fixed blockers only when projected as ordinary geometry. Attack visuals do not create property damage unless a future authoritative object-interaction contract says so.

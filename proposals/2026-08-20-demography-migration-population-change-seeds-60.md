# Ouros Demography, Migration & Population Change Seeds — Pass 60

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original material derived from research patterns. No external characters, dialogue or plots are copied.

Date: 2026-08-20

## 1. The Village That Outgrew Its Clinic

A research institute doubles the number of long-term residents over three years, but the clinic still operates at its original capacity. The conflict is staffing and service pressure, not a mysterious disease.

## 2. The Festival Count

Two institutions publish very different visitor totals after the same festival. One counted ticket scans. The other estimated mobile presence around the whole district. Both can be methodologically defensible.

## 3. Last Ferry Commuters

A route closure turns a small ferry into the main daily connection for workers. The harbor population barely changes overnight, but daytime presence and transport pressure rise sharply.

## 4. The Empty Dormitory

An academy expands, builds a dormitory, then enrollment changes before the building opens. The structure becomes a candidate for adaptive reuse rather than a failed ruin.

## 5. Orchard Season

A settlement receives a recurring seasonal workforce during one harvest window. Temporary lodging, food demand and transport shift for six weeks, then return close to baseline.

## 6. Research Boom

A major discovery attracts researchers, technicians, traders and visitors. The first year produces mostly temporary presence. Only later do some households relocate permanently.

## 7. The Returning Evacuees

After a crisis, most residents return quickly, some remain elsewhere temporarily, and a few choose not to return. The system must not decide any individual choice without authored evidence.

## 8. Two Population Maps

A civic office and a transport operator disagree about which district is busiest. The first measures residents. The second measures daily entries. Both maps are correct for different questions.

## 9. The Street That Sleeps

A market district is packed during the day but nearly empty overnight. New players mistake it for a major residential district because they first visit at noon.

## 10. The New Platform

A new rail stop increases commuter traffic and small businesses before any large housing project begins. The district's use changes before its resident count does.

## 11. The Village of Former Students

An academy settlement gradually changes because many graduates stay nearby. Former temporary residents become workers, mentors and household members over several years.

## 12. The Old Mining Row

A mine closes. Some residents leave, but others remain and convert old worker housing into workshops, studios and guesthouses. Population decline produces reuse rather than abandonment.

## 13. The Census That Missed the Expedition

A population count happens while a large expedition crew is away for a month. Later records appear to show sudden growth when the team returns.

## 14. The Harbor Without Bedrooms

A port serves thousands of daily passengers but has relatively little housing. A public debate incorrectly treats passenger volume as proof that the district needs equivalent residential expansion.

## 15. Three Generations of One District

A neighborhood begins as temporary expedition lodging, becomes worker housing and later turns into a mixed residential-commercial area. Physical continuity preserves all three layers.

## 16. The Visitor Who Never Left

An NPC arrives for a short tournament placement, extends the stay for work, then eventually establishes a residence. Each state transition must be recorded separately.

## 17. The Overcounted Crisis Camp

A relief organization reports bed capacity while a civic office reports actual occupants. News coverage mistakes the capacity number for displaced population.

## 18. Closed School, Open Workshop

A shrinking settlement no longer needs a full school building. The site is reused for vocational training and community workshops without framing the town as dying.

## 19. The Weekend Town

A small lakeside settlement has a low usual population but large recurring weekend presence. Wildlife disturbance follows the presence pattern rather than residence totals.

## 20. The Wrong Newcomers Story

Residents say “everyone moving here is a tourist.” Data shows most newcomers arrived for a clinic expansion and construction work. Public belief and population evidence diverge.

## 21. Cross-Valley Commuters

Two settlements depend on one another: one has housing, the other has jobs and services. A landslide exposes how much daily life relies on the connecting route.

## 22. The Retired Route

A road bypass removes through-traffic from an old town center. Resident numbers stay stable, but shops and services change because transient presence disappears.

## 23. Quiet Growth

A settlement gains only a few households per year, but over fifteen years a new neighborhood forms. No dramatic boom event is required.

## 24. The Temporary Research Quarter

Portable labs and cabins become semi-permanent after a multi-year study. The question is when temporary infrastructure should be recognized as a real district.

## 25. The House With Three Addresses

An old road renaming and district boundary change create duplicate records for one household. A registry mismatch looks like population growth until provenance is checked.

## 26. Return Migration

A transport restoration makes it practical for people who moved away during a long closure to return. Some do. Others keep ties without relocating.

## 27. The Seasonal Clinic

A remote clinic opens extra capacity during a predictable migration/tourism period. The service model follows population seasonality rather than permanent expansion.

## 28. The Newcomer Market

New residents introduce products and services they already know how to make. Local adoption is gradual and mixed; origin does not determine individual cultural practice.

## 29. Wild Zone Neighboring District

A city redesign adds protected Pokémon habitat beside residential areas. Human occupancy, visitor flows and wildlife presence shift by zone without one population replacing another.

## 30. The Population Claim Nobody Can Verify

A famous historical plaque says a town once held “ten thousand souls.” Archives, housing footprint and transport records cannot independently confirm it. The number remains a historical claim, not world truth.

# Long Arc A — From Field Camp to City

Year 1: a scientific expedition establishes temporary quarters.

Year 2: workshops, clinic services and transport become regular.

Year 3: some staff bring households or establish permanent homes.

Year 4: merchants and service workers arrive because demand is now stable.

Year 5: the settlement must decide whether temporary roads, labs and dormitories should become permanent infrastructure.

Year 6+: the original expedition becomes only one institution among many.

The arc can produce research, housing, civic, transport, workplace and ecology content without requiring one central villain.

# Long Arc B — The Town After the Boom

A discovery creates sudden population growth.

Early phase:
- temporary workers;
- tourists;
- researchers;
- housing pressure;
- overloaded services.

Middle phase:
- new construction;
- speculation and competing proposals;
- conservation pressure;
- transport expansion;
- neighborhood formation.

Later phase:
- the discovery becomes less novel;
- temporary visitors decline;
- some institutions remain;
- some businesses close or adapt;
- residents decide what kind of place remains.

The arc is about normalization after rapid growth, not collapse.

# Long Arc C — The Two-Sided Valley

One side of a valley has affordable housing and older neighborhoods. The other has an academy, hospital and research facilities.

For years, daily commuter flows bind them together.

Possible developments:
- bridge failure;
- new ferry;
- remote-work alternatives;
- housing construction near the institutions;
- service expansion on the residential side;
- ecological corridor concerns;
- eventual multi-center settlement identity.

The valley can become more integrated or more self-sufficient in each half depending on accumulated decisions.

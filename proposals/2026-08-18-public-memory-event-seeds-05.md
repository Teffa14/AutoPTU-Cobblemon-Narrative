# Ouros Public Memory & Event Seeds — Pass 05

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros candidates derived from structural research. Names, locations, institutions and mechanics remain placeholders until canon and PTU/AutoPTU review.

## Seed AS — The Festival With Two Histories

A settlement holds an annual celebration honoring the trainers who supposedly ended an old regional disaster.

The public story is clean and heroic. Archive fragments and older residents preserve a messier version involving several groups, a failed evacuation and one faction whose contribution was removed from the official account.

Players can enjoy the festival without investigating anything.

If they dig deeper, the historical dispute can affect:
- current faction legitimacy;
- a memorial inscription;
- family grievances;
- who receives public honors;
- ownership of an old site;
- the theme of next year's event.

Canonical past events do not change. Only the present understanding of them does.

## Seed AT — The Returning Champion

A recurring tournament begins a new edition with a former champion returning after several years away.

Their past victories are public record, but people disagree about why they originally stopped competing.

Possible current roles:
- competitor;
- mentor;
- commentator;
- Gym staff;
- sponsor representative;
- reluctant guest of honor.

The story can become a rivalry, reconciliation, investigation or simple sporting arc depending on player involvement.

Exact tournament mechanics remain undefined.

## Seed AU — The Name on the Bridge

A bridge repaired through old player actions was eventually named after one highly visible participant.

Years later, another community argues that several forgotten workers, Pokémon and rescuers deserve recognition too.

The conflict is about public memory rather than proving the original repair happened.

Possible outcomes:
- rename the bridge;
- add a broader memorial;
- preserve the old name but add an exhibit;
- uncover that the famous participant never wanted sole credit;
- leave the dispute unresolved but change public discussion.

This seed gives old Chronicle state a low-combat social afterlife.

## Seed AV — Market Week

A normally quiet settlement hosts a temporary regional market and exhibition week.

During preparation:
- vendors arrive;
- transport increases;
- temporary workers are hired;
- factions compete for stalls and sponsorship;
- local wild Pokémon react to noise and food waste.

During the event:
- players can trade, socialize, enter competitions, perform jobs or investigate incidents;
- visiting NPCs create unusual relationship combinations;
- one minor crisis does not automatically become the whole story.

During aftermath:
- some merchants stay;
- garbage or habitat disturbance may create work;
- a successful local business gains visibility;
- a faction may gain influence from sponsorship.

## Seed AW — Witnesses Disagree

A public battle ends with a controversial moment visible from several angles.

Different witnesses sincerely report different things because of distance, obstruction, attention and prior assumptions.

The system stores separate witness reports.

Later consequences can include:
- a protest;
- sponsor withdrawal;
- a disciplinary review;
- an NPC defending the player;
- edited footage or selective retellings;
- a rival exploiting the controversy.

The actual battle log remains canonical evidence. Public opinion does not automatically match it.

## Seed AX — The Gym After the Leader

A respected Gym Leader leaves office.

The Gym remains important because its building, staff, training philosophy, local duties, recurring events and public expectations already exist.

A successor may:
- preserve tradition;
- modernize the arena;
- change how challengers qualify;
- reduce civic involvement;
- expand community training;
- create conflict with longtime staff.

This allows institutional continuity without treating one NPC as the entire organization.

## Seed AY — The Invitation Nobody Expected

A player receives an invitation to a prestigious public event because of an earlier action that generated unexpected public recognition.

Possible reasons:
- a rescue became widely known;
- a research report changed local policy;
- a rival publicly praised the player;
- a tournament organizer wants an unconventional participant;
- a faction wants visible association with them.

The invitation itself creates choices about visibility, sponsorship, obligations and reputation.

## Seed AZ — The Quiet Competitor

An NPC repeatedly places well in public competitions but avoids attention outside them.

The public treats the NPC as mysterious because almost nothing is known about their life.

Players may build a relationship with them through ordinary interactions rather than by defeating them.

Their public prestige and private social state remain separate.

This character can become:
- a respected rival;
- an ally;
- an occasional teammate;
- a source of specialist knowledge;
- someone who simply remains private.

The system must not generate a hidden tragic backstory merely to explain quiet behavior.

## Seed BA — The Memorial Route

A dangerous route contains physical traces of previous disasters and rescues: repaired shelters, plaques, altered paths and old warning systems.

Travel itself teaches regional history.

Players can encounter:
- descendants of affected residents;
- old equipment still in use;
- changed Pokémon populations;
- conflicting commemorations;
- a location where the public story and physical evidence do not fully agree.

The route becomes environmental storytelling rather than a lore book.

## Seed BB — Festival Rivalry

Two settlements host related annual festivals and compete over which one better represents a shared regional tradition.

The rivalry is mostly friendly, but sponsors, tourism and civic pride create real stakes.

Players might:
- compete for either side;
- help coordinate a joint event;
- expose sabotage;
- mediate over historical claims;
- participate socially without taking sides;
- influence which traditions survive next year.

The world records each edition's outcome and public reaction.

## Seed BC — The Last Edition's Rule

A recurring competition changed its format after a controversial previous edition.

The new rule solved one problem but created another.

Players can encounter:
- veterans who prefer the old format;
- newcomers who only know the new one;
- organizers defending the change;
- competitors trying to exploit unintended consequences.

This makes rules history part of institution identity.

Exact competitive mechanics require review before implementation.

## Seed BD — The Retired Hero Is Not Available

A current crisis resembles one solved by a famous former player character.

NPCs naturally ask why the old hero cannot simply fix it again.

The system does not invent an answer.

Permitted handling:
- the historical record confirms only that the character retired from active public work;
- current institutions must solve the problem themselves;
- another character may inherit an approved tool, office, research archive or obligation if canon supports it;
- the famous figure's later private life remains undefined.

This seed turns authorship protection into worldbuilding: societies have to grow beyond one hero.

## Seed BE — The Crowd Changes the Rivalry

Two recurring rivals have always interacted privately until a major public event puts their next confrontation in front of a crowd.

The battle or competition now has consequences beyond the result:
- spectators form opinions;
- sponsors take interest;
- faction representatives watch;
- statements after the event matter;
- an act of sportsmanship or humiliation becomes public memory.

The rivalry can become more formal, more hostile or unexpectedly respectful.

## Seed BF — The Museum Acquires the Wrong Story

A museum opens an exhibit about a famous expedition using donated objects and incomplete archival records.

One displayed interpretation is wrong.

The mistake is not necessarily malicious.

Players can:
- provide contradictory evidence;
- locate a missing field report;
- speak with surviving witnesses;
- accept that some uncertainty cannot be resolved;
- uncover a faction trying to influence the exhibit.

Correcting the museum changes public knowledge but does not rewrite the old Chronicle event.

## Seed BG — One Event, Many Invitations

A large regional celebration uses different entry hooks for different players.

Examples:
- a battler receives a tournament invitation;
- a researcher is asked to present findings;
- a courier gets a temporary logistics contract;
- a performer joins the opening ceremony;
- an investigator is quietly warned about a security concern;
- a faction member attends a sponsor reception;
- an explorer is invited to lead a historical route walk.

All players enter the same dense world state through their own life rather than one generic quest marker.

## Seed BH — Cleanup Is Story

After a huge event, the crowd leaves but consequences remain.

Potential aftermath content:
- lost Pokémon or belongings;
- damaged public spaces;
- unexpected vendor debts;
- habitat disturbance;
- leftover temporary structures;
- an NPC choosing to remain in town;
- rumors created during the event;
- evidence overlooked during the celebration;
- faction influence gained through sponsorship.

The event lifecycle therefore continues through aftermath rather than ending at the award ceremony.

## New implementation questions

- How should witness propagation work across loaded and unloaded Minecraft regions?
- Which public-standing values should be visible to players and which remain inferred through NPC behavior?
- How should event crowds be represented without harming server performance?
- Which recurring competitions use PTU/Caelo Contest rules, League battles, custom objectives or non-mechanical social judging?
- How should records from AutoPTU battles feed audience/public-memory events?
- What consent/canon workflow is required before a retired PC can appear physically as an NPC?
- How should recurring event editions advance if no player attends one year?
- Which institutions are foundational enough to receive persistent identities in Ouros canon?
- How should public memory decay, spread or become contested over long time spans?

# Ouros Finance, Sponsorship, Patronage & Risk Seeds — Pass 40

Status: NON-CANON proposals.
Date: 2026-08-19

These are original Ouros candidates derived from structural research. They do not copy external characters, corporations, plots, contract terms or reward formulas.

## 1. The Sponsor Who Only Wants to Be Seen

A local equipment maker offers to fund repairs to an aging Contest Hall. The requested return is visible acknowledgement at the venue. The sponsor does not ask to control judging or scheduling.

Possible tension:
- residents dislike commercial signage;
- the Hall needs repairs;
- another donor offers less money with no visibility request;
- performers disagree about how much branding is appropriate.

No party is automatically corrupt or malicious.

## 2. The Null-Result Grant

A university funds a seasonal field survey because earlier reports suggested an unusual migration. The team completes the work and finds no evidence that the migration occurred this year.

The actual question becomes whether the institution values a well-supported negative result.

## 3. The Restricted Recovery Fund

After a major storm, a relief fund can pay for transport restoration and emergency shelter but cannot legally be redirected to rebuild a private workshop under its authored terms.

Players can look for another funding source rather than treating the relief institution as an enemy.

## 4. Prize Verification Delay

A regional challenge ends cleanly, but the prize transfer is delayed because a result record and identity verification do not match.

The official result remains valid.

The delayed payment becomes an administrative case rather than a rematch.

## 5. The Gym Without Its Old Sponsor

A long-time sponsor withdraws after the Gym changes leadership and philosophy.

The new Leader must decide whether to:
- reduce programming;
- seek several smaller sponsors;
- rely on community support;
- change the venue model;
- delay a planned expansion.

The old sponsor may simply prefer a different public identity.

## 6. Competing Logos

Two funders support the same regional tournament. Their commercial visibility packages conflict in one part of the venue.

The dispute is about signage and contractual scope, not the outcome of battles.

## 7. Scholarship Pressure

A promising Trainer receives travel support from a public scholarship. Local media begins treating every result as a referendum on whether the award was “worth it.”

The scholarship itself has no win requirement.

This creates public pressure without changing the actual agreement.

## 8. The Honest Expensive Expedition

A research expedition returns with excellent samples and records but costs more than planned because a route closure forced a legal detour.

The funding review asks whether the overrun was justified.

The answer depends on records, not battle performance.

## 9. Two Towns, One League Investment

A League-linked institution can only support one major new training facility this cycle.

Both settlements have legitimate arguments:
- one has more Trainers;
- the other is recovering economically and lacks services;
- each proposal creates different transport and employment effects.

## 10. The District Around the Gym

A successful Gym gradually attracts food stalls, lodging, training services and media crews.

Years later, a renovation would increase capacity but displace some of the small businesses that grew around it.

## 11. The Sponsor-Control Rumor

A popular rumor claims a sponsor secretly decides a Leader’s roster.

Records show the sponsorship agreement contains no such right.

The investigation shifts toward why the rumor became credible and who benefits from repeating it.

## 12. Partial Coverage

A workshop damaged during a route incident has an authored risk-transfer agreement, but only certain equipment is covered.

The owner must decide which operations to restore first while the claim is reviewed.

No universal insurance mechanics are implied.

## 13. The Equipment Plaque

A donor provides expensive field equipment to a conservation station and asks that the gift remain publicly acknowledged.

A later staff team wants to remove the old plaque during renovations.

The conflict is about memory, gratitude and institutional identity.

## 14. Festival Funding Mosaic

A major festival is funded by a civic body, local merchants, a cultural institution and a battle club.

Each supports a different part of the event.

If one partner withdraws, only that part of the program is immediately threatened.

## 15. The Ferry Pilot Fund

A cooperative finances a temporary ferry service to prove there is enough demand for a permanent route.

The pilot can succeed socially but still fail financially, or the reverse.

## 16. Community Prize Pool

A small regional event has no wealthy sponsor. Residents, clubs and local shops collectively build the prize pool.

The social value of the event becomes larger than the mechanical reward.

## 17. One Sponsor, Two Rivals

A sporting brand sponsors two Trainers who are public rivals.

Neither has exclusivity.

Fans assume the sponsor wants one to win, but the agreement rewards visibility from both careers.

## 18. Open Research Versus Sensitive Habitat

A grant requires public dissemination of findings. During the project, researchers discover that exact coordinates could expose a fragile nesting site.

The research team must reconcile transparency with conservation obligations through a formal exception or redaction process.

## 19. The Expiring Grant

A restoration grant expires before the project finishes because an unrelated archaeological discovery stopped construction.

The unfinished work is legitimate.

The next story is about extension, rescoping or alternative support.

## 20. Emergency Reserve

A clinic previously chose to maintain a modest emergency reserve instead of expanding its lobby.

Months later, an outage disrupts normal income and deliveries. The reserve keeps critical services operating.

An old “boring” decision becomes meaningful world state.

## 21. Documentation Freeze

A funding program pauses payments because required records are incomplete after a staff transition.

There may be no theft or fraud.

Recovering institutional knowledge can reopen the program.

## 22. Apprentice Microgrant

A small craft fund gives an apprentice enough support to buy one missing tool and accept limited commissions.

The workshop does not instantly become more powerful. Its service capacity changes in a narrow, traceable way.

## 23. Too Much Sponsor Visibility

A famous Trainer accumulates so many visible sponsors that local community notices become hard to see at their home venue.

The issue becomes public-space design rather than moral condemnation of sponsorship.

## 24. The Replacement Funder

A long-running field program loses its original patron. Because the researchers preserved clear records and a strong public reputation, another institution can continue the project without resetting years of data.

## 25. The Ruins Boom

A newly verified archaeological site triggers hotel expansion, transport proposals, research grants and speculative business plans before long-term stewardship rules are settled.

The discovery is real. Many economic expectations around it are uncertain.

## 26. Transport Support Ends

A remote route has received temporary operating support for three years. The program ends as planned.

The operator must decide whether demand now supports continued service, reduced frequency or closure.

No hidden betrayal is required.

## 27. Restoration Beats Expansion

A grant committee chooses to repair existing habitat infrastructure instead of funding a new visitor center.

The rejected visitor-center proposal remains viable for a future cycle.

## 28. The Gift With Maintenance Costs

An institution receives sophisticated equipment as a donation.

The gift is genuine and useful, but nobody initially budgeted for maintenance, calibration and replacement parts.

The problem emerges months later through the technology/workplace layers.

## 29. A Lucrative Offer Declined

A community club rejects a major sponsorship because the visibility package conflicts with how members want their shared space to look.

The sponsor accepts the decision and supports another organization.

A large financial disagreement ends without an antagonist.

## 30. The Quiet Benefactor

A donor has funded small improvements across several settlements for years but always requests anonymity.

When residents try to build a public memorial to local benefactors, staff must decide how to acknowledge contributions without violating privacy.

# Longer arc A — The Gilded Circuit

Several battle institutions across one region become increasingly dependent on visible sponsorship.

Early phase:
- sponsor support repairs facilities and improves public events;
- smaller venues gain access to equipment they could not otherwise afford;
- Trainers begin receiving personal offers.

Middle phase:
- different sponsorship models create different institutional identities;
- one venue prefers many small partners;
- another relies heavily on one sponsor;
- a third attempts community funding.

Pressure phase:
A regional economic shock forces several sponsors to reduce spending at once.

The arc asks how institutions adapt without assuming the sponsors were corrupt or the old model was inherently wrong.

Possible outcomes:
- diversified support;
- reduced event calendar;
- community ownership of some equipment;
- shared facilities;
- new regional funding arrangements.

Battle results remain authoritative AutoPTU outcomes. Sponsor state never modifies battle mechanics.

# Longer arc B — The Grant Years

Players participate in a multi-year regional research program.

Year 1:
A broad survey establishes baseline data.

Year 2:
Funding priorities split between ecology, geology and public education.

Year 3:
A major null result challenges the project’s original theory.

Year 4:
A new discovery makes earlier baseline data unexpectedly valuable.

Year 5:
Players help decide whether the project becomes a permanent institution, a smaller archive, several independent teams or a completed historical program.

The arc records:
- grants;
- milestones;
- publications;
- staff careers;
- equipment provenance;
- settlements affected by field activity;
- public understanding;
- what research questions remain open.

# Longer arc C — After the Flood

A severe flood damages several settlements.

Immediate response:
- relief funds;
- donated equipment;
- emergency transport;
- temporary clinics;
- public shelters.

Recovery:
Different assets have different funding paths. Some are publicly supported, some privately repaired, some covered by authored agreements, and some remain unfunded.

Complication:
A single large donor offers to accelerate one reconstruction project, while another settlement argues that regional funds should prioritize a less visible but more essential water system.

Long-term result:
The region’s financial architecture changes through experience: emergency reserves, mutual-aid agreements, diversified procurement and clearer records.

No theft or villain is required for the arc to remain dramatic.

# Canon questions exposed by these seeds

Before promotion, Ouros still needs authored decisions on:
- mechanical currency and prices;
- whether banking exists;
- whether loans or insurance exist;
- who can issue grants and scholarships;
- which League-linked institutions can accept sponsors;
- business ownership by PCs;
- shared-club financial authority;
- privacy of balances and agreements;
- inheritance of financial obligations;
- public funding versus private funding traditions in each region;
- how Minecraft displays sponsorship and project funding without turning the UI into accounting software.

# Ouros Drinking Water, Treatment & Distribution Seeds — Pass 113

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Requires originality, continuity, PTU/Caelo and implementation review before promotion.

Pass: 113.

These candidates assume the proposed Drinking Water, Treatment & Distribution layer. They do not establish which Ouros settlements have centralized water systems.

## 1. The Hill District Runs Dry First

A citywide source remains normal, but the highest service zone repeatedly loses pressure during evening demand peaks.

Possible threads:

- old pump capacity;
- a new housing district;
- an inaccurate demand model;
- a partially closed valve;
- an unrecorded bypass.

No sabotage assumption.

## 2. The Plant Is Fine, the Tap Is Not

Treatment-site observations are normal while one neighborhood reports unusual service-point results.

The investigation must move downstream through storage and distribution instead of blaming the source.

## 3. The Tap Is Fine, the Rumor Is Not

A widely shared message claims a district’s water is unsafe. Current observations do not support the claim, but the institution still has to investigate and communicate carefully.

Good handoff to Media/Communications and Public Memory.

## 4. The Empty Tower That Still Matters

An old elevated tank is no longer used for routine service but remains a landmark, emergency asset and piece of institutional history.

A later outage may force a review of whether it can safely return to service.

## 5. The Last Working Valve Map

Three network maps exist. Two are newer. The oldest is the only one that correctly shows a valve installed before a major redevelopment.

The correct map is not the newest map.

## 6. One Street, Two Systems

A boundary road sits between two distribution zones. One side has normal service and the other does not.

Residents understandably assume unequal treatment before the hydraulic cause is understood.

## 7. The Clinic’s Roof Tank

A rural clinic keeps a small local reserve because its normal line is unreliable.

The reserve creates resilience, but it also creates maintenance and replenishment dependencies.

## 8. The Pump That Fails Only at Festival Time

The pump is mechanically sound during ordinary demand. It repeatedly enters a degraded state during a large annual event.

The real story may be capacity, scheduling, backup power or valve configuration.

## 9. The Well and the Reservoir

A settlement normally uses a reservoir but keeps older wells as backup.

A dry year makes those obsolete records suddenly operationally important again.

## 10. The Intake Nest

A wild population begins using a protected structure near a raw-water intake.

The utility needs access. Conservation wants to understand whether the use is temporary or recurrent. The Pokémon are not automatically an obstruction to remove.

## 11. The Water Company’s Missing Hour

A monitoring station has a one-hour data gap during a service incident.

The gap may reflect communications loss, power loss, maintenance or record handling. It is evidence of uncertainty, not evidence of concealment.

## 12. Clear Water, Open Question

A visibly clear stream feeding an intake has an unresolved quality investigation.

Players who rely only on Minecraft appearance will draw the wrong conclusion.

## 13. The Dry Public Fountain

A fountain is intentionally disconnected during a water-saving period, but visitors interpret the dry basin as proof that the entire city has a shortage.

Useful Media/Tourism handoff.

## 14. The Leak Under the Old Market

A suspected trunk-main leak lies under a district where excavating the obvious route would disrupt a historic market.

Possible solutions involve alternate isolation, night work, temporary service or a more precise survey.

## 15. The Pipe That Was Never Removed

A superseded main still exists physically under a street. A later excavation exposes it and creates confusion because workers assume it is active.

Physical presence and operational state stay separate.

## 16. Emergency Water at the Station

A rail station becomes a temporary public distribution point after a district outage because it has space, transport access and staffing.

The location becomes part of public memory even after normal service returns.

## 17. The Wrong Spare Pump

A replacement pump arrives quickly but belongs to a different revision of the station.

Supply is available. The correct usable supply is not.

## 18. The Valve Nobody Was Authorized to Touch

A valve is physically accessible and has clearly been moved. The investigation must determine whether it was emergency work, maintenance, an error or unauthorized interference.

No automatic criminal framing.

## 19. The Reservoir Edge Survey

Changes in reservoir operating level expose a shoreline that becomes valuable for ecological and archaeological survey.

Utility operations, science and conservation must coordinate timing.

## 20. The Tank That Became a Landmark

A water tower is retired after a new storage system opens. Residents campaign to preserve the structure even though its utility function is gone.

Architecture/Public Memory crossover.

## 21. The New District Outgrew the Old Main

Housing and businesses expand faster than the major distribution link serving them.

The issue becomes a public-works choice among reinforcement, a second feed, new storage or demand management.

## 22. The Pokémon Everyone Blamed

A Water-type repeatedly appears beside a failing intake. Public attention focuses on it because it is visible.

Historical maintenance records show performance began degrading before the Pokémon arrived.

## 23. The Pokémon That Actually Is Blocking Access

A persistent wild individual occupies a narrow access route near an asset during the exact maintenance window.

The fact is real. The response still needs to respect Pokémon Agency and Conservation rather than assuming KO/capture is the solution.

## 24. The Boil-Notice Equivalent Without Invented Standards

An Ouros utility issues a temporary restricted-use advisory while investigating a pressure-loss incident.

The exact wording, health rationale and threshold remain authored/canon decisions. The story focuses on information delivery, emergency supply and investigation rather than custom illness mechanics.

## 25. Three Complaints, Three Causes

Three residents report “bad water” in the same week.

One case is a building-local issue, one is a distribution-zone incident and one is unrelated taste/odor with no service failure.

The generator should resist collapsing them into one mystery without evidence.

## 26. The Backup Generator That Was Never Tested Under Load

A treatment or pump site has backup power on paper. A real outage reveals that routine inspections never tested the full operating load.

Infrastructure memory matters more than villainy.

## 27. The Reopened Spring House

A historic spring facility was replaced decades ago. A regional emergency makes the institution inspect whether it can support limited non-routine use again.

Groundwater, Archives and Architecture crossover.

## 28. The Water Route Became a Park

An old above-ground conveyance corridor is no longer needed after infrastructure modernization. Parts become a linear park and habitat corridor while major easements remain for maintenance access.

Land Tenure/Road Ecology/Public Space crossover.

## 29. The Repair Fixed the Leak but Not the Problem

A major leak is repaired, yet the district continues to experience intermittent low service.

The leak was real but not the only contributing factor.

## 30. The Distribution Atlas

A long-running utility archive keeps every major revision of source links, tanks, pressure zones, failures, repairs and expansions.

Later investigators can see that what looks like one modern network is actually several historical systems layered together.

# Long Arc A — Five Summers at Meridian Waterworks

Year 1:
A high district experiences intermittent low service during a festival. Temporary measures work.

Year 2:
The utility installs monitoring and discovers that several old assumptions about demand and valve state are wrong.

Year 3:
A heat event plus population growth produces a larger outage. Emergency water distribution becomes a visible public institution.

Year 4:
A capital project adds redundancy, but construction exposes an old pipe corridor with archaeological and ecological significance.

Year 5:
A new incident tests the revised network. Some previous fixes work exactly as intended; one new bottleneck appears elsewhere.

Purpose:
Show infrastructure learning through Chronicle rather than repeatedly resetting to “water crisis solved.”

# Long Arc B — Source, Plant, City

A rural source, treatment plant and urban district form three distinct locations.

Over several years:

- the source experiences hydrological variability;
- the plant changes process revision;
- the city expands into a new elevation zone;
- a supply-chain shortage affects maintenance;
- an advisory produces public-memory consequences;
- a later outage originates in the city, not at the source.

Purpose:
Teach players that water-system causality can run through several layers and that the same visible symptom may have different origins.

# Long Arc C — The Old Main Years

An old trunk main has served several districts for decades.

Possible progression:

1. archival evidence shows several undocumented repairs;
2. a leak investigation exposes a superseded branch;
3. a public-works proposal debates replacement versus parallel redundancy;
4. construction disrupts a market and urban wildlife route;
5. the old pipe is retired but retained in parts as a historical artifact;
6. years later, maps still show it and confuse a new generation of technicians.

Purpose:
Turn infrastructure age into persistent world history instead of invisible maintenance flavor.

# Ouros Seismic Faults & Ground-Failure Seeds — Pass 71

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros candidates derived from abstract research patterns. No external plot, character or prose is transplanted.

## Design intent

These candidates use the proposed seismic layer to create stories from observation, uncertainty, local geology, infrastructure, travel, wildlife and long recovery tails.

They deliberately avoid a recurring formula of “earthquake happens → defeat Ground-type boss.”

## 1. Three Towns, One Quake

A single moderate seismic event is recorded across three nearby settlements.

One reports almost no physical damage. One has broken utility connections. The third has serious ground settlement around a reclaimed waterfront.

The story is about understanding why consequences differed rather than finding three separate causes.

Connections: Seismic, Soil, Architecture, Infrastructure, Science.

## 2. The Silent Station

One monitoring station stops transmitting minutes before a regional event.

Rumor claims the station detected the quake in advance and was suppressed. Investigation may instead find a power fault, communications outage, maintenance problem or another cause supported by evidence.

Connections: Seismic, Communications, Technology, Media, Cases.

## 3. Aftershock on the Repaired Road

A mountain road is cleared after the main event and reopened under inspection restrictions.

A later aftershock does not restore the original landslide. It triggers a fresh inspection because new cracks and small rockfalls have appeared elsewhere.

Connections: Seismic, Travel, Crisis, Public Works.

## 4. The Rain After the Slide

A slope failed during an earthquake months ago. The debris was removed and the road repaired.

During a later rainy period, instruments show renewed movement because the old failure changed drainage and vegetation.

The immediate earthquake is history; the present problem belongs to slope recovery.

Connections: Seismic, Soil, Flora, Meteorology, Travel.

## 5. Waterfront Sand Boils

After shaking, residents report fountains of water and sediment in isolated parts of a waterfront district.

The event creates a scientific/engineering investigation and targeted access restrictions. It does not automatically create battle terrain penalties.

Connections: Seismic, Freshwater, Architecture, Infrastructure, Science.

## 6. The Wrong Epicenter

Early automated estimates place an event near a rural settlement. Later sensor data shifts the solution substantially.

The first map remains historically real and may already have influenced emergency deployments, media coverage and public memory.

Connections: Seismic, Science, Media, Crisis, Cartography.

## 7. The Quarry Blast

A quarry blast is felt by residents as a short tremor shortly after a sequence of natural earthquakes.

Some people assume it is another aftershock. The network must compare timing, location and records before attribution.

Connections: Seismic, Geology, Workplaces, Media.

## 8. The Battle Everyone Blamed

A public Ground-type battle takes place shortly before nearby residents feel shaking.

The coincidence produces a rumor that one Pokémon caused the event. The system preserves the battle transcript and seismic evidence separately.

No world-scale effect is inferred from the Move used in battle.

Connections: Seismic, Battle Institutions, Media, Science.

## 9. Fault Across the Rail Cut

A new survey after an earthquake reveals surface displacement crossing an important transport cut.

The railway operator, geologists and nearby settlement need different information: safe operation now, long-term mapping and future route planning.

Connections: Seismic, Travel, Cartography, Public Works.

## 10. The Spring Changed Overnight

A spring used by a small settlement changes flow or turbidity after a quake.

The water change is real. Its cause remains open until Freshwater and Geology evidence are compared.

Connections: Seismic, Freshwater, Science, Settlements.

## 11. The Den Behind the Slide

A landslide exposes a previously inaccessible cavity used by wild Pokémon.

The discovery creates ecology and access questions. The exposed den does not automatically become a dungeon or capture zone.

Connections: Seismic, Geology, Conservation, Wild Collectives.

## 12. The Temporary Lake

A slope failure dams a tributary and forms a temporary lake.

Upstream routes, downstream water availability, fish movement and settlement risk change at different times.

The eventual breach or stabilization is a Freshwater/Seismic question, not a scripted boss phase.

Connections: Seismic, Freshwater, Fisheries, Travel, Crisis.

## 13. Two Accurate Maps

Two historical maps show a fault trace in different places.

Research reveals that one mapped surface rupture after an older earthquake while the other mapped a later scientific interpretation of the underlying fault.

Both records can be historically accurate descriptions of different things.

Connections: Seismic, Cartography, Archives, Science.

## 14. The Crooked Door District

Residents report doors and windows no longer fitting after a quake despite little visible exterior damage.

Architecture inspections reveal differential settlement in only part of the district.

The story can become a slow repair/public-works arc rather than a disaster scene.

Connections: Seismic, Architecture, Housing, Public Works.

## 15. The Sensor Pokémon Rumor

Observers report that several wild Pokémon left a valley before a later earthquake.

The chronology is interesting enough to study. It is insufficient to establish prediction ability.

A multi-year observation program may eventually find seasonal, weather, human-activity or seismic correlations—or none.

Connections: Seismic, Science, Wild Collectives, Seasonality.

## 16. Tunnel Alignment

A long utility tunnel still functions after the main event, but inspections find a small offset where it crosses a deformation zone.

Operators must decide whether to keep it in limited service, repair it or build redundancy.

Connections: Seismic, Infrastructure, Technology, Workplaces.

## 17. Museum After the Mainshock

A museum survives with its structure usable, but several collection objects move or fall.

The main work is condition assessment, custody, re-cataloguing and safe reopening rather than a combat scenario.

Connections: Seismic, Archives/Museums, Architecture, Crisis.

## 18. The Unbroken Bridge

A bridge looks intact after strong shaking and becomes a symbol of resilience in public reporting.

Its operator still restricts traffic until inspection is complete. Public appearance and operational authorization remain separate.

Connections: Seismic, Infrastructure, Media, Credentials/Permissions.

## 19. The Broken Main

Ground deformation damages a water main. The earthquake did not directly “turn off the town's water”; infrastructure failure propagates through the dependency graph.

Repair changes service state while the fault/deformation history remains unchanged.

Connections: Seismic, Infrastructure, Freshwater, Workplaces.

## 20. The Old Landslide Houses

A neighborhood was built near an old stabilized slide long before current players arrived.

New monitoring after a quake finds no immediate failure but raises questions about drainage, vegetation and future review.

The arc can remain about preparedness without requiring catastrophe.

Connections: Seismic, Soil, Housing, Governance.

## 21. The Ferry That Kept Running

Road approaches fail after an earthquake while the harbor and ferry remain operational.

The ferry suddenly becomes critical regional infrastructure and experiences demand it was never designed to handle.

Connections: Seismic, Maritime, Demography, Workplaces, Travel.

## 22. The Cave Survey Moratorium

An earthquake exposes a new cave entrance near an archaeological site.

Scientists, heritage stewards and explorers agree to a temporary access pause until stability and provenance plans exist.

The discovery remains exciting without forcing immediate dungeon entry.

Connections: Seismic, Geology, Archaeology, Agreements, Credentials.

## 23. The Misleading Crack

A large crack appears in a plaza after shaking and becomes widely photographed.

Survey later shows it follows a utility trench rather than the mapped fault trace.

The photos remain authentic. The popular interpretation changes.

Connections: Seismic, Photography, Media, Infrastructure.

## 24. The Hill That Moves Millimeters

A hillside shows slow deformation over several seasons with no dramatic collapse.

Players can help maintain sensors, compare rainfall, inspect drainage and document vegetation changes.

A meaningful ending may simply be a better assessment and revised land-use decision.

Connections: Seismic, Soil, Meteorology, Flora, Science.

## 25. Cross-Region Sensor Exchange

Two neighboring regions operate different seismic networks and data standards.

After a border-area event, each has partial coverage. Institutions create a temporary exchange protocol that may later become permanent.

Connections: Seismic, Interregional Mobility, Digital Systems, Agreements.

## 26. The Emergency Train Slowdown

A sensor network issues an early-warning message after an earthquake begins. A railway automatically slows trains before the strongest shaking reaches some districts.

The warning is useful even if the railway itself suffers no damage.

This is an infrastructure/communication success story rather than a failed prediction plot.

Connections: Seismic, Communications, Travel, Technology.

## 27. The Archive of Felt Reports

An archive contains decades of resident descriptions of past shaking, many written before modern instruments existed.

Researchers use them carefully as historical evidence while preserving uncertainty and language differences.

Connections: Seismic, Archives, Language, Public Memory, Science.

## 28. Reopened Trail, New Scarp

A route closed after a quake reopens months later with a new viewpoint created by surface deformation.

The new landscape becomes a scientific landmark and eventually a tourism pressure point.

Connections: Seismic, Travel, Tourism, Science, Conservation.

## 29. The Dam Reinspection

A dam remains operational after an event but enters an inspection protocol because of shaking exposure.

No crack or failure needs to be invented. The tension comes from water demand, cautious operations and incomplete information.

Connections: Seismic, Freshwater, Infrastructure, Crisis.

## 30. The Sequence Ends Quietly

A region experiences weeks of small aftershocks after a larger event. None produces another disaster.

The story follows inspections, reopening, declining alerts, institutional fatigue/backlog and gradual return to routine without requiring a second climax.

Connections: Seismic, Crisis, Workplaces, Media, Public Memory.

# Long Arc A — The Fault Beneath Three Towns

Year 1: a moderate event exposes that three settlements sharing one valley have different shaking and ground response.

Year 2: instrument coverage improves. One town retrofits critical infrastructure; another prioritizes route redundancy; the third funds soil/groundwater investigation.

Year 3: a smaller event tests those choices. It is not stronger, but it produces different operational consequences because the world has changed.

Year 4: historical archives reveal an older event that was remembered differently by each community.

Year 5: a regional monitoring/response compact may emerge if the participating institutions and players support it.

The arc has no required villain and no required catastrophic finale.

# Long Arc B — After the Mainshock

Week 1: response and inspections dominate.

Month 1: route clearing, temporary services and aftershock monitoring continue.

Month 3: rainfall reactivates one previously failed slope.

Month 6: reconstruction uncovers archaeological and ecological consequences.

Year 1: repaired districts have different layouts and infrastructure priorities.

Year 2: public memory begins simplifying the event in ways that archives and witnesses complicate.

The main earthquake occurs once. The campaign content comes from durable consequences.

# Long Arc C — The Valley That Shifted

A relatively small amount of permanent ground deformation crosses farmland, a road, a water channel and an old boundary marker.

Different systems react on different timelines:
- Travel needs immediate route inspection.
- Freshwater needs flow and structure checks.
- Agriculture needs irrigation assessment.
- Cartography needs new survey control.
- Archaeology may care about newly exposed material.
- Governance must decide which public works to prioritize.

The arc studies how a small physical change can reorganize a living world without treating the deformation itself as a combat mechanic.

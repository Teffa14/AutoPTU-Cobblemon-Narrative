# Ouros Place Name, Address & Location Reference Seeds — Pass 139

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS.
Date: 2026-08-29

These seeds depend on `design/place-name-address-location-reference-continuity-extension.md`. They are candidates only. Names, institutions, settlement forms and historical events must be replaced or canon-approved before use.

## The Map Changed Before the Sign

A district adopts a new street name and the current map edition already uses it. One physical sign still carries the former label because replacement work is scheduled later.

The interesting consequence is ordinary divergence: visitors following the map think they are lost; locals understand both references; an old shop receipt remains historically correct.

No fraud or sabotage is required.

## The Sign Changed Before the Directory

A public sign displays a newly adopted place name while a courier directory still routes parcels under the former descriptor.

A parcel reaches the correct service node but is held for location clarification rather than silently delivered.

Useful handoffs: Public Notices, Courier, Cartography, Archives.

## Three Names, One Bridge

A bridge has a current standardized name, a long-used local nickname and a historical engineering-project label. Three witnesses describe an event using three different names.

The mystery is solved by chronology and reference linkage rather than by deciding that two witnesses are wrong.

## Two Places Called Lantern Hill

Two nearby hills share the same colloquial name in different communities. An old field notebook says only `north side of Lantern Hill`.

Both candidate linkages remain plausible. Later evidence may resolve the reference, or the correct result may be `ACCEPTED_AMBIGUITY`.

## The Old Street That Never Moved

NPCs say that an institution `used to be on Old Market Street`. Modern players assume the institution relocated. The building never moved; the street was renamed.

The reveal can be made through old signage, receipts, map editions and neighboring businesses rather than exposition.

## The Building Moved, the Name Did Not

A service relocates to a replacement structure but keeps its familiar institutional name. Old directions correctly identify the former structure for historical events but fail for current visits.

This seed tests the opposite boundary from the previous one: stable name does not guarantee stable geometry.

## The Delivery Went to the Right Building

A courier resolves the destination structure correctly but attempts delivery at a former loading entrance that closed after renovation.

The parcel remains undelivered until an active delivery point is identified. Construction history and Courier state both matter.

## The Temporary Door Became Permanent

During a long renovation, a side entrance is designated temporarily. Local businesses, commuters and Pokémon routines adapt around it. After construction, the institution keeps the entrance because the new circulation pattern works better.

Months later, old architectural plans and current service directions legitimately disagree.

## The Station Kept Its Old Name on Tickets

A transit stop is renamed. Current signs and announcements use the new form, while tickets printed before the change and archived schedules preserve the former one.

A collector's old ticket becomes a chronology clue, not a counterfeit clue.

## The Landmark Is Gone, the Directions Survived

A tree, tower, warehouse or other landmark disappears. Residents continue saying `past the old tower` because the phrase has become part of local wayfinding.

The location reference survives the physical feature that originally motivated it.

## The Pokémon Waits at the Former Entrance

A recurring Pokémon continues visiting a former public entrance after a building changes circulation. Staff know the routine and occasionally leave through that door to meet it.

The behavior creates character continuity without proving ownership, training, employment or unusual species knowledge.

## The Shop Uses the District Name Nobody Prints

A cluster of residents and businesses use a traditional district name absent from current public maps. The name is real as a local reference even if no institution recognizes it as a standardized district.

The story can explore cultural memory without making either vocabulary set objectively false.

## The Address Was Correct When Written

An old letter appears to name a nonexistent street. A historical map shows that the descriptor was current at the time of writing.

The letter needs historical linkage, not correction.

## The Wrong North Gate

Two institutions in the same district both colloquially call an entrance `North Gate`. A dispatch request contains the phrase without a site name.

The request remains incomplete until context disambiguates it. A dispatcher must not choose based solely on text similarity.

## The Same Plaza Has Two Spellings

Two communities write the same place name differently because of language or transliteration practice. Records can link both forms to one place while preserving exactly which form each source used.

No spelling should be silently declared culturally superior without authored canon.

## Mystery — Five Times the Place Was “Renamed”

Records show five apparent renamings. Investigation reveals five different events:

1. a new local nickname spread;
2. a map corrected a spelling;
3. a sign was replaced;
4. an institution adopted a standardized form;
5. a later historical catalog linked an older variant.

Only one event may have been an actual public naming decision.

## Mystery — Four Addresses, One Building

Four records use different location descriptors for one persistent structure: an old street name, a current street name, a service entrance descriptor and a temporary construction entrance.

The solution depends on effective dates and purpose scope.

## Mystery — The Location That Cannot Be Proven

A century-old note references `the lower mill`. Two mills existed below the same ridge and all surviving landmarks changed. Evidence is insufficient.

Correct closure: `ACCEPTED_AMBIGUITY`.

The unresolved result can itself become public-memory material.

## Exploration — The Alley With Two Generations of Signs

An old commercial lane contains faded painted names behind newer panels, former entrance numbers, repurposed loading doors and businesses whose names survived relocation.

Players can reconstruct the area's history through physical layers without any combat mechanic.

Implementation status: READY with static Minecraft world state and ordinary evidence interaction.

## Exploration — The District That Starts in Three Places

Residents, current maps and an old municipal plan each draw the colloquial start of a district differently. None of those boundaries creates property or access rights.

The exploration asks players to discover why each reference exists rather than force one universal polygon.

Implementation status: READY.

## Long arc — A City Learns Its New Directions

Phase one establishes normal routines and the existing vocabulary of streets, landmarks, entrances and service points.

Phase two introduces a physical change such as reconstruction, a new transit entrance or a renamed public feature. Different systems adopt the new references at different speeds.

Phase three creates ordinary friction: parcels are held, visitors ask directions, an old notice remains visible, a dispatch report uses the traditional name and a business keeps its familiar advertising language.

Phase four resolves the major operational problems while preserving history. Current maps, old letters, local aliases, new entrances and historical signage all remain valid within their scopes.

Months later, players can see which temporary references became permanent and which older names survived socially.

The arc does not require centralized naming law, malicious misinformation or a universal postal system.

## NPC archetypes

The Walking Gazetteer remembers old names and local shortcuts. Their memory is useful evidence but not automatic authority.

The New Courier knows the current directory very well but not historic references.

The Longtime Shopkeeper uses local names that predate current signage.

The Map Editor understands edition history and corrections but does not decide physical route state.

The Records Clerk can explain when an institution changed its internal descriptor but cannot declare every other system wrong.

The Accessibility Advocate notices that the named front entrance and the usable entrance are not always the same. Their observations hand off to the appropriate access/facility owner rather than creating universal standards.

Reduced version: the valid access point is established outside combat. AutoPTU handles static combatants and geometry. Victory clears the immediate approach but does not authorize entry, correct records or complete a service transaction.

Reduced version: all place-linkage reasoning remains outside BattleSpec. Combat can secure the immediate site but can never decide which landmark a historical report meant.

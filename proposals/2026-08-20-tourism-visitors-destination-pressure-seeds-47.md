# Ouros Tourism, Visitors & Destination Pressure Seeds — Pass 47

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros material derived from structural research themes.

## 1. The Overlook Everyone Found

A once-quiet scenic ridge becomes famous after a widely shared battle photograph. The trail was built for local foot traffic, not crowds. The story is about rerouting, signage, wildlife disturbance and whether the settlement wants to invest in permanent access.

## 2. The Last Room in Town

A regional tournament fills every inn and guest room. A research team, several competitors and a family attending a memorial all need accommodation. The problem is capacity and priorities, not a hidden villain.

## 3. Returning Guest

A visitor comes back to the same lodge every year. Staff remember prior actions, but the person’s relationship to the town remains temporary unless later authored otherwise.

## 4. Guidebook from Five Years Ago

A popular visitor guide still recommends a route that was rerouted after a landslide. Tourists keep arriving at the old trailhead because copies remain in circulation.

## 5. The Quiet Valley Goes Viral

A rare seasonal bloom is photographed and publicized. Visitor interest rises before conservation staff know how sensitive the site is.

## 6. Resort Edge Encounters

Wild Pokémon begin appearing around a resort perimeter. Possible causes include food waste, habitat change, seasonal movement, quieter nearby land becoming disturbed, or learned behavior. Do not assume aggression.

## 7. Closed for Nesting

A viewpoint closes during a nesting window. Some visitors understand. Others think the closure is arbitrary because the species is not currently visible.

## 8. The Tour That Never Updated

A guided route still tells a simplified historical story that the local museum revised years ago. The guide is not malicious; the training material is old.

## 9. Festival Spillover

A small town hosts a successful annual celebration. Nearby villages now receive overflow visitors, parking, noise and business they never planned for.

## 10. The Famous Café Nobody Can Enter

A café becomes a destination after a media feature. Its success creates queues, staff burnout and pressure on neighboring businesses.

## 11. The Wrong Kind of Souvenir

Visitors begin removing ordinary stones from a heritage trail because a rumor claims they are lucky. The stones have no authored supernatural effect, but cumulative removal damages the site.

## 12. A Lodge Without a Road

A remote lodge remains open after its normal transport connection fails. Guests can stay safely, but leaving requires a different route plan.

## 13. First-Time Visitor Center

A settlement opens a modest visitor desk after a new ferry route. It gradually becomes a source of maps, warnings, guide contacts and public notices.

## 14. The Off-Season Town

A destination that is crowded for two months becomes almost empty afterward. Seasonal workers, shops and wild Pokémon all respond differently to the quiet period.

## 15. Attraction Closed, Town Still Open

A famous landmark closes for restoration. Visitors assume the whole destination has nothing else to offer. Local institutions try to redirect attention without inventing fake attractions.

## 16. Pilgrims and Sightseers

An authored sacred site receives both committed practitioners and casual visitors. The tension is about behavior, interpretation and access, not proving whose beliefs are correct.

## 17. The Photograph Queue

Visitors line up to reproduce a famous photo at the exact same spot. The resulting crowd blocks a route used by residents and creates a new maintenance problem.

## 18. The Wildlife Tour That Worked Too Well

A responsible guided wildlife program becomes popular because sightings improve. Its success itself creates pressure that may reduce future sightings.

## 19. Temporary Competitor Village

A large event creates temporary lodging, food stalls, medical capacity and transport services. After the event, the settlement must decide what remains useful.

## 20. Two Resorts, Two Futures

Two neighboring communities pursue different visitor strategies. One invests in high-volume access. The other limits capacity and emphasizes guided experiences. Neither is automatically morally correct.

## 21. The Guest Who Knows More Than the Guide

A returning researcher knows a specific valley better than a newly trained local guide. Formal role and actual expertise diverge.

## 22. The Missing Shuttle

A visitor-management plan assumes a seasonal shuttle exists. Staffing shortages cancel it, causing crowding at a trailhead that was designed around reduced private traffic.

## 23. The Museum Rush

A newly displayed fossil draws crowds. The museum can physically admit visitors, but conservation staff cannot safely sustain the handling, tours and security schedule at that pace.

## 24. Night Tourism

A town promotes nighttime viewing of a harmless natural phenomenon. The new schedule changes noise, lighting, transport demand and nocturnal Pokémon observations.

## 25. The Town That Became a Shortcut

A new transport link makes a quiet settlement a transfer stop. Many people pass through without intending to visit. Businesses and residents disagree about whether that counts as opportunity.

## 26. The Resort Worker’s Season

A recurring seasonal employee has relationships with coworkers, wild Pokémon and returning guests. Their career can progress even if the player only visits the destination occasionally.

## 27. Visitor Map vs Research Map

The public map intentionally omits a sensitive habitat. Visitors notice that researcher vehicles appear to use an unmarked route and begin speculating online.

## 28. The Anniversary Crowd

A famous historical event reaches a major anniversary. Commemorations, tourists, descendants, journalists and researchers all arrive with different expectations.

## 29. Weather Window

A mountain attraction is accessible only during a broad seasonal weather window. A week of unusual conditions compresses demand into fewer safe days.

## 30. The Destination That Says No

A settlement decides not to promote a newly discovered site yet. The story follows information control, public pressure, conservation review and local debate rather than a forced reveal.

# Longer arcs

## Arc A — From Stopover to Destination

A small settlement begins as a transport stop. A public event, one successful restoration project and repeated media coverage gradually increase visitor interest. Over several seasons the town gains lodging, guides and services, then faces questions about crowding, housing, habitat pressure and which parts of growth residents actually want.

## Arc B — The Protected Coast

A coastal route becomes famous for seasonal Pokémon observations. The first year brings curiosity. The second brings tour operators. The third creates ecological pressure and resident disagreement. Players can contribute through research, guiding, infrastructure, conservation, media or business, with no single correct growth model.

## Arc C — The Old Road Pilgrimage

An authored regional tradition follows an old route through several settlements and historic sites. Modern transport has made most of the path unnecessary, but participants still choose to walk it. Over time the route intersects restoration, local businesses, protected habitats and competing interpretations of tradition. The story tracks the route’s meaning without declaring any supernatural claim true unless canon later does so.

Dependencies: complete movement/interception; AI tactical policy; terrain/weather/hazards if present; adapter/playback.

REDUCED version: evacuation resolves outside the grid. Any battle uses only verified legal combat. Wildlife motivation remains unresolved until evidence supports it.

Dependencies: complete movement/interception/forced movement; terrain/zones/reactions; AI tactical policy; adapter/playback.

REDUCED version: closures, crowd flow and protection happen in world state. Combat, if any, occurs on a fixed arena with no invented crowd or zone mechanics.

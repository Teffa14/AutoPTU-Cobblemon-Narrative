# Ouros Cartography, Surveying & Wayfinding Seeds — Pass 43

Status: NON-CANON proposals. These are original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research patterns. No external plots, prose, characters or maps are copied.

## Seed 1 — The Road That Is Still on Every Map

A public road remains printed on current regional maps even though a landslide closed it months ago.

The interesting problem is not merely clearing the road. Different institutions updated at different speeds, so travelers, emergency services and businesses are acting on conflicting map editions.

Possible outputs:
- correction campaign;
- temporary signage;
- rerouted transport;
- business impact;
- public frustration;
- old map preserved as evidence of when the closure occurred.

## Seed 2 — Two Entrances, One Cave

Two towns each know a cave entrance and believe their cave is local and separate.

A survey team suspects the two systems connect underground.

The full arc can involve:
- independent route traces;
- geological comparison;
- old maps;
- competing local names;
- a newly discovered passage;
- later decisions about tourism, conservation or infrastructure.

Discovering a connection does not settle who owns or administers anything.

## Seed 3 — The Survey Marker in the Wrong Valley

A century-old marker appears far from where historical maps place it.

Possible explanations remain open:
- map error;
- marker relocation;
- landslide;
- reused marker stone;
- incorrect transcription of the old survey;
- deliberate movement for an unknown reason.

The marker itself is evidence, not the answer.

## Seed 4 — Field Map with a Missing Settlement

A widely used old route map omits a small settlement that definitely existed at the time.

That omission becomes a historical question rather than proof of conspiracy.

## Seed 5 — The Shortcut Everyone Knows Except the Map Office

Local workers routinely use a safe unofficial trail that no official regional map records.

Players can decide whether to:
- submit it for inclusion;
- keep it unofficial;
- document only part of it;
- raise concerns about habitat disturbance;
- help improve signage.

## Seed 6 — The Shortcut the Map Knows but Locals Avoid

An official trail is perfectly real and physically passable, but locals stopped using it after repeated ordinary problems.

The map is geographically correct while operational knowledge has changed.

## Seed 7 — Moving Riverbank

Seasonal erosion causes the practical river crossing to move every year.

A map should represent a crossing zone and recent observations rather than pretend one coordinate is permanent.

## Seed 8 — The Club Map Wall

A player-run exploration club maintains a physical map wall.

Members can add:
- surveyed trails;
- old expedition routes;
- camp locations;
- public hazards;
- missing segments;
- research notes.

Private discoveries remain optional and are not copied automatically.

## Seed 9 — The Incorrectly Famous Peak

A popular tourist map labels one summit as the highest point in the district.

A careful survey suggests a less dramatic ridge may actually be slightly higher.

The story can involve pride, measurement, tourism and institutional correction without making anyone dishonest.

## Seed 10 — Outdated Emergency Map

A shelter map predates a major district reconstruction.

During a crisis, the problem becomes immediately operational.

The map failure can later drive a public-works review.

## Seed 11 — The Unnamed Trail

Players repeatedly use and document a route that has no formal name.

Different groups start using different informal names.

Canon should not select one automatically until a naming process or accepted usage emerges.

## Seed 12 — Map of a Lost Coastline

An archive holds a reliable map of the coast before decades of erosion or construction.

It becomes valuable for:
- archaeology;
- ecological comparison;
- old property records;
- missing wreck research;
- understanding historical transport.

## Seed 13 — Survey Team with Conflicting Instruments

Two teams map the same cliff route and disagree.

The issue turns out to be a calibration or method difference rather than incompetence.

## Seed 14 — The Redacted Nesting Map

A conservation institution publishes habitat distribution but deliberately removes exact nesting coordinates.

Players working on an authorized survey can see more detail than the public.

The private map must remain private in multiplayer UI.

## Seed 15 — The Map Seller with Perfectly Legal Old Stock

A shop still sells an old edition because the stock is legal and cheap.

The cover clearly states the publication year, but travelers often assume it is current.

The conflict is information literacy, not fraud.

## Seed 16 — The Trail Sign Nobody Updated

A physical sign points toward a bridge that was removed.

The digital map is correct.

This creates a simple example where world signage and map data diverge.

## Seed 17 — The Landmark That Fell

A distinctive rock formation used for generations as a navigation landmark collapses naturally.

The route still exists, but old route descriptions become less useful.

## Seed 18 — Fog Season Survey

A mountain survey must be repeated in another season because visibility prevented reliable landmark confirmation.

Failure creates a better later mission rather than an arbitrary penalty.

## Seed 19 — The Player Annotation That Becomes Important

A player casually marks an unusual water sound during an expedition.

Months later, a different research project finds the note relevant.

The original annotation retains authorship and context.

## Seed 20 — The Wrong Cave Name

Two maps use different names for the same cave because each was published from a different settlement's perspective.

The conflict is cultural and historical, not factual.

## Seed 21 — The Transit Diagram Is Not Geography

A simplified transit map makes two stops look adjacent even though they are physically far apart.

A traveler mistakes schematic convenience for real geography.

The design lesson becomes explicit inside the world.

## Seed 22 — The Map That Was Correct Yesterday

A storm creates a new channel, collapse or debris field overnight.

The map is not “wrong”; it is stale.

## Seed 23 — Surveying the Underground from Above

Researchers notice surface sinkholes and old well records that may reveal an unknown underground network.

The first stage is inference. Direct access still needs field confirmation.

## Seed 24 — The Memorial Path

An old commemorative walking route has gradually changed as streets and buildings changed.

The community debates whether the map should preserve the historical route or show the modern walking equivalent.

## Seed 25 — The Guide Who Draws Memory Maps

A local guide creates hand-drawn maps from lived experience rather than formal surveying.

They can be operationally excellent while geometrically approximate.

## Seed 26 — The Research Heatmap Problem

A public ecology heatmap appears to show a species absence.

The actual issue is lack of observations in that area.

This reinforces survey coverage versus population truth.

## Seed 27 — Cartographers in Disagreement

Two respected institutions disagree on whether a seasonal trail should count as an official route.

Both maps accurately show different definitions.

## Seed 28 — The Abandoned Road Becomes Habitat

An obsolete road disappears from transportation maps but becomes increasingly important on conservation maps.

The same geometry gains a different functional identity.

## Seed 29 — Mapping After the Flood

A major flood changes multiple route segments, buildings and river edges.

Players help build the first provisional map, then return over several months as repairs and ecology stabilize.

## Seed 30 — The First Complete Map That Is Immediately Incomplete

A major regional survey project celebrates publication of a new comprehensive map.

Within weeks:
- a cave entrance is discovered;
- a seasonal crossing closes;
- a new service opens;
- an old trail is restored.

The point is that a living world can never have a permanently final map.

# Longer Arc A — The Atlas Years

A regional institute begins a multi-year atlas project.

Year 1:
Players survey major routes and public services.

Year 2:
The project adds ecological and historical layers.

Year 3:
A crisis exposes stale assumptions and missing rural coverage.

Year 4:
Players help reconcile local maps, institutional maps and player-contributed notes.

Year 5:
The atlas is published, preserving both a current edition and historical survey records.

The story continues because future changes create new editions rather than invalidating the entire project.

# Longer Arc B — The Underways

Several settlements possess unrelated maps of caves, wells, mines and cellars.

Players slowly discover that many segments connect into one large subsurface system.

The arc can braid:
- geology;
- archaeology;
- infrastructure;
- old transport;
- wild habitat;
- missing-person cases;
- conservation;
- water systems.

The final result is a network map, not necessarily a boss fight.

# Longer Arc C — The Map War That Is Not a War

Two regions publish different maps of a shared frontier.

At first the disagreement looks political.

Investigation reveals several independent causes:
- old survey baselines;
- seasonal river movement;
- different naming traditions;
- an obsolete road;
- differing definitions of a protected zone.

The arc becomes a long collaborative survey and negotiation process rather than a villain plot.

- targeting/footprints/range/LoS;
- base movement legality;
- complete movement including forced movement if slopes can displace combatants;
- core calculations;

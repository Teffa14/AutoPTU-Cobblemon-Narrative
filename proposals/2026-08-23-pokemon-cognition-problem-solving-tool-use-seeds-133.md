# Proposed Ouros Seeds — Pokémon Cognition, Problem Solving, and Tool Use — Pass 133

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS.
Date: 2026-08-23

These candidates derive from the Pass 133 research scan. None changes PTU mechanics, species canon, established Ouros lore, or Pokémon capabilities by itself.

## 1. The Latch That Changed

A recurring wild Pokémon has opened the same workshop gate for years. Maintenance replaces the latch. The next observations record repeated use of the old method, abandonment, exploration, and eventually a different route.

Use: longitudinal problem-solving history without a general intelligence score.

## 2. Hammer Version Seven

A persistent hammer-making Pokémon is associated with seven physically distinct tool revisions across several years. Earlier versions survive as scrap, photographs, workshop notes, or museum candidates.

Use: cognition + Material Culture provenance.

## 3. The Tool Everyone Thought Was One Tool

Researchers initially believe one distinctive object has been carried between three sites. Material analysis later shows there were three near-identical objects.

Use: identity/provenance correction without rewriting the behavioral observations.

## 4. The Easy Door, the Hard Door

A Pokémon quickly opens a complicated-looking door but repeatedly fails at a visually simple one because the second requires a body movement it cannot perform comfortably.

Use: morphology and affordance as alternatives to intelligence labels.

## 5. The Stone Under the Gate

A Pokémon repeatedly places a loose stone under a gate before carrying food through. Years later the gate is replaced and the same method stops working.

Use: task versions and strategy revision.

## 6. The Shortcut Was Not Learned

Several individuals use the same narrow route around a barrier. Researchers suspect social learning until old maps reveal that the route is the most obvious remaining passage.

Use: convergent solution versus transmission.

## 7. The Workshop Visitor

A wild Pokémon begins moving harmless discarded parts into consistent arrangements behind a workshop. Staff initially call it theft; later observation shows many parts are returned or ignored.

Use: avoid intent/ownership assumptions.

## 8. The Part That Was Too Heavy

A previously successful object-use behavior disappears after a replacement component becomes heavier. The Pokémon still performs the same preparatory sequence.

Use: physical constraint versus cognition.

## 9. The Familiar Box, New Opening

A research box gains a second access method. The same Pokémon persists with the old method for several sessions before trying the new one.

Use: flexibility and perseveration as observations, not personality labels.

## 10. The First Observed Tool Is Twenty Years Old

An archival photograph reveals that a behavior celebrated as a recent innovation existed decades earlier.

Use: first observed versus first performed.

## 11. The Object With Bite Marks

A shaped object near a feeding site resembles a manufactured tool. Repeated observations never show it being used.

Use: object shape alone does not prove tool use.

## 12. The Orchard Hook

An individual repeatedly uses a naturally fallen hooked branch to pull unreachable fruit closer. A later storm removes that branch.

Use: specific external-object use without crafting mechanics.

## 13. The Replacement That Was Rejected

Researchers provide a cleaner replica of a repeatedly used object. The Pokémon ignores it and continues searching for the original.

Use: preference, familiarity, material property, or prior history remain competing explanations.

## 14. The Broken Routine After Evolution

A persistent Pokémon evolves and can no longer perform an old manipulation in exactly the same way because its body changed. It may adapt, abandon the behavior, or use another route.

Use: Evolution + persistent identity + morphology.

## 15. Former Partner, Familiar Puzzle

A released former partner is later observed solving a type of access problem it encountered while living with a Trainer. Whether the solution was retained from that period or reacquired later remains uncertain.

Use: Agency history without ownership or command authority.

## 16. The Problem That Solved Itself

A research team reports apparent puzzle success. Video review shows wind moved the relevant object before the Pokémon touched it.

Use: false attribution and observation quality.

## 17. Three Attempts, No Quest

A wild Pokémon spends part of one afternoon trying to open a discarded container, fails, and leaves. Nothing else happens.

Use: Chronicle can record interesting behavior without generating a quest.

## 18. The Object Was Baiting the Observer

A camera position or research apparatus changes how the Pokémon approaches the task, making the study itself part of the environment.

Use: Research Ethics + observer effects.

## 19. Scrap Yard Seasons

The materials available in a scrap yard change with manufacturing cycles. A tool-making population shifts material preferences over time.

Use: Supply Chains/Manufacturing → behavioral context without crafting bonuses.

## 20. Oranguru’s Signals

Observers document a persistent Oranguru using repeated signals before other forest Pokémon move. Competing interpretations include leadership, response to a shared cue, learned routine, or species-typical coordination.

Use: complex behavior without converting it into Trainer Orders.

## 21. The Tool Cache Nobody Owns

Several external objects accumulate in a sheltered location used by multiple wild Pokémon. No evidence establishes which individual brought each object.

Use: custody/provenance uncertainty.

## 22. The Bridge Piece

A Pokémon repeatedly drags one loose board to span a small gap. After public works repair the bridge, the behavior disappears because the problem disappears.

Use: infrastructure can remove a behavioral opportunity without population decline.

## 23. The New Lock Changed the Ecology

A redesigned waste enclosure prevents one recurrent Pokémon from accessing food scraps. Its activity shifts elsewhere in the district.

Use: Urban Wildlife + Waste + problem-solving response.

## 24. The Floating Object

A Pokémon repeatedly uses floating debris to reach a stable resting point. Researchers disagree whether the object is intentionally positioned or merely tolerated after drift.

Use: high uncertainty; no Swim/current mechanics invented.

## 25. The Demonstration Comes Later

An individual independently develops a solution. Months later another Pokémon witnesses it and later performs a similar method.

Use: clean handoff from individual innovation to Pass 125 Social Learning.

## 26. The Training History Nobody Recorded

A supposedly wild problem-solving prodigy is later linked to an old institutional care record. Prior training may explain some behavior, but the record is incomplete.

Use: Identity/Agency/Archives uncertainty.

## 27. The Puzzle With No Better Solution

A multi-access apparatus is modified until every path requires the same physical action. The study can no longer distinguish strategy flexibility and is retired rather than forcing a conclusion.

Use: good science includes abandoning a bad protocol.

## 28. The Tool Became a Landmark

An unusually large external object repeatedly used by one Pokémon remains at the site after the behavior stops. Visitors begin treating it as a landmark before its function is understood.

Use: Tourism/Public Memory/Material Culture connection.

## 29. The Same Result, Different Strategy

Two Pokémon reach the same goal using visibly different action sequences. The world records both without ranking one as smarter.

Use: individual variation.

## 30. The Behavioral Atlas

A regional research program catalogs task-specific behaviors across decades, preserving videos, failed attempts, object provenance, environmental versions, and confidence levels rather than assigning species intelligence rankings.

Use: durable Science/Archives content.

# Longer Arc A — Five Latches at Southworks

Year 1: staff discover that one persistent wild Pokémon uses a maintenance latch after closing time.

Year 2: the latch is repaired for unrelated safety reasons; the old method fails.

Year 3: the same individual begins using a drainage-side access route. Researchers debate whether this is strategy switching or discovery of an already familiar route.

Year 4: another individual appears while the first performs the new route. Later observations may or may not support social transmission.

Year 5: redevelopment removes both old access points and creates a safe wildlife passage. The behavior disappears because the environment no longer presents the same problem.

Possible outcomes:

- the original individual adapts again;
- the behavior ends;
- a new access pattern emerges independently;
- Social Learning receives evidence of transmission;
- the study concludes that prior interpretations were too strong.

No outcome grants a general Intelligence score.

# Longer Arc B — The Hammer Archive

A persistent tool-making Pokémon is observed through several life stages and environmental changes.

The story can connect:

- scrap provenance;
- successive tool revisions;
- theft/loss/damage without assuming human property concepts;
- changes after evolution;
- manufacturing waste policy;
- public photography;
- eventual collection of discarded earlier tools by a research institution or museum;
- uncertainty about which modifications were intentional versus material constraints.

The living Pokémon never becomes a museum object. Its tools may become collection objects if provenance and institutional authority support that transition.

# Longer Arc C — The Puzzle Atlas

Three research stations use comparable but non-identical harmless tasks across several regions.

At first, similar solutions appear to imply one widespread cognitive pattern.

Later years reveal a mixture of:

- species-typical behavior;
- individual innovation;
- prior training;
- local affordances;
- social transmission;
- observer effects;
- failures caused by morphology;
- ambiguous cases that remain unresolved.

The final atlas is more valuable because it preserves uncertainty rather than ranking species by intelligence.

# Encounter Contract — Workshop Latch Study

## FULL

A persistent Pokémon attempts to reach a known access point while a separate disturbance produces a combat encounter. The target may try an old method, reposition around objects, abandon a blocked route, and withdraw.

Required families:

- targeting/footprints/range/LoS — VERIFIED;
- base movement legality — VERIFIED;
- complete movement including push/pull/knockback/interception/forced movement — BLOCKING for moving environmental objects/interception;
- core calculations — VERIFIED;
- action economy/initiative — VERIFIED;
- full turn/round lifecycle — PARTIAL;
- full stateful damage pipeline — PARTIAL;
- status lifecycle — PARTIAL if used;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions — BLOCKING if the changing workshop state affects tactical spaces;
- move-specific behavior — PARTIAL;
- abilities — PARTIAL;
- items — PARTIAL if a tool becomes mechanically active;
- Trainer Features/perks — PARTIAL;
- AI legal-action infrastructure — VERIFIED;
- AI tactical policy — BLOCKING for `REACH_ACCESS_POINT`, `TRY_ALTERNATIVE`, `WITHDRAW`;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback — BLOCKING.

## REDUCED

Resolve the cognitive interaction before the battle. Record attempts and the final access state in world state. Freeze geometry. If conflict remains, run a conventional static battle. The result cannot determine what the Pokémon understood.

# Encounter Contract — Floodgate Debris Problem

## FULL

A known refuge route no longer works after hydrological changes. A persistent Pokémon may interact with loose objects while water and other actors alter access.

Primary blockers:

- complete movement including object displacement/forced movement;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions if water/debris creates live tactical effects;
- AI tactical policy for environmental objective solving;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics playback.

## REDUCED

Freshwater/Hydrology and cognition resolve the access/refuge state outside battle. Any subsequent fight uses the resulting static safe geometry.

# Encounter Contract — Tool Recovery at Scrap Yard

## FULL

A Pokémon returns to a known material site during a conflict. The intended version can include object selection, carrying, route choice, withdrawal, and interruption.

If object manipulation is tactical, complete movement, AI tactical policy, items where mechanically relevant, and adapter playback are required.

## REDUCED

Material selection, tool revision, worker response, and Pokémon agency resolve first in world state. AutoPTU handles only an independent static confrontation.

# Non-combat Contract — Multi-Access Study

A harmless apparatus offers several access methods. Across task versions, methods are blocked or changed. The same persistent individuals can be observed over months or years.

No battle capability is required.

Research Ethics controls participation and stop conditions. Science controls the protocol and conclusions. This layer stores task-specific behavior. Social Learning receives only later evidence of transmission.

# Canon gates

Do not promote any seed until Ouros authors decide:

- which species/populations have authored object-use behavior;
- which individual behaviors should emerge only from Chronicle;
- what world objects autonomous Pokémon may safely manipulate;
- how player-built research apparatuses are governed;
- what evidence threshold qualifies as tool use;
- how prior training is represented;
- how much behavioral state advances offline.

No proposal creates Intelligence ranks, puzzle DCs, crafting recipes, improvised weapon rules, Trainer Orders, lock-bypass abilities, or cognition-based battle modifiers.

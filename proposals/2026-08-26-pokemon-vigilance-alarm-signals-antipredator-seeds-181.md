# Ouros Candidates — Pass 181 — Vigilance, Alarm Signals and Anti-Predator Behavior

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANON
Date: 2026-08-26

These candidates derive from the Pass 181 research. None is canon until reviewed.

1. The Lookout Who Stopped Watching — a persistent wild individual once observed taking frequent lookout positions no longer does so; age, injury, social change and simple coincidence remain separate hypotheses.
2. The Alarm Everyone Believed — a settlement closes a trail after repeated warning behavior, but no external threat is confirmed.
3. Three Calls, Three Meanings — researchers discover that calls previously grouped together correlate with different receiver responses.
4. The All-Clear Nobody Recorded — historical recordings captured alarms but missed the quieter signal associated with returning to baseline behavior.
5. The Rail Noise Season — a new timetable overlaps a feeding window and alarm reception becomes harder to interpret.
6. One Watchog, No Sentinel System — a famous individual repeatedly stands watch, while the rest of the population shows no evidence of formal role rotation.
7. The Rotating Lookout — a collective appears to share vigilance among several members rather than relying on one leader.
8. The Empty Lookout Post — a traditional observation point is unused this year even though the group remains nearby.
9. The False Predator — a harmless maintenance machine repeatedly triggers an anti-predator response.
10. The Predator Nobody Saw — multiple receivers respond to warnings, but the original trigger is never observed.
11. The Quiet Predator — a known predator is present without producing the expected alarm pattern.
12. The Juveniles Learn Late — younger members initially ignore a warning cue and later begin responding; Social Learning remains a hypothesis until supported.
13. The Newcomer Understands — a recently arrived individual responds immediately to a local call, weakening the assumption that the signal must be learned locally.
14. Mixed-Species Breakfast — two species forage together and one reacts to the other's alarm signal; Interspecies Ecology owns any persistent relation.
15. The Signal That Crossed the River — one population appears to respond to calls from another population with which it has little direct contact.
16. The Mobbing Tree — several species repeatedly approach one tree while calling; the target is initially unknown.
17. The Harmless Owl-Shaped Sign — a new roadside sign triggers repeated approach-and-warning behavior before habituation is observed.
18. The Dangerous Silence — public concern rises because a familiar warning repertoire is absent, but sampling coverage was poor.
19. The Alarm After Dark — warning behavior moves into a nighttime period after urban lighting changes.
20. The Feeding Cost — a group remains healthy but leaves a productive patch earlier whenever alarms increase.
21. The Shelter Bottleneck — several species use the same safe refuge after alarms, creating a temporary access conflict without changing territorial claims.
22. The Signal in the Archive — an old recording reveals that a supposedly new warning call existed decades earlier.
23. The Wrong Emitter — a famous alarm recording is later attributed to a mimic or different species.
24. The Survey That Created Vigilance — repeated close approaches by researchers alter the very behavior the project is trying to measure.
25. The Former Partner Warns the Group — a released persistent Pokémon emits a warning around wild companions; the event does not restore partnership or command authority.
26. The Alarm Became Tourism — visitors gather to hear a famous call, increasing disturbance and changing its frequency.
27. The All-Clear Festival Myth — a community tradition interprets one call as a blessing; research supports a behavioral function but not the folklore explanation.
28. The Mobbing That Wasn't Defense — repeated approach behavior later proves linked to a resource rather than a threat.
29. The Highway Reduced False Alarms — after a barrier redesign, warning behavior becomes less frequent for reasons still under review.
30. Nothing Happened at Watch Point Four — a full season of normal vigilance becomes the best baseline for later comparison.

## Long arc — Five Years at Cedar Meadow

Year one establishes ordinary feeding, watch behavior and several signal types. A road project changes acoustic conditions in year two. Researchers initially interpret increased vigilance as higher predator pressure. By year three, monitoring suggests some calls are being masked while other signals remain effective. A quieter route treatment in year four changes receiver behavior. Year five tests whether the collective returns to earlier use patterns. No single year proves the causal model.

## Long arc — The Lookout Lineage That Wasn't

Residents believe the same family line has supplied lookout Pokémon for generations. Identity records, photographs and field notes gradually show that several unrelated individuals occupied the same visible perch at different times. The social tradition remains meaningful even when the biological story changes.

## Long arc — The Warning Network

Several species around a wetland appear to respond to each other's signals. Over many seasons, Community Science, Passive Acoustics and direct observation separate shared-threat responses from actual cross-species information use. Some edges become supported, some disappear and some remain unresolved.

## Non-combat scenario — Which Signal Means Danger?

Three seasons of recordings produce competing interpretations of a signal repertoire. Soundscapes, Passive Acoustics, Wild Collectives and this layer compare emitter, receiver and context records. No battle is required and `UNRESOLVED` is an acceptable result.

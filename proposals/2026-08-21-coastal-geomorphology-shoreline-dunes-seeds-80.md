# Coastal Geomorphology, Shoreline & Dunes Seeds — Pass 80

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research structures. No external plot, character or prose is imported.

## Candidate 01 — The Beach That Moved Overnight

A strong storm leaves survey stakes dozens of meters from the new waterline. Residents disagree over whether the beach was lost, shifted or only temporarily reworked. Players compare old photographs, current profiles and storm records before anyone commits to rebuilding an access path.

## Candidate 02 — Dune Gap After the Storm

A narrow gap opens through a dune ridge and creates an unexpected route toward the shore. It is physically passable for a short period but crosses a sensitive habitat and may close naturally. Travel, Conservation and Tourism actors want different things from the same opening.

## Candidate 03 — The Old Boardwalk Ends in Sand

A boardwalk built to reach the beach now stops well inland because the shoreline advanced. The town can extend it, leave the new dune field alone or redesign the access entirely. Old postcards prove the structure was once correctly positioned.

## Candidate 04 — Sandygast at the Survey Stakes

Several Sandygast repeatedly appear around shoreline-survey markers. One group believes they are moving the markers; another suspects visitors are disturbing both the survey and the Pokémon. The quest is solved by observation and evidence before any species-specific conclusion is accepted.

## Candidate 05 — Palossand on the Nourished Beach

A recently nourished beach attracts a Palossand. Its presence becomes a symbol in public debate even though no one knows whether it prefers the new sediment, arrived coincidentally or is simply passing through. The project tracks the Pokémon as an individual without assigning it control over the beach.

## Candidate 06 — The Missing Access Path

A familiar path through the dunes is gone after several weeks of wind and sand accumulation. Nothing catastrophic happened. The local question is whether to reopen the same alignment or establish a route that can move as the dune system changes.

## Candidate 07 — Two Maps, One Shoreline

Two respected maps place the coast in different locations. Both were accurate when published. Players reconstruct the shoreline history and discover that a current land-use dispute depends on treating an old map as if it were current.

## Candidate 08 — The Buried Steps Reappear

Storm erosion exposes stone steps beneath a beach. They could belong to an older boardwalk, a forgotten building or something much earlier. Exposure creates an archaeological case and access problem without assigning ownership or age automatically.

## Candidate 09 — The House That Was Inland

A coastal house once stood comfortably behind a dune ridge. Years of retreat have moved the edge closer. The story follows residents, surveyors, public works and habitat managers deciding what to protect, move or abandon without declaring one option morally correct.

## Candidate 10 — Nesting Behind the New Dune

Natural sand accumulation forms a low dune where a flat recreational area existed before. Pokémon begin using the sheltered side during a sensitive season. A simple proposal to flatten the dune now has ecological consequences that did not exist when the beach plan was written.

## Candidate 11 — The Borrow Sand Question

A nourishment project has authorization and funding but still needs compatible material. Candidate borrow sites create different transport, habitat and provenance consequences. The players' role is investigation and field verification rather than deciding the project by combat.

## Candidate 12 — The Wrack Line Archive

A researcher has photographed the storm-wrack line on the same beach for twelve years. One season's pattern is completely different. The change may reflect storms, currents, visitor cleanup, vegetation or a new upstream source of debris.

## Candidate 13 — After the Sea Wall

A protective structure appears to have succeeded in front of one district while the neighboring beach narrows. The system records observations and competing hypotheses rather than announcing the wall as the cause. Players build the evidence graph across multiple shoreline segments.

## Candidate 14 — The New Tidal Cut

A storm cuts a temporary channel through a barrier segment. Water, fish and small craft begin using it before anyone knows whether it will persist. Maritime and Estuary layers handle navigation/water consequences while Coastal state tracks the physical breach.

## Candidate 15 — Festival Moved Inland

A yearly beach festival loses its traditional footprint because the dune face has changed. The organizers can relocate inland, reduce the event, split activities across several sites or skip a year. Public Memory preserves why the old layout mattered.

## Candidate 16 — The Photograph from Before the Storm

A famous photograph draws visitors to recreate the exact composition. The landmark still exists, but the beach shape no longer allows the same viewpoint. Tourism pressure around the old camera position becomes the real problem.

## Candidate 17 — Lighthouse Path Retreat

The lighthouse remains structurally sound but the walking path along the cliff edge keeps retreating. Each repair lasts for a different length of time. The long-term arc can eventually shift access inland while the lighthouse itself stays in service.

## Candidate 18 — The Sandbar That Became an Island

A recurring sandbar remains exposed longer than usual and vegetation begins to establish. Residents start calling it an island. The world-state model refuses to promote that social label into permanent geography until repeated surveys justify a new classification.

## Candidate 19 — The Beach With No Sandygast

A beach known for Sandygast has none during a seasonal survey. The absence can trigger research but does not prove population loss. Tide timing, visitor pressure, sampling time, relocation or a real ecological change remain competing explanations.

## Candidate 20 — The Shoaling Entrance

A small harbor entrance becomes increasingly difficult to use. Maritime owns service consequences while Coastal/Estuary observations investigate sediment accumulation. Dredging may solve access and create another question about material placement.

## Candidate 21 — The Cliff Path Closure

A popular coastal trail is closed after a new fracture appears near the edge. The closure itself is straightforward; the story comes from guides, residents and visitors adapting to a route that may never reopen in its old alignment.

## Candidate 22 — What the Storm Uncovered

A storm exposes a layer containing old timber, ceramics and modern utility debris together. The mixed deposit prevents an easy historical story. Archaeology, Infrastructure and Material Provenance must separate several periods before interpretation.

## Candidate 23 — Uneven Dune Recovery

Three adjacent dune segments were damaged in the same storm. One rebuilds quickly, one barely changes and one continues to erode. Players investigate vegetation, foot traffic, sediment supply and local geometry rather than assuming a single regional recovery rate.

## Candidate 24 — The Overwash Fan Trail

A broad overwash deposit creates a temporary easy walking surface behind the dunes. Visitors quickly begin using it as a shortcut. The shortcut may interfere with recovery, but closing it requires a credible alternate route.

## Candidate 25 — The Access-versus-Habitat Boardwalk

A proposed raised access route would concentrate visitors and protect some dune vegetation while crossing another sensitive area. The best solution may be seasonal routing, multiple smaller accesses or no new structure. Nothing requires a villain.

## Candidate 26 — The Old Sand Pit

Historical records show that sand was removed from a coastal segment decades ago. The old extraction area is now partly vegetated and culturally forgotten. A current erosion study asks whether the historical removal still matters or whether present drivers dominate.

## Candidate 27 — Five Years of Dune Stakes

A school or research club maintains simple dune-monitoring stakes year after year. The resulting record becomes more valuable than any single measurement and eventually informs a major project. This can make low-stakes fieldwork pay off much later.

## Candidate 28 — The Boardwalk That Learned to Move

A settlement replaces a repeatedly damaged fixed boardwalk with modular sections designed for relocation. The project becomes a visible example of institutional learning rather than a permanent victory over the coast.

## Candidate 29 — The Famous Beach Without Its Famous Shape

A beach retains its name, traditions and social importance even after several storms completely alter the shape shown in old posters. The story explores cultural continuity without demanding physical restoration to an earlier snapshot.

## Candidate 30 — The Shoreline Atlas

Several institutions combine old charts, survey monuments, photographs and new measurements into a versioned regional shoreline atlas. Missing years and conflicting methods remain visible. Future quests can use the atlas as evidence rather than an omniscient map.

# Long Arc A — Five Storms at Lantern Coast

Year one establishes beaches, dunes, visitor routes and baseline ecology. A moderate storm damages one access but the system mostly recovers.

Year two brings a different storm direction and exposes an old foundation. Archaeology and tourism appear before the physical coast has stabilized.

Year three sees a public-works project on one segment. Monitoring later finds that neighboring areas respond differently than expected.

Year four has no major storm but continued gradual change. This prevents every shoreline story from being an emergency.

Year five brings a severe event. Players and institutions now act using four years of measurements, old decisions, preparedness projects and public memory. The ending is a revised coast with accumulated history, not restoration to the year-one map.

# Long Arc B — The Barrier That Walked

A long barrier coast slowly migrates landward over several years. Old paths disappear, overwash opens new low areas and infrastructure that once sat inland approaches the active shore.

The arc connects Coastal, Maritime, Estuary, Architecture, Conservation and Public Memory. Decisions can include retreating structures, creating new access, protecting a limited asset, allowing natural migration in another segment or maintaining monitoring only.

The barrier remains one persistent geographic entity through every revision.

# Long Arc C — The Shoreline Atlas Years

A small survey project begins after residents notice that historical maps disagree. Players help recover photographs, locate old survey markers and make new observations.

Over time the atlas becomes shared infrastructure for crisis planning, tourism, archaeology and conservation. Later discoveries can correct earlier interpretations without deleting the old editions.

The climax is institutional rather than combat-focused: several settlements need to decide which version and uncertainty level is good enough for a major regional plan.

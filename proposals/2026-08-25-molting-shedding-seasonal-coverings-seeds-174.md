# Pass 174 Proposals — Molting, Shedding & Seasonal Coverings

Status: NON-CANON candidates. Research-derived concepts only.

These proposals extend the proposed molt/shedding layer. They do not establish species biology, PTU mechanics, institutions or Ouros locations.

## Thirty worldbuilding candidates

1. The Rough-Looking Spring. A familiar wild Pokémon appears patchy each spring. New observers report illness; long-term records show the same appearance during normal coat replacement. Care remains available if other signs emerge.

2. The Shed-Skin Waterfall. Researchers repeatedly find shed skins near a cascade. Dratini-like lore inspires the question, but the source population remains an original Ouros decision.

3. Three Skins, One Individual? Field Signs, old photographs and later telemetry disagree about whether several shed skins came from one persistent Pokémon or multiple animals.

4. The Molt Site Became a Beach. A quiet seasonal haul-out slowly becomes a popular public recreation site. Conservation must manage timing and access without treating visitors as villains.

5. The Beach Became Quiet Again. A new boardwalk concentrates people away from the most sensitive section. The ecological success is uneventful and produces fewer emergencies.

6. The Coat Changed Early. One observed individual begins its seasonal replacement earlier than the historical population window. The event becomes a phenology question rather than immediate evidence of climate change.

7. The Late Individual. Most of a population appears finished while one persistent Pokémon is still in active molt. The system preserves individual variation instead of correcting it to the average.

8. The Missing Molt Count. A monitoring year has poor coverage because storms prevented surveys. The archive records a data gap instead of a population crash.

9. The Famous Feather. A single shed feather becomes locally famous because it was found during an old expedition. Museums later discover that its species label was revised twice.

10. The Feather Was Never Collected. A photograph of a shed feather becomes historically important even though the physical trace was left in place.

11. The Unauthorized Collection. A visitor picks up shed material from a protected site. The issue is custody/access and research provenance, not Pokémon theft.

12. The Material Was Legally Collected, Badly Labeled. A legitimate sample enters an archive with an incorrect location tag and creates a later reconstruction problem.

13. The Winter Coat in Summer. An individual seen with a thick covering during warm weather prompts multiple hypotheses: delayed cycle, local microclimate, old image timestamp, welfare concern, or misidentification.

14. The Photo Was from Last Year. A viral image appears to prove a surprising seasonal change but is correctly dated to a previous cycle after Visual Records review.

15. The Model Changed, Not the Pokémon. A Cobblemon texture update changes how a seasonal covering looks. Chronicle identity and observations must remain anchored to authoritative state, not renderer version.

16. The Form Question. Observers debate whether a visible seasonal change represents a mechanical Form. The answer remains unresolved until the authoritative species/rules source settles it.

17. The Festival Missed the Molt. A local celebration was historically timed around a visible seasonal change. Over several years the biological peak shifts while the civic date stays fixed.

18. The Festival Moved with the Animals. Another community chooses the opposite policy and moves its observance based on field monitoring rather than a calendar date.

19. The Rail Closure Nobody Resents. A short recurring operational hold protects a concentration site. Years of predictable scheduling turn a former conflict into routine infrastructure planning.

20. The Old Tunnel Became a Shed Site. An abandoned industrial structure gains seasonal ecological value. Mining/Architecture retain physical history while this layer records episode use.

21. The Growth Myth. Locals claim every shed means a young Pokémon is about to evolve. Longitudinal evidence shows shedding and Evolution are not reliably linked.

22. The Healthy Mess. A research station learns to distinguish a normal messy transition from signs that require Care review, reducing unnecessary handling.

23. The Actual Welfare Case. A later individual looks similar to normal molt but other evidence shows a genuine problem. The earlier lesson does not become a blanket dismissal.

24. The Museum Series. Authorized shed material from one known persistent Pokémon spans twenty years, making a rare longitudinal collection without requiring invasive sampling.

25. The Former Partner's Skin. Years after release, a shed trace could belong to a former partner. Identity remains a hypothesis until corroboration reaches the appropriate threshold.

26. The Population Changed Sites. Counts fall at the traditional molt location while another site increases. The first interpretation is redistribution, not automatic decline.

27. The New Site Is Inconvenient. A population begins using a ferry terminal edge during a short cycle window, creating a recurring operational coordination problem.

28. The Shorter Window. Monitoring suggests the visible transition period has compressed, but survey frequency also changed. Metrology/Community Science review comparability first.

29. The First Good Baseline. One completely ordinary season becomes extremely valuable ten years later because method, coverage and timing were unusually well documented.

30. Nothing Happened at Molt Point. The cycle proceeds normally, surveys complete, nobody interferes, and the event enters Chronicle only as a stable baseline.

## Longer arc — Five Molt Seasons at Cedar Reach

Year 1 establishes a reliable survey window and a normal-looking seasonal aggregation.

Year 2 produces lower counts during poor weather and reveals how much interpretation depends on survey effort.

Year 3 a boardwalk opens. Visitors increase, but disturbance declines because access is better directed.

Year 4 the apparent peak shifts earlier. Researchers record the change but avoid attributing a cause prematurely.

Year 5 counts at Cedar Reach fall while a second site rises. The institution revises its monitoring network rather than announcing a population collapse.

Possible long-term consequence: a once-temporary seasonal closure becomes a routine transport/public-space policy requiring little player intervention.

## Longer arc — One Skin, Four Interpretations

A large shed skin is photographed beside a remote stream.

Initial local interpretation identifies a famous persistent Pokémon.

A later taxonomic review questions the species identification.

A historical photograph shows the same pattern in a different individual.

Years later, a scientific technique may narrow the source while still leaving exact individual identity unresolved.

The physical evidence never changes. The interpretation history does.

## Longer arc — The Seasonal Form Debate

A regional Pokémon population displays pronounced recurring appearance changes.

Researchers, Trainers and public guides use inconsistent terminology: coat, seasonal form, local variety, ordinary molt.

The project preserves observations first.

Taxonomy and authoritative mechanics later decide whether any mechanical Form distinction exists.

If the final answer is “only appearance,” old records remain historically useful rather than being erased as mistakes.

## Encounter contract — Molt-Site Access Disturbance

Narrative premise: a short seasonal concentration coincides with a sudden access problem at a public/research site.

FULL implementation requires dynamic wildlife withdrawal, civilian/researcher evacuation, protected routes and objective-aware AI.

Required capability status:

- targeting/footprints/range/LoS: VERIFIED
- base movement legality: VERIFIED
- complete movement incl. push/pull/knockback/interception/forced movement: BLOCKING
- core calculations: VERIFIED
- action economy/initiative: VERIFIED
- full turn/round lifecycle: PARTIAL
- full stateful damage pipeline: PARTIAL
- status lifecycle: PARTIAL when invoked
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions: BLOCKING when the environment has tactical consequences
- move-specific behavior: PARTIAL
- abilities: PARTIAL
- items: PARTIAL
- Trainer Features/perks: PARTIAL
- AI legal-action infrastructure: VERIFIED
- AI tactical policy: BLOCKING
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback: BLOCKING

REDUCED implementation: close the site, evacuate noncombatants and resolve wildlife withdrawal through world state. If a separate conflict remains, use a static AutoPTU arena. The seasonal episode continues afterward.

## Encounter contract — Seasonal Crossing Window

Narrative premise: a road or rail route overlaps a short recurring period of concentrated movement/site use during coat replacement.

FULL implementation needs moving non-hostile actors, crossing objectives, withdrawal behavior and adapter playback. Environmental family is required only when weather/ground state has validated tactical effects.

REDUCED implementation stops traffic first, resolves the crossing outside battle and only then opens a static confrontation if one exists.

A battle result never decides whether the population completed the seasonal episode.

## Investigation contract — Shed-Skin Identity Survey

Preferred form: no battle.

Field Signs records location and trace. Visual Records preserves photographs. Science handles sampling. Taxonomy handles species determination. Pokémon Agency handles identity of any persistent known individual.

Possible outcomes include `ONE_KNOWN_INDIVIDUAL_PROBABLE`, `MULTIPLE_INDIVIDUALS_POSSIBLE`, `SPECIES_ONLY`, `UNRESOLVED`.

A combat victory cannot improve identity confidence.

## Canon firewall

None of the sites, institutions, species behaviors, seasonal timings, uses of shed material or historical events above are canon.

Any future adoption must identify the canonical species/population, location, authority and PTU/Caelo rule references separately.

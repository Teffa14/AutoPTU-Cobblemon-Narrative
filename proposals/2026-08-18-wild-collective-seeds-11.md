# Ouros Wild Collective Seeds — Pass 11

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros candidates derived from structural research. Species, places, institutions and mechanics remain placeholders until canon and PTU/AutoPTU review.

## Seed FA — The Pack Nobody Has Counted

Residents describe a nearby pack as enormous because they repeatedly see different members along several trails.

A field survey suggests the same individuals may be circulating through a wide home range, causing people to double-count them.

Players can:
- establish observation points;
- compare markings or persistent individuals;
- track route use;
- interview residents;
- determine whether there is one group, several overlapping groups or a temporary aggregation.

Design purpose: group size is knowledge, not an omniscient number.

## Seed FB — The Leader Everyone Chose for Them

A settlement is convinced that one conspicuous wild Pokémon is the leader of a troublesome group because it is larger, older or more visible.

Longer observation shows that coordination may come from another individual, several members, or no stable leader at all.

Removing or capturing the presumed leader may therefore change nothing.

The scenario rewards observing structure before treating a hierarchy assumption as fact.

## Seed FC — The Narrow Crossing

A seasonal movement route passes through the only easy crossing between two settlements for several days each year.

Current options include:
- temporary route closure;
- alternate transport;
- observation from a safe distance;
- timed crossing windows;
- infrastructure changes;
- attempting to force the group away.

A solution affects future travel, public memory and ecological behavior. The collective itself is not automatically hostile.

## Seed FD — The School That Exists Only Under Pressure

Travelers report a huge aquatic group appearing near a harbor whenever storms or another threat affect the coast.

During calm periods, no comparable group can be found.

Researchers initially disagree over whether it is:
- a permanent hidden population;
- several populations converging;
- a defensive aggregation;
- a response to a changing food source.

The answer comes from repeated state-aware observation rather than one encounter.

## Seed FE — The Colony Above the Market

A large group of wild Pokémon has used roofs, beams or another urban structure as a recurring roost for years.

Most residents tolerate it. A growing market changes lighting, waste, noise and foot traffic enough to alter the group's behavior.

Possible tensions:
- merchants want deterrence;
- residents value the seasonal sight;
- cleanup workers need practical changes;
- researchers worry the roost may be abandoned;
- another species begins exploiting food waste.

The issue can be solved through infrastructure and behavior management without requiring combat.

## Seed FF — The Fragment After the Storm

A severe weather event splits a previously observed collective into several smaller groups occupying unfamiliar areas.

Players encounter consequences separately:
- one subgroup reaches a settlement edge;
- another uses an abandoned dungeon entrance;
- a persistent individual is missing;
- a known feeding area remains empty.

Later research can determine whether the collective reforms, permanently splits or relocates.

## Seed FG — The Empty Core Site

A well-documented den, nest or roost is suddenly unused.

Nothing obvious has replaced the group.

Potential causes include:
- resource change;
- predator/competitor pressure;
- human disturbance;
- seasonal movement;
- disease only if deliberately authored;
- a safer site becoming available;
- temporary absence misunderstood as abandonment.

The absence itself becomes a research and route-state hook.

## Seed FH — Two Groups, One Water Source

Two persistent collectives use the same scarce water source at different times.

A drought compresses their schedules until their visits overlap.

Players may observe escalating warning behavior and competition before any battle occurs.

Potential interventions:
- restore another water source;
- reduce an artificial blockage;
- protect access windows;
- monitor without interfering;
- assist a settlement whose own water use contributes to pressure.

The system should not invent a permanent interspecies war from one clash.

## Seed FI — The Foraging Party

A settlement believes the six wild Pokémon repeatedly seen near farms are the entire nearby group.

Researchers discover that these are a recurring foraging subset returning to a much larger collective outside town.

Driving away the visible members alone may:
- change foraging time;
- cause a different subgroup to appear;
- redirect pressure elsewhere;
- have no durable effect.

The problem becomes one of resource access and group behavior rather than a six-enemy combat loop.

## Seed FJ — The Group That Learned the Schedule

A wild collective begins appearing near a transport stop shortly after predictable arrivals.

The group is not necessarily intelligent in a human sense. It may be responding to:
- dropped food;
- warm machinery;
- shelter;
- water runoff;
- insects or prey attracted by lights;
- reduced disturbance between services.

Changing the service schedule or waste handling creates a measurable behavioral response.

## Seed FK — The Warning Chain

Observers notice that one part of a dispersed group reacts to danger before distant members also change behavior.

The system records a communication hypothesis, not telepathy.

Players can investigate:
- sound;
- visual signals;
- movement of intermediary members;
- environmental cues shared across the area;
- coincidence.

Any species-specific signal or mechanical effect requires source validation.

## Seed FL — The Mixed Roost

Two species repeatedly occupy the same sheltered site overnight.

Nobody knows whether the arrangement is:
- simple shared refuge;
- mutual warning;
- tolerance caused by abundant space;
- one species benefiting more than the other;
- temporary seasonal overlap.

A construction project threatens the site, turning the unresolved ecological relationship into a practical planning question.

## Seed FM — The Territory That Moved

A group long associated with one valley begins appearing progressively farther toward a neighboring route.

The old range has changed because of a concrete pressure such as:
- construction;
- depleted food;
- restoration elsewhere;
- another group expanding;
- repeated player traffic;
- a changed dungeon hazard.

The world shows the movement over time rather than spawning a new problem at the destination without cause.

## Seed FN — The Capture With a Consequence Nobody Predicted

A player captures a persistent individual believed to have an important group role.

No automatic punishment occurs.

Follow-up observation determines what changed:
- another member takes over the observed role;
- the group fragments;
- activity becomes less coordinated;
- the group relocates;
- nothing changes and the original theory was wrong.

The consequence becomes ecological discovery rather than a moral script against capture.

## Seed FO — The Capture That Changed Nothing

Residents blame one highly visible Pokémon for repeated group activity near town and demand that it be removed.

After the individual leaves, the same collective pattern continues.

The event reveals that the problem was tied to habitat/resources rather than one creature.

This seed discourages the recurring procedural mistake of converting every systemic problem into a boss target.

## Seed FP — The Abandoned Road Nursery

A road closed for years has become a recurring nesting or shelter site for a wild collective.

Infrastructure planners now want the route restored.

Possible responses:
- seasonal reopening;
- route realignment;
- another access connection;
- habitat replacement;
- delayed construction;
- accepting that the old road now serves another function.

The collective has no legal ownership by default; the conflict comes from current ecological use and human needs.

## Seed FQ — The Scavengers After the Festival

A temporary public event produces unusual food waste and draws a large aggregation of normally dispersed wild Pokémon.

Residents interpret the sudden abundance as a population explosion.

Cleanup and observation reveal that the group may disperse once the artificial food source disappears.

Possible consequences:
- improved event cleanup policy;
- temporary observation opportunity;
- another predator following the abundance;
- one subgroup becoming conditioned to return.

## Seed FR — The Group in the Old Machine Hall

A dungeon or industrial ruin cleared months earlier now houses a wild collective using the altered structure as shelter.

A new expedition wants access to machinery deeper inside.

Players can:
- find a route avoiding the core site;
- observe occupancy times;
- restore another shelter;
- accept a more dangerous approach;
- force the group out and deal with relocation consequences.

Dungeon state memory and ecology intersect directly.

## Seed FS — The Sentry Assumption

Players repeatedly see one wild Pokémon positioned away from the rest of a group and assume it is guarding them.

Possible explanations remain open:
- actual warning role;
- preferred feeding point;
- individual temperament;
- injury limiting movement;
- coincidence;
- watching another species rather than protecting its own group.

The system should store `sentry behavior suspected` until observations justify a stronger claim.

## Seed FT — The Seasonal Merge

Two small collectives that remain separate for most of the year gather at one site during a short seasonal window.

The gathering creates:
- rare observation opportunities;
- route congestion;
- public interest;
- temporary changes in predator/prey distribution;
- faction or tourism pressure.

After the window, the groups separate again. The merge does not erase their original identities.

## Seed FU — The Group That Avoids One Trainer

After several encounters, a persistent collective begins avoiding a particular player's usual approach route.

The system records the observable pattern without deciding that the group hates or fears that person as an internal emotional fact.

Possible causes:
- repeated battles;
- captures;
- loud movement;
- feeding behavior;
- associated Pokémon scent/noise;
- unrelated timing coincidence.

A field report can test whether the effect persists when conditions change.

## Seed FV — The Group That Waits for Food

Residents regularly feed a nearby wild collective, believing the practice keeps it friendly.

Over time the group increasingly waits near homes and becomes disruptive when food is unavailable.

Players may help residents change the practice gradually, secure waste, identify natural food sources or decide that the coexistence is acceptable with modifications.

This is a conditioning/ecology story, not a morality judgment about feeding wildlife.

## Seed FW — The Census Expedition

A multiplayer research operation attempts to estimate several collective states across one region without requiring everyone to perform the same activity.

Teams can:
- observe core sites;
- track route crossings;
- interview residents;
- verify reports from transport workers;
- identify persistent individuals;
- compare night/day behavior.

The result is a shared field report with uncertainty bands rather than a perfect spawn count.

## Seed FX — The Rival Collective Takes the Empty Space

A group relocates after a player-linked event. Another collective begins using the vacated resource or shelter.

Returning players discover that restoring the first group's old conditions will now affect the second group.

This creates genuine ecological succession at a small narrative scale without requiring a full ecosystem simulator.

## Seed FY — The Outbreak That Was a Stopover

A settlement reports hundreds of the same species appearing over several days and fears a permanent change.

Evidence instead suggests a temporary migration stop or refuge event.

The real challenge is managing short-term pressure:
- route access;
- crop/storage protection;
- visitor curiosity;
- observation safety;
- public information.

When the group leaves naturally, the Chronicle records the event rather than leaving a permanent inflated encounter table.

## Seed FZ — The Old One Everyone Knows

One identifiable wild Pokémon has been observed with the same collective for years and is recognized by local residents.

Nobody owns it.

Its disappearance creates concern because it is a useful marker for the group's continuity, not because it is automatically the leader or a sacred Pokémon.

Players can search, compare old observations and determine whether the individual moved, was captured, was injured, or simply stopped appearing where people usually watch.

## Cross-system hooks

These seeds extend existing Ouros layers rather than replacing them:
- Observation owns behavioral evidence and field reports.
- World Agency owns World Pulse and persistent individual Pokémon.
- Mission Grammar converts ecological problems into playable action blocks.
- Travel consumes validated route/collective intersections.
- Settlement state handles human responses to neighboring groups.
- Dungeon Memory records occupation and relocation.
- Cases can investigate disappearance, damage or disputed claims without treating wild behavior as criminality.
- Public Memory can preserve famous migrations, outbreaks and long-observed individuals.
- Myth/Archaeology can connect stewardship to groups while keeping cultural claims separate from ecology.

## New implementation and canon questions

- Which Pokémon species need explicit authored collective profiles because their group organization is distinctive?
- Can the Cobblemon bridge tag naturally spawned entities with a collective ID without interfering with ordinary despawn behavior?
- Which persistent individuals deserve stable identity within otherwise aggregate groups?
- Which PTU/Caelo rules for Pack Mon, swarms, intimidation, escape and wild disposition should carry into Ouros?
- How should AutoPTU return retreat/dispersal outcomes to the narrative layer?
- How should capture of one persistent member update a collective while preserving player freedom to catch Pokémon?
- How should seasonal aggregation state progress when nobody has the relevant Minecraft region loaded?
- How should the UI communicate uncertain population/group estimates without exposing hidden exact counts?
- Should group disturbance history decay over time, and if so under what authored rules?
- How can ecological state avoid becoming exploitable rare-spawn farming metadata?

# Ouros Identity, Names, Aliases & Record-Linkage Seeds — Pass 122

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Every entry is a candidate for later review. None establishes Ouros law, citizenship, compulsory registration, family structure, Trainer licensing or hidden identity as canon.

## 30 original candidates

### 1. Two Trainers, One Name
A tournament office finds two active participants with the same public name. Their battle histories have started to merge in public reports even though the internal records remain separate. The story is about correction, communication and public memory rather than uncovering a fraud.

### 2. The Old League Card
A collector owns a League-style card from years earlier showing a Gym Trainer under a former public name and role. The card is authentic, the current profile is also authentic, and the interesting question is how the institution links both periods without rewriting either.

### 3. The Wrong Winner in the Newspaper
An old newspaper credited a championship result to the wrong person because two competitors shared a surname and initial. Decades later, a museum exhibition reproduces the error from the newspaper. Correcting the exhibit creates a new historical record; it does not erase the original publication.

### 4. Three Spellings at the Ferry Office
A ferry manifest, academy register and clinic referral all spell the same visiting researcher's name differently. All three records can be legitimate products of different regional conventions. Players help reconcile the records before a time-sensitive transfer without imposing one universal spelling on every institution.

### 5. The Researcher With Two Bylines
A scientist publishes field work under one short professional name while employment and grant records use another form. A future literature review mistakenly treats the output as two separate researchers. The correction improves the research graph without changing authorship history.

### 6. The Duplicate Employee
A workplace migration to a new digital system creates a second staff profile after an older record fails to import. One profile has the training history; the other has the current schedule. The problem looks suspicious until ordinary reconciliation explains it.

### 7. The Challenger Who Changed Their Public Name
A recurring rival returns under a different public battle name. Some fans think a new challenger has appeared; some archival records still use the old name. The world can preserve both fan interpretations until public records catch up.

### 8. Same Pokémon, New Nickname
A Pokémon transferred between two long-term caretakers has accumulated several nicknames over the years. A museum photograph uses the oldest one, while current records use the newest. The persistent `pokemon_entity_id` links the history without declaring any nickname more “real” than the others.

### 9. Same Nickname, Different Pokémon
Two unrelated Growlithe in the same district are both called Ember. A lost-Pokémon notice links sightings of both animals into one false route. Players can disentangle the observations without treating the duplicate nickname as anyone's mistake.

### 10. Former Partner at the Orchard
A released Pokémon appears near an orchard years later. The species and nickname match an old partner, but those facts alone are insufficient. Photograph comparison, distinctive old records and observed behavior can strengthen or weaken the linkage while agency remains separate.

### 11. The Archive Under a Former Name
A crucial expedition notebook cannot be found because it was catalogued under a former byline. The actual challenge is archival search and name authority, not a hidden room or stolen book.

### 12. The Stage Name Became More Famous
A performer becomes regionally known by a stage name. Years later, most visitors believe the stage name is the person's ordinary name, while close associates use another one. Neither group is “wrong”; they know different public contexts.

### 13. The Retired Title
Everyone still calls a retired harbor captain “Captain.” A new official with the same title arrives. The setting can preserve the historical honorific without confusing who currently holds authority.

### 14. The Badge Office Uses Yesterday's Name
A credential update propagates to most systems but not to an old badge-printing terminal. The printed card looks inconsistent with the central record. The task is reconciliation, not proving identity through whichever card looks newer.

### 15. The Public Profile With No Surname
A well-known Trainer uses a single public name. Some institutions legitimately need a more precise internal identifier; others do not. Ouros does not invent a family name merely to make databases look conventional.

### 16. The Mapmaker in Two Scripts
A historical cartographer appears under two scripts in regional archives. An earlier researcher treated them as separate people and split their maps into two collections. A modern project can restore the linkage while preserving both name forms.

### 17. The Wrong Portrait
Two public figures shared a name decades ago, and an archive attached one person's photograph to the other's biography. Correcting the image produces a modest historical investigation with real consequences for exhibitions and family archives, without requiring conspiracy.

### 18. The Club Founder Who Used a Nickname
A club's earliest records mention only a nickname. Later members want to identify the founder for an anniversary display. The correct outcome may remain “probable but unconfirmed” if evidence never becomes strong enough.

### 19. The Leaderboard Collision
A public leaderboard truncates long display names and makes two Trainers appear identical. The internal IDs remain distinct. Fans build a rivalry around a technical presentation bug before the operator fixes it.

### 20. The Courier With the Same Initials
A parcel is routed to the wrong branch because two staff members share initials. The package history remains intact, and the error becomes a process problem for Postal Logistics rather than an identity crime story.

### 21. The Wrong Patient Record
A clinic discovers that two similarly named patients have one appointment record linked incorrectly. Medical content remains private; the party only sees the operational consequences appropriate to their role. Identity correction must not expose either case unnecessarily.

### 22. The Gym Leader's Old Challenge Name
A Gym Leader used a different challenge-circuit name early in their career. An aspiring Trainer studying historical battles thinks the old challenger was a separate person and develops two competing strategies. The discovery becomes a lesson about evidence and historical context.

### 23. The Alias Everyone Already Knows
A local shopkeeper uses an alternate name that is technically an alias but is public knowledge and entirely ordinary. The setting resists treating every alias as a secret identity or criminal flag.

### 24. The Person Who Shares a Name With a Famous Rival
An ordinary worker has the same name as a notorious former competitor. New visitors repeatedly make assumptions. The NPC's story can explore unwanted public association without giving them any hidden connection to the celebrity.

### 25. The Account Was Real, the Attribution Was Wrong
A public post came from a real institutional account, but several staff had access during the relevant shift. The account log is evidence of account use, not proof of the physical author. Cases, Digital Systems and Identity must cooperate.

### 26. The Pokémon Before Evolution
An old photograph identifies a Pokémon by its pre-evolution species and contains no nickname. Years later, researchers suspect it is the same evolved individual now known locally. The link remains a hypothesis until stronger individual evidence appears.

### 27. The Nickname That Entered Official Use
An informal Pokémon nickname becomes so common that a research station starts using it in notes. Later teams must distinguish the nickname from taxonomy and from individual identity. The same label can be useful without becoming a species name.

### 28. The Two Professors
Two unrelated researchers have the same surname and title. Citations, interviews and public comments are repeatedly attributed to the wrong one. Their institutions build better authority records after several small errors.

### 29. The Identity Reconciliation Day
A regional archive schedules a low-drama project to reconcile decades of duplicate person records after several institutions adopt a shared search service. Players can contribute research, scanning, interviews or field verification. The outcome is better world memory rather than loot.

### 30. The Name Nobody Can Recover
A historically important worker appears in dozens of records only through a role label and initials. The archive may never identify the person with confidence. Ouros can preserve meaningful anonymous contribution rather than inventing a definitive identity for narrative neatness.

## Longer arc A — Five Names, One Career

Year 1: a young competitor enters local events under an informal battle name.

Year 2: a League institution issues a public profile under another preferred form.

Year 3: research or sponsorship records begin using a professional byline.

Year 4: public reporting starts to split these records into multiple people.

Year 5: an archive project links the career correctly while preserving every historical label and publication.

Possible long-term consequences:
- fans remember different phases under different names;
- old merchandise remains historically correct;
- leaderboards gain better cross-references;
- a rival who knew only one phase genuinely fails to recognize the connection at first;
- the person retains control over which private name forms become public.

No reveal needs to be dramatic. The arc works best as persistent world memory becoming more accurate over time.

## Longer arc B — The Registry That Grew Sideways

Several institutions independently create identifiers for battle participation, clinic records, employment, research and public events.

For years the system works because the population is small. Growth creates duplicate records, stale names, bad cross-links and inconsistent transliterations. A crisis finally exposes the problem when responders cannot quickly determine whether several records refer to one person or multiple people.

The region gradually builds an identity cross-reference service without creating a single universal civil database. Each institution keeps its own authority. The shared layer stores only bounded linkage and search information.

This arc connects Digital Systems, Credentials, Archives, Emergency Services, Privacy and Public Memory.

## Longer arc C — The Pokémon With Three Histories

A single Pokémon appears in three apparently separate historical contexts:
- an early photograph before evolution;
- a period with an institutional caretaker and a nickname;
- later sightings after release under another local nickname.

Players can spend years accumulating evidence. The final outcome may be CONFIRMED, PROBABLE or still UNRESOLVED. Even if confirmed, the Pokémon's current agency remains independent from every former caretaker.

This arc connects Photography, Pokémon Agency, Evolution, Archives, Field Signs and Public Memory.

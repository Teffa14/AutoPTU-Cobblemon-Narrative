# Wildfire, Fire Ecology & Landscape Recovery Seeds — Pass 64

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research patterns. No external plot, character, institution or dialogue is copied.

Date: 2026-08-20

These candidates use `design/wildfire-fire-ecology-landscape-recovery-layer.md`.

## 1. The Unburned Pocket

A severe regional fire leaves one small valley largely untouched. Wildlife begins concentrating there, and responders debate whether to close the only public trail temporarily.

Useful systems: conservation, wild collectives, tourism, access policy, research.

The intact patch is valuable because it remained intact, not because it grants a mechanical buff.

## 2. Lightning on the Western Ridge

Several witnesses report lightning shortly before smoke appears. A damaged relay on the same ridge creates a competing infrastructure hypothesis.

Useful systems: meteorology, evidence, infrastructure, cases.

## 3. The Fire-Type Scapegoat

Residents blame a known wild Fire-type Pokémon for a recent ignition because it was photographed nearby. Archived weather data and older scorch evidence complicate the story.

The Pokémon’s presence is real. Causation remains unresolved.

## 4. First Rain After the Fire

Weeks after containment, the first intense rain sends ash and sediment into a downstream creek. A water-treatment facility reports operational stress.

Useful systems: freshwater, sanitation, crisis, science, infrastructure.

## 5. The Road Through the Scar

A major transport route reopens before surrounding habitat recovers. Drivers begin reporting displaced Pokémon using the road edge as a corridor.

Useful systems: travel, wild collectives, conservation, public works.

## 6. Three Burn Severities

A research team maps three adjacent patches that burned at very different intensities. Each develops a different pattern of regrowth and wildlife use over the next year.

No universal “post-fire spawn table” is created.

## 7. The False Containment Rumor

A local message says the fire is contained. The official operations log says one sector remains active. Both messages are old by the time players receive them.

Useful systems: communications, media, crisis, public belief.

## 8. Orchard Smoke Without Flame

A farming district experiences heavy smoke from a distant fire while remaining outside the active perimeter. Harvest schedules, visitors and field work change even though no structure burns.

Useful systems: agriculture, meteorology, workplaces, health surveillance.

## 9. The Refuge Waterhole

Multiple normally separate wild collectives appear around one surviving waterhole after a fire. Their temporary co-location must not be interpreted as a new permanent group.

Useful systems: wild collectives, interspecies ecology, freshwater.

## 10. The Burned Survey Station

A remote ecological station survives structurally but loses instruments, records and surrounding reference plots.

Useful systems: science, digital systems, archives, architecture.

## 11. The Nest Boxes Arrive Too Early

A recovery organization installs artificial habitat immediately after a fire. Later surveys show that some target species are using different parts of the scar than expected.

The intervention is reviewed rather than retconned.

## 12. Old Fire Line, New Forest

A decades-old suppression line is still visible in vegetation patterns and settlement memory. A new fire approaches from a different direction.

Useful systems: public memory, architecture, cartography, crisis.

## 13. The Empty Creek

After a fire, residents notice fewer aquatic Pokémon downstream. Water tests, habitat surveys and older population records are needed before anyone attributes the change to ash runoff.

## 14. Burn Scar Tourism

A dramatic post-fire landscape becomes a popular photography destination before local recovery teams are ready for crowds.

Useful systems: tourism, photography, conservation, transport.

## 15. The Regrowth That Changed the View

Rapid young vegetation blocks a historic viewpoint and alters trail visibility. Residents disagree over whether the change is recovery, loss or simply a different landscape stage.

Useful systems: public memory, tourism, architecture, ecology.

## 16. Volunteer Camp Handoff

An emergency response camp becomes a long-term recovery base. Temporary staff leave, local organizations inherit equipment and several undocumented responsibilities remain.

Useful systems: workplaces, staffing, material provenance, institutions.

## 17. The Second Fire Was Smaller

A later low-intensity fire affects part of an earlier scar. Public fear treats it as a repeat catastrophe, while ecological evidence suggests a different event class.

Useful systems: media, science, public memory, fire regime.

## 18. The Burned Archive Trail

A fire damages trail markers but leaves an old stone route physically passable. Modern maps and historical maps disagree about which path should reopen first.

Useful systems: cartography, archives, travel, governance.

## 19. The Firebreak Garden

A settlement converts a long-maintained cleared strip into a community garden during quiet years. A new fire-management review forces a discussion about the land’s current and former purposes.

Useful systems: food, public works, memory, stewardship.

## 20. The Smoke-Sensitive Observatory

A regional observatory loses several important observing nights because of distant wildfire smoke. The fire never threatens the facility itself.

Useful systems: astronomy, meteorology, communications, science.

## 21. Charred Equipment Without a Case

Damaged field equipment is found inside a recent scar. Its owner is unknown and there is no evidence it caused the fire.

Useful systems: provenance, cases, research, public belief.

## 22. The Returning Collective

A known wild collective leaves during the fire. Months later only part of the group is observed returning to its old range.

The system records observed individuals/subgroups without deciding what happened to the rest.

## 23. Fire at the Old Quarry

Vegetation that colonized an abandoned quarry burns differently from the surrounding terrain. The event exposes old industrial remains and new geological surfaces.

Useful systems: geology, archaeology, pollution, adaptive reuse.

## 24. The Water Tank Debate

A remote settlement proposes a larger emergency water tank after a fire. Farmers, residents, conservation staff and transport operators all support the project for different reasons.

Useful systems: governance, freshwater, finance, crisis preparedness.

## 25. The Patch That Did Better Alone

One area receives intensive restoration. Another comparable patch is left to recover naturally. Years later, the results are mixed and force a management review.

Useful systems: conservation, science, long-term monitoring.

## 26. The Fire Season That Never Came

A fire-adapted landscape goes through an unusually long period without expected low-intensity disturbance. Researchers document changes before anyone proposes intervention.

No planned burn is generated automatically.

## 27. Post-Fire Market Shift

A town’s market temporarily becomes a supply and repair hub for recovery crews. Some businesses return to normal later; others permanently change role.

Useful systems: economy, workplaces, demography, adaptive reuse.

## 28. The Memorial Before the Cause

A community plans an anniversary ceremony while the ignition investigation remains unresolved. The event focuses on losses and recovery rather than assigning blame.

Useful systems: memorials, public memory, cases, events.

## 29. Ash in the Reservoir

A reservoir remains physically full after a fire, but monitoring finds a change in incoming sediment and treatment demand.

Useful systems: freshwater, technology, sanitation, science.

## 30. The Reopened Forest

Years after a major fire, one sector is ready for ordinary use while another remains under monitoring. Reopening becomes patch-specific rather than a single regional switch.

Useful systems: conservation, tourism, route access, public information.

# Longer arc A — The Black Ridge Years

A ridge fire becomes a five-year regional story.

Year 0:

- uncertain ignition;
- emergency response;
- route closures;
- wildlife displacement;
- smoke across nearby settlements.

Year 1:

- severity mapping;
- unburned refugia become conservation priorities;
- first-rain runoff creates downstream monitoring;
- recovery projects begin.

Years 2–3:

- different burn patches develop different habitats;
- tourism returns unevenly;
- roads and facilities reopen in stages;
- public explanations of the cause continue to evolve.

Years 4–5:

- a new management plan is reviewed against actual recovery data;
- some areas resemble the old landscape;
- others become permanently different;
- the fire remains part of public memory without remaining the only story the region tells.

# Longer arc B — Fire Above, Water Below

A mountain wildfire ends before the main player arc begins.

The later campaign follows its downstream consequences:

- first-rain sediment;
- treatment-plant changes;
- altered stream habitat;
- damaged monitoring equipment;
- competing explanations for wildlife changes;
- erosion-control work;
- a later storm that tests whether recovery measures worked.

The original fire can remain causally important without reappearing as an active hazard every session.

# Longer arc C — The Mosaic Project

A large stewardship area contains patches with different historical fire patterns.

Researchers, local residents, land managers and conservation groups build a long-term picture of which habitats respond to different disturbance histories.

The arc can include:

- historical map recovery;
- field surveys;
- route closures and reopenings;
- planned management proposals;
- public outreach;
- null results;
- disagreements over interpretation;
- post-project monitoring.

If any authored fire stewardship tradition later exists in Ouros, it must be original and reviewed. Real living Indigenous cultural-burning practices are not to be copied into this arc.

# Grasslands, Grazing & Rangeland Ecology Seeds — Pass 79

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Original Ouros candidates derived from research structures. No PTU mechanic is established here.

Date: 2026-08-21

## 1. The Missing Watering Route

A Bouffalant collective that used the same seasonal route for years suddenly stops appearing at one water point. The water source still functions. Survey teams must compare herd-route history, recent fencing, forage use, road work and disturbance before deciding what changed.

## 2. The Short Grass Argument

Residents insist a public meadow is being destroyed because one sector is cropped unusually short. Long-term records show that the patch has always been heavily used during this part of the season. The real question is whether current use differs from the historical pattern.

## 3. Wooloo at the Old Gate

A managed Wooloo herd keeps gathering around an obsolete gate even after a new route is built. The old gate may still be part of the herd's learned routine, or another feature nearby may be attracting them. No obedience or Loyalty conclusion is assumed.

## 4. Two Herds, One Spring

A managed herd and a wild collective begin using the same spring during drought. Neither group is automatically aggressive. The conflict comes from timing, access, pressure on the spring margin and human expectations.

## 5. The Burn Patch Return

A herd repeatedly moves into a recently burned grassland patch during regrowth. Researchers want to know whether the pattern persists over several seasons. The burn remains Wildfire state; grazing response is stored separately.

## 6. The Quiet Wallow

A shallow depression historically used by a large herd begins holding water longer after a wet year. New vegetation appears around it. A small landscape feature becomes relevant to Freshwater, Flora and wildlife observation without becoming a battle hazard.

## 7. The Wrong Leader

Survey notes identify the largest Bouffalant as herd leader. Later movement observations show another individual repeatedly initiates route changes. The archive must preserve both interpretations and why the assessment changed.

## 8. Fleece Week

A town's annual shearing period increases staffing, transport and workshop demand. The event can generate hospitality, labor and material-provenance stories without assigning automatic Wool yields or item bonuses.

## 9. The Broken Rotation

A ranch follows the same range-use rotation it used for years, but a new drought makes one resting pasture less recovered than expected. The problem is not villainy; an old management plan stopped fitting current conditions.

## 10. The Gate That Became a Trail

A fence gap repaired decades ago remains visible as a worn crossing used by wild Pokémon. A proposed construction project would erase it. Cartography, conservation and grassland records disagree on whether the route is still significant.

## 11. The Meadow With No Herd

A grassland expected to host a seasonal herd remains empty. Possible causes include forage distribution, disturbance, water access, changed migration, observer timing or a genuine population shift. Absence is evidence, not a conclusion.

## 12. The Overcrowded Shade Line

A new shade structure successfully improves managed-herd welfare but concentrates use enough to change soil and vegetation beneath it. The story becomes a design problem about distribution rather than removing the structure.

## 13. Gogoat Above the Tree Line

A mountain Gogoat herd begins using a higher meadow than local observers remember. Snowline, drought, forage, disturbance and social movement are all candidates. Mountable and leadership mechanics remain separate.

## 14. The Roadside Flock

A flock begins resting beside a newly quieter road after traffic is rerouted. The road closure solves one transport problem and creates an unexpected wildlife-use pattern that affects future reopening plans.

## 15. Three Pastures, Three Histories

Three fields look equally worn. One has a long history of concentrated grazing, one recently hosted a festival, and one has poor infiltration from older machinery. Similar appearance does not imply one cause.

## 16. The Herd After Release

A previously partnered Pokémon is later observed moving with a wild collective. Its old Trainer's history remains relevant, but no authority, ownership or leadership follows from that history.

## 17. The Mareep Signal Field

A population of Mareep and Flaaffy begins spending nights near an aerial corridor and producing visible light activity. Aviation complaints arrive before ecological surveys do. The problem can be solved by route timing, lighting, observation or other changes without making the Pokémon antagonists.

## 18. The Dry Trough Mystery

A trough is repeatedly found empty, and the first suspicion is excessive herd use. Records instead show an intermittent supply problem. Ecology and infrastructure must be investigated together.

## 19. The Rested Field That Did Not Recover

A heavily used pasture is rested for a full season but vegetation recovery remains weak. Soil, seed availability, drought history and previous disturbance become separate hypotheses.

## 20. The Festival Herd

A public festival temporarily changes noise, food availability and fencing near a grassland. A wild collective shifts its route for the week. The pattern should disappear, persist or transform based on later observations rather than a scripted outcome.

## 21. The Old Shepherd's Map

A retired handler's hand-drawn route map differs from the official range map. Both may be correct at different dates. The older route reveals water points and crossing habits that explain present-day wear patterns.

## 22. The New Fence Debate

A new boundary would reduce managed-herd escapes but cut through a long-used wild route. Players can investigate alternatives, timing changes, gates or route redesign. No faction needs to be malicious.

## 23. The Post-Fire Forage Boom

A regrowth patch draws multiple herbivore groups after a wildfire. Observers disagree about whether this is beneficial recovery or excessive pressure. The answer may differ by patch and year.

## 24. The Grassland Without Grass

A unit classified historically as grassland becomes increasingly shrub-dominated. Fire history, grazing pattern, drought and land-use changes all interact. The location keeps its identity while its vegetation state changes.

## 25. The Waterpoint Camera Gap

Camera-trap coverage around a major water point fails for several days during a migration window. Later herd-route inference must preserve the gap rather than filling it with invented movement.

## 26. The Ranch That Stopped Selling Wool

A ranch remains active, the herd remains healthy, but the household deliberately shifts away from wool production toward another livelihood. The herd's identity should not be reduced to its previous economic output.

## 27. The Public Meadow Pilot

A town tests seasonal managed grazing in a public meadow to alter vegetation structure. The pilot has explicit monitoring criteria and can succeed, fail or produce mixed outcomes. It never grants battle bonuses.

## 28. The Hidden Concentration Point

Most of a range looks lightly used, but one narrow crossing near water accumulates heavy disturbance. Coarse regional averages miss the hotspot until repeated observations reveal it.

## 29. The Herd Census Dispute

Two teams estimate a wild herd at very different sizes because one counted repeated subgroups moving through the same corridor. The case links Wild Collectives, Photography and Survey methodology.

## 30. The Prairie Archive

Decades of maps, photographs, burn records, vegetation surveys and herd observations reveal that the grassland has never been static. The archive itself becomes a source of quests because every new project must be compared against older states rather than an imagined pristine baseline.

# Long arc A — Five Seasons at Redgrass Range

Year 1 establishes herd routes, water points and vegetation mosaics.

Year 2 brings a dry season that concentrates use around one spring.

Year 3 a fire changes the distribution of fresh growth.

Year 4 a new road diverts traffic and alters one old crossing.

Year 5 shows that some patches recovered, some shifted vegetation and one new route became permanent.

The arc can produce research, ranch work, conservation, transport and public-works stories without requiring a central antagonist.

# Long arc B — The Wool Road

A mountain community depends partly on Wooloo/Mareep husbandry, local workshops and transport between several settlements. Over multiple years, evolution, changing demand, route disruptions, new materials, weather and worker succession change the system. The central question is how the community adapts while treating Pokémon as persistent individuals and groups rather than production machines.

# Long arc C — The Two Grasslands

Two neighboring grassland systems look similar but have different histories. One is shaped mainly by wild herbivores and fire. The other has long-term managed grazing, fences and artificial water points. A regional development plan initially treats them as interchangeable, and the campaign gradually reveals why their responses to drought, tourism and restoration differ.

# Guardrails

- Do not use loaded Cobblemon entity count as population truth.
- Do not infer overgrazing from short vegetation alone.
- Do not infer leadership from size, Level or spawn order.
- Do not turn managed herds into one combat unit.
- Do not convert Sap Sipper, Run Away, Pack Mon, Naturewalk or Mountable into generic herd ecology rules.
- Do not create automatic wool, milk, food or material yields.
- Do not apply Rough Terrain, Slow, Tripped, damage, cover or Accuracy changes from grazing state unless an exact PTU/Caelo rule and Java implementation support them.
- Do not treat a wild herd using private land as automatically hostile or owned.
- Preserve chronology when routes, fences, water points or management plans change.

# Ouros aridity, drought & desert ecology seeds — pass 73

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros material derived from high-level research patterns. No external location, character, dialogue or plot is copied.

## 30 candidate seeds

1. The Pool That Did Not Fill
A familiar rain-fed basin remains dry while two nearby basins briefly hold water. The first quest is to document whether the difference comes from rainfall, sediment, drainage change or simple timing.

2. Last Reliable Spring
A spring becomes the only dependable water point on a route late in a dry season. Wildlife, travelers and a maintenance crew all use it, and none automatically has priority.

3. Dust from Somewhere Else
A settlement suffers repeated dust plumes, but local fields are stable. The likely source is many kilometers away and the investigation must follow wind, surface condition and timing rather than blame the nearest construction site.

4. The Dry Channel Map
A cartographer marks a channel as abandoned because it has been dry for years. A short storm reactivates one reach and proves the channel still matters to route planning and ecology.

5. Cacnea Bloom Window
A local Cacnea population flowers for a very short period after a particular combination of conditions. Researchers want repeat observations; tourism actors want publicity; no rare-spawn guarantee exists.

6. The Wrong Drought
A regional bulletin labels an area dry, but one settlement's water shortage is actually caused by a failed pump. The drought is real; the local crisis has a different proximate cause.

7. Oasis Without Ownership
A small oasis is used by several wild collectives, caravan operators and a research team. The conflict concerns timing and disturbance, not possession of the Pokémon.

8. The Buried Trail Marker
Moving sand exposes an old route marker and hides a newer one. The map was not wrong when printed; the physical route changed.

9. Night Desert Census
Day surveys suggest a nearly empty desert. Night observations reveal substantial activity from species adapted to cooler or darker hours.

10. The Empty Trapinch Pits
A field of old funnel nests remains visible, but fresh activity has stopped. Possible explanations include migration, disturbance, prey changes or survey timing.

11. Dust on the Observatory
An astronomy station records unusually poor nights even when clouds are absent. The problem links light, dust deposition and regional source tracking.

12. Waterpoint Camera Gap
A camera trap at a drought refuge stops recording during the busiest week. Missing images are not evidence of absence.

13. The Shade Corridor
A transport route is still physically open but becomes unsafe at certain hours after shade structures are removed for repairs. Travel schedules change without inventing heat-damage mechanics.

14. Dry Season Ferry Failure
A ferry technically still exists, but water depth no longer supports operation. The route remains geographically connected while the service state changes.

15. The Sediment Plug
An ephemeral pool fills less than usual because sediment changed its capacity. Soil, hydrology and ecology all intersect before anyone can call it climate change.

16. Three Springs, Three Histories
Three nearby springs decline at the same time. One follows regional drought, one has a groundwater issue, and one is affected by infrastructure maintenance.

17. Dust Road Closure
A highway closes repeatedly for visibility. Traders demand a bypass; conservation staff worry that the new route would cross a refuge.

18. Sandile Under the Boardwalk
A settlement expands over sandy habitat and repeated observations suggest Sandile are using buried routes beneath new structures. Presence does not imply aggression.

19. The False Sandstorm Report
Players see blowing dust and a public post calls it a Sandstorm. The narrative layer records the visual event while battle Weather remains unset until mechanically validated.

20. Refuge Pressure Ledger
A long drought increases use of one water point by multiple species. The challenge is tracking changing pressure over months without simulating every visit.

21. After the First Rain
A dry valley changes quickly after rain: pools fill, plants respond and different Pokémon become observable. The system records a temporary phenology window instead of spawning a mandatory special encounter.

22. The Spring That Became a Seep
Residents insist a spring has vanished. Field work shows it still exists at lower output and now appears as a diffuse seep.

23. Dune over the Door
A ruin entrance becomes buried between expeditions. Archaeological site identity persists while access changes.

24. Dust on the Snow
A distant dust event deposits material on a mountain snowpack. The follow-up links desert source zones with Cryosphere and Freshwater research later in the season.

25. The Empty Water Tank
A public tank is empty during drought, but records show demand was normal. The investigation becomes an infrastructure/service case rather than an ecological mystery.

26. Desert Market Week
A seasonal market forms around a dependable route and water source. Attendance changes with transport reliability and drought pressure rather than a fixed calendar alone.

27. The Refuge Nobody Advertises
Researchers deliberately keep a small perennial water site out of tourist maps because visitor pressure could damage the site. Media and conservation layers must preserve the redaction.

28. The Old Dust Source
A former industrial yard stopped operating years ago but remains an exposed dust source. Liability, current land use and actual physical causation stay separate.

29. The Sand Colony Count
Hippopotas are observed in several clusters. The system must determine whether this is one moving collective, several groups or repeated sightings before changing population state.

30. The Dryland Atlas
A long-term project maps springs, pools, night activity, dunes, dust sources and changing routes across an arid region. Each edition remains historically valid for its date.

## Longer arc A — Seven Dry Years

Year 1 establishes normal variation through surveys of springs, pools and seasonal routes. Years 2–3 show a prolonged dry pattern, but consequences differ by subregion. A town with strong infrastructure remains functional while an isolated route becomes unreliable and one wildlife refuge receives growing pressure.

Year 4 brings a major dust season whose strongest impacts occur outside the driest zone. Year 5 includes an unusually wet pulse that temporarily restores some pools but does not erase accumulated infrastructure or ecological changes. Years 6–7 focus on which adaptations persist and which were temporary.

The arc can end without a villain and without declaring a permanent climate trend unless sufficient world data exists.

## Longer arc B — The Oasis Network

Several small water sites once treated as unrelated landmarks turn out to form a practical network for travelers and wildlife. Each site has different reliability, governance, ecology and history.

Players gradually map the network, document which routes depend on which refuges, resolve local conflicts, uncover one historical route buried by moving sand and decide what information should remain public.

The climax is institutional rather than apocalyptic: several settlements need a shared plan for route access and monitoring without converting the entire desert into one centrally controlled system.

## Longer arc C — Dust Across Regions

A distant dry basin begins producing dust events that affect a port, observatory, farming district and snowpack in different ways. Early reports blame separate local causes.

Over multiple seasons, meteorology, soil surveys, photography, health signals and route closures build a stronger source model. The source landscape itself has several contributing changes, so the resolution is a portfolio of interventions rather than defeating one actor.

Years later, old dust-event records become useful evidence for a new generation of researchers.

# Ouros Proposals — Diel Activity and Circadian Rhythms Seeds

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS
Pass: 96

These concepts are original Ouros material derived from high-level structural research. They must not be promoted to canon without review.

1. The Dawn That Moved
A marsh survey team notices that a familiar population now leaves its roost almost an hour earlier than in records from five years ago. New road noise, warmer nights, altered lighting and a predator shift are all plausible explanations.

2. Hoothoot Bell
A rural settlement historically checked its clocks against a local Hoothoot congregation. The birds' observed rhythm no longer matches the public clock after infrastructure repairs, creating a research problem rather than proof that either source is wrong.

3. The Empty Noon Route
Players repeatedly cross a route at midday and conclude a species has vanished. Camera and track records later show the same population is still present but has shifted activity to pre-dawn hours.

4. Dreepy at Last Light
A coastal observation program records a recurring Dreepy movement at dusk. One year the group arrives later, overlapping ferry traffic and creating a navigation and research issue.

5. The Two Markets
A public square is a produce market in the morning and a food/night market after sunset. Wild Pokémon use its edges at completely different hours, producing two ecological profiles for one physical place.

6. Oddish After Closing
A municipal garden appears nearly empty of Oddish during visitor hours. Night surveys reveal regular movement through paths assumed to be unused.

7. The Missing Dawn Chorus
A familiar morning soundscape weakens. The species are still present, but several begin calling later after a change in artificial lighting.

8. Three Cameras, Three Conclusions
Three camera stations on the same forest edge imply diurnal, nocturnal and cathemeral activity because each station has different placement, outages and human traffic.

9. The Night Shift Ranger
A Ranger works night patrols for one season and becomes the region's best source of nocturnal field observations. Their schedule is occupational state, not a personality label.

10. The Roost Above the Depot
A transport depot expands beneath an old roost. The population remains locally present but changes arrival and departure windows before any change in total occupancy becomes clear.

11. The Warm-Night Week
A heatwave produces unusually late activity in several normally daytime populations. The shift may disappear when temperatures normalize.

12. The Clocktower Colony
A colony uses a historic tower at predictable hours. Restoration work changes access without destroying the structure, creating a multi-year before/after study.

13. The Cathemeral Surprise
A species described locally as nocturnal is repeatedly observed at both midday and night in one valley. The right outcome may be a local-profile revision rather than declaring the old field guide fraudulent.

14. The Wrong Quiet Hours
A conservation site establishes visitor quiet hours based on old data. New observations show the most sensitive activity peak now occurs outside the protected window.

15. The Predator Who Arrives First
Two populations use the same water source. Their temporal overlap increases after a drought even though neither changes its home range.

16. The Commuter Trail
A wild collective crosses a roadside corridor after the last commuter bus each night. A new late service changes that overlap without changing the road itself.

17. Festival Morning After
A large annual festival ends at midnight, but wildlife timing around the square remains shifted for several days afterward.

18. The Roost That Stayed Empty Once
A single empty morning triggers concern. Follow-up work shows the group used a secondary site for one night after a storm.

19. The Old Field Notebook
A retired naturalist's notes contain decades of dawn/dusk observations. Modern researchers discover the times were written against an old local clock convention and need reinterpretation.

20. The Aquarium Lights
A waterfront attraction changes its lighting schedule. Nearby nocturnal activity shifts, but evidence is not yet sufficient to prove causation.

21. The Moonless Survey
Researchers expect a light-driven change during a dark night. The predicted shift does not appear, producing a useful null result.

22. The Night Ferry Window
A ferry operator and conservation team negotiate a short daily window that avoids the strongest wildlife movement. The agreement must be reviewed when the movement window changes seasonally.

23. The Former Partner at Dawn
A released former partner is observed repeatedly at a dawn feeding site years later. The sightings establish presence and timing, not a desire to reunite with the former Trainer.

24. The Schoolyard at Dusk
A schoolyard becomes an informal evening foraging site after students leave. A schedule change creates new overlap between people and Pokémon without changing ownership of the space.

25. The Burrow Shift
A burrowing population begins emerging after dark instead of before sunset. Soil temperature, road activity and predation are competing hypotheses.

26. The Seasonal Night
A population appears nocturnal in summer and crepuscular in winter. The field guide eventually stores separate seasonal profiles instead of one universal label.

27. The False Absence Report
A conservation report says a species was not observed for two months. Audit shows monitoring cameras were active only during the wrong hours.

28. Last Train, First Call
A station soundscape changes when a late train service is cancelled. A local Pokémon call that had been masked for years becomes detectable again.

29. The Dusk Map
Players build a map that marks what becomes active around sunset rather than where landmarks sit. Later editions show how urban growth changes those timing layers.

30. Twenty-Four Hours at One Pond
A research event samples the same pond continuously for a full day. Different teams discover that the location effectively contains several ecological communities separated by time.

## Long Arc A — Five Years at East Marsh

Year 1 establishes a coarse day/night baseline from imperfect surveys.

Year 2 adds cameras and acoustic monitors, revealing a strong dusk peak missed by earlier work.

Year 3 a new road opens and the population shifts later without abandoning the marsh.

Year 4 a heatwave pushes some activity toward pre-dawn and creates unusual overlap with another species.

Year 5 the regional field guide is revised. Old editions remain historically correct for what researchers knew at the time.

Possible outcomes include road schedule changes, quieter periods, new monitoring, no intervention, or a conclusion that several causes contributed.

## Long Arc B — The City in Three Shifts

A district is studied as three overlapping worlds: morning deliveries and commuters, afternoon public-space use, and nocturnal wildlife/service activity.

Players can discover that the same alley, plaza, rooftop or canal edge has different users across the day. Infrastructure changes can benefit one temporal community while harming another.

The arc can culminate in a district plan that manages time as well as space rather than simply adding or removing access.

## Long Arc C — The Diel Atlas

Multiple institutions build a regional atlas of activity windows over many seasons.

Early entries are simple labels. Later editions add local variation, seasonal profiles, sampling confidence, disturbance histories and known exceptions.

The atlas becomes a source of quests because discrepancies generate research questions, not because the system procedurally invents a crisis every day.

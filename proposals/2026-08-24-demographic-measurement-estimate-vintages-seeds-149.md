# Pass 149 Proposals — Demographic Measurement & Estimate Vintages

Status: NON-CANON. Supplements Pass 60; does not create a second demography authority.

## 30 candidates

1. **Two Counts, Both Correct** — An old estimate used the former town boundary; a newer publication uses the annexed one.
2. **The Missing Ferry Crew** — A field count happens while many usual residents are offshore for work.
3. **The Festival Census** — A one-day manual count accidentally captures visitors and gets repeated for years as the town’s “population.”
4. **The Survey That Changed Method** — A methodological revision creates an apparent jump with almost no actual movement.
5. **The Old Number Became Identity** — A rough historic estimate becomes a slogan residents still use after better data exists.
6. **The Evacuation Window** — An estimate taken during displacement is technically accurate for physical presence but misleading as usual residence.
7. **The Dormitory Opened Before Recruitment** — New capacity exists months before population responds.
8. **The Closed Road Sample** — One district is undercounted because the survey team cannot reach it.
9. **The Duplicate Register** — One actor appears twice after a name change; Identity fixes linkage without rewriting the original source records.
10. **The Quiet Seasonal Quarter** — The same homes are occupied every winter and mostly empty every summer.
11. **The Hospital Number** — Overnight patients are accidentally treated as local residents in a public infographic.
12. **The Commuter Town** — Daytime population rises sharply after rail opens while usual-resident population barely changes.
13. **The Research Boom** — Temporary staff produce a service surge that disappears when the expedition ends.
14. **The Staged Return** — Each reopened flood district gets a separate estimate revision instead of one instant population reset.
15. **The Former Resident Visits** — A festival attendance record is wrongly interpreted as evidence of permanent return.
16. **The Boundary Followed the River** — A river migration changes an old administrative reference and makes archived totals difficult to compare.
17. **The New Estimate Is Less Precise** — Better honesty about missing data widens uncertainty instead of fabricating an exact count.
18. **The Old Estimate Was Better Than Expected** — A later audit finds a rough historic count surprisingly close.
19. **The Population Claim Has No Method** — A famous guidebook number cannot be traced to any survey or register.
20. **The Census Without a Census Bureau** — Several institutions publish estimates from housing, school, water and transit data before Ouros has any formal enumeration body.
21. **The One Block Nobody Counted** — A newly built annex was absent from the survey map.
22. **The Empty House Problem** — Housing stock falls out of sync with residence because several homes are maintained but unused most of the year.
23. **The Temporary Camp That Stayed** — Emergency accommodation gradually becomes long-term housing, forcing a new residence interpretation rather than a visual reset.
24. **The Survey Arrived Too Early** — Seasonal workers were still en route when the reference date passed.
25. **The Survey Arrived Too Late** — A shutdown already sent temporary staff home.
26. **The Revised Past** — Better records produce a new historical estimate for five years earlier; Chronicle preserves both the old published number and the new reconstruction.
27. **The Same Town Name, Different Footprint** — Three atlas editions use the same label for three different geographic extents.
28. **The Service Data Lied by Proxy** — Water use falls because of conservation measures, not because population fell.
29. **The Count That Prevented a Quest** — A supposed depopulation mystery disappears when analysts notice the method changed.
30. **Nothing Happened Between Vintages** — Two estimates differ only because better source data arrived later.

## Long arc — Five Vintages of Meridian Ward

Five annual publications gradually improve coverage, change a boundary once and revise the previous two years after better records arrive. The story is institutional learning, not five separate population crises.

## Long arc — The Number Everyone Remembers

A settlement’s famous founding-era population figure appears on signs, anniversary material and schoolbooks. Research over several years reconstructs how that figure was created and why it was reasonable for its time even though modern estimates differ.

## Long arc — The Return Estimate

After a regional emergency, Crisis manages evacuation while Pass 60 tracks displacement and return. This protocol records changing methods for estimating how many people have actually re-established usual residence. The final result can remain a band rather than a perfect count.

## Encounter contract — Return Day at Riverside

FULL: if returnees/responders must move through threatened space, require `complete movement`, `AI tactical policy` and `Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback`; add `terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions` only if flood/debris mechanics are tactically active.

REDUCED: pause civilian return in world state, clear the perimeter, use a static legal battle, then resume the return process. Battle victory never determines the population estimate.

## Encounter contract — Seasonal Arrival at North Depot

FULL: moving passengers/wildlife require `complete movement`, objective-aware AI and adapter/playback.

REDUCED: Rail/Road Transit redirects passengers outside the grid; AutoPTU resolves only an independent static confrontation. Temporary-presence counts remain world state.

## Non-combat contract — Estimate Reconciliation Workshop

Researchers compare two vintages, their boundaries and source inputs. The correct outcome may be that both are valid for different purposes. No Skill roll or battle is needed unless future PTU/Caelo sources explicitly define a relevant mechanic.

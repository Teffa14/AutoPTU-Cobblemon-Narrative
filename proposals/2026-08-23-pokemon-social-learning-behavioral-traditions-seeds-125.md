# Ouros Behavioral Traditions & Social Learning Seeds — Pass 125

Status: NON-CANON proposals. All names, places, populations and events are candidates for later review.

These seeds use the proposed `pokemon-social-learning-behavioral-traditions-layer.md`. None grants PTU mechanics, Moves, Abilities, Features, Skills, capture modifiers or tactical bonuses.

## 1. The Orchard Knock

A local wild group has been documented knocking fallen fruit against one particular stone before eating it. A replacement retaining wall removes the stone. Researchers know the behavior is local, but do not yet know whether it is socially transmitted or simply easier at that location.

Possible consequence: workers can preserve the original stone, install a similar surface or do nothing and monitor the response.

## 2. The Phrase That Crossed the River

Two Chatot groups historically use different shared sayings. Recordings begin showing one phrase appearing on both sides of the river after a bridge and market route open.

Question: visiting individuals, human repetition, independent imitation or actual transmission between groups?

No semantic meaning is assumed.

## 3. The Last Experienced User

One persistent Pokémon is the only currently identified member known to use a complex local foraging sequence. It stops appearing at the site.

The story is observation, search and longitudinal follow-up. The technique is not automatically lost.

## 4. The New Lid Problem

Urban Pokémon that previously opened one style of refuse container stop accessing food after a sanitation retrofit. Weeks later, one individual is recorded using a new sequence. Months later, several others do the same.

The change can connect Urban Wildlife, Waste/Sanitation and behavioral monitoring without treating the animals as pests by default.

## 5. Three Valleys, Three Calls

The same species occupies three nearby valleys. Each population has a slightly different repeated contact-call pattern.

The investigation can remain unresolved for years: local learning, acoustic environment, population structure or another cause.

## 6. The Safe Crossing Tradition

A wildlife overpass was initially ignored. One recurring individual begins using it. Later observations show more members following the same approach path.

Road Ecology owns whether the crossing works physically. This layer owns only the possible transmission of the approach behavior.

## 7. The Ferry Bell

Wild Pokémon near a harbor regularly move away from the loading ramp before a ferry departs. The timing is too consistent to dismiss, but researchers disagree whether they respond to the bell, vibration, workers or the ferry itself.

A new silent boarding system creates a natural experiment without needing a crisis.

## 8. The Wrong Teacher

A famous individual is widely credited with teaching a local technique. Archived footage later shows younger Pokémon using it before that individual arrived.

Public Memory and Science can correct the story without erasing why the famous Pokémon became associated with it.

## 9. The Demonstrator Who Returned

A Pokémon released years earlier is observed using a harmless routine learned while partnered with a Trainer. Later, a wild group begins showing a similar sequence.

The system records chronology and possible influence. It does not infer that release caused transmission or that the former Trainer regained authority.

## 10. The Quiet Generation

A vocal tradition documented for decades becomes much less frequent in one season. The population remains present.

Possible explanations include cohort turnover, soundscape change, sampling bias, temporary behavior shift or genuine repertoire replacement.

## 11. The Better Shortcut

A group historically uses a difficult route to a water source. After one storm exposes a new passage, a few individuals begin using it. Years later the old route is nearly absent from observations.

The route can change without implying Evolution, increased intelligence or a mechanical movement bonus.

## 12. The Technique Without the Tool

Researchers believe a local tradition depends on one natural object. After that object disappears, the group reproduces the same sequence using a different material.

This is evidence that the learned sequence may be more persistent than the original physical context.

## 13. The Tool Without the Technique

A restoration project recreates the physical object associated with a historical behavior, but no current Pokémon uses it.

Restoring habitat conditions does not automatically restore learned knowledge.

## 14. The Market Closing Routine

Several urban Pokémon appear only after merchants finish closing stalls. New juveniles initially arrive at random times, then gradually converge on the established window.

This may support a social-learning hypothesis, but Diel Activity and Urban Wildlife must first rule out simpler attractant and timing explanations.

## 15. The Call That Was Human

A local Chatot phrase is treated for years as a population-specific saying. Archives reveal that it originated from a long-closed railway announcement.

The behavior can still be a genuine local tradition even if its original source was human.

## 16. The Tradition Nobody Performs Alone

A behavior is observed only when several Pokémon gather. Individuals tested or observed alone never perform the complete sequence.

This supports investigation into group context without creating a combo Move or shared action economy.

## 17. The False Tradition

Five individuals use the same unusual feeding method. A habitat survey later discovers that the local resource can only be accessed in that manner.

The correct conclusion may be ecological convergence rather than social transmission.

## 18. The Split Collective

A persistent wild collective divides after habitat change. One subgroup keeps the old routine. The other develops a different sequence.

Both retain historical continuity with the original group, but their behavioral revisions diverge.

## 19. The Reunited Repertoires

Two subgroups separated for years begin using the same stopover again. Their calls have drifted apart.

Long-term monitoring can record convergence, coexistence of variants or continued divergence.

## 20. The Researcher Created It

A field team repeatedly uses bait to demonstrate a puzzle-like procedure. Months later wild Pokémon continue performing it after the researchers leave.

The archive must mark the behavior as human-mediated. It cannot be published or represented as an untouched natural tradition.

## 21. The Tradition That Survived the Fire

A wildfire changes a habitat severely. The same foraging technique reappears after the population returns, but now uses different vegetation.

Wildfire and Flora own habitat recovery. This layer records continuity of the behavior only if observation supports it.

## 22. The Tradition That Did Not Survive

A habitat is restored successfully, the population returns, but an old local technique is never observed again despite strong monitoring effort.

The loss can remain a meaningful consequence even though population recovery succeeds.

## 23. The Visitor’s Trick

A migrating subgroup spends only two weeks per year at a stopover. Local residents begin performing one of the visitors’ resource-handling behaviors.

Migration owns the seasonal contact. Behavioral Traditions owns the possible cross-group transmission.

## 24. The Same Trick, No Contact

Two distant populations show nearly identical techniques with no known connection.

The system should preserve independent innovation as a live explanation rather than forcing an ancient common tradition.

## 25. The Archive of Wrong Names

Historical naturalists documented a local behavior under three different species labels because taxonomy changed over time.

Taxonomy and Identity/Record Linkage can reconcile the records while the observation history remains intact.

## 26. The Demonstration That Failed

An experienced Pokémon repeatedly performs a technique in front of younger members. None adopts it during the observed season.

The absence of transmission is itself useful evidence. Demonstration does not guarantee learning.

## 27. The Avoidance Tradition

A group consistently avoids one harmless-looking structure after older members encounter it. New individuals begin avoiding it after joining the group despite never experiencing the original incident.

The story can investigate social information and historical memory without creating Fear, Flinch or a status condition.

## 28. The Technique Becomes Ordinary

A behavior first considered remarkable spreads so widely across neighboring populations that later researchers stop treating it as a local curiosity.

Its history remains in Chronicle even if contemporary people consider it normal.

## 29. The Lost Recording Year

A monitoring archive has a five-year gap exactly when a major repertoire change appears to have occurred.

No procedural system fills the missing years with invented certainty. Researchers can narrow possibilities using later recordings, diaries and observations.

## 30. The Tradition Without a Quest

A local behavioral tradition changes normally over several years while players never intervene.

The world still records the change. Not every meaningful ecological history must become a player mission.

# Long arc A — Five Generations at Cedar Orchard

Year 1: one persistent individual is recorded using an unusual fruit-access technique.

Year 2: several naïve individuals are observed near the demonstrator; two later perform a similar sequence.

Year 3: orchard infrastructure changes and the original physical aid disappears.

Year 4: one subgroup adapts the sequence to a new object while another stops using it.

Year 5+: the original experienced Pokémon is no longer observed, but one variant persists.

Potential outcomes:

- well-supported behavioral tradition;
- independent local innovation remains plausible;
- tradition persists but changes form;
- technique fades;
- archive shows the behavior was always restricted to a few individuals.

The arc should work even if players only observe one or two years directly.

# Long arc B — The Chatot Repertoire Atlas

Three settlements maintain recordings of local Chatot groups for decades.

Early archive: each place has a recognizable shared repertoire.

Middle period: rail, markets and festivals increase movement between settlements.

Later period: phrases spread unevenly, old variants disappear, and one historical saying survives only in recordings.

Possible institutions:

- local natural-history archive;
- soundscape research network;
- community radio contributors;
- schools collecting public recordings.

Important boundary:

The project records sound and possible social transmission. It does not assume the phrases have propositional language meaning.

# Long arc C — The Knowledge Route

A mountainous wild collective historically uses a sequence of safe stopping places that experienced individuals appear to know.

A road, wildfire and changing snowpack alter the route over several years.

Questions accumulate:

- do younger Pokémon follow experienced individuals?
- can the route shift when one stop disappears?
- does a separated subgroup retain the sequence?
- what happens when former routes become safe again?

Migration, Cryosphere, Road Ecology and Behavioral Traditions each own different facts. No single layer claims the cause automatically.

# Promotion questions

Before any seed becomes canon, review:

- species grounding;
- whether the behavior is authored or procedurally observed;
- evidence needed before calling it socially transmitted;
- interaction with persistent Pokémon identity;
- whether coordinates or sensitive habitat information should be public;
- whether a human intervention created the behavior;
- whether a supposed tradition duplicates a real PTU Move/Ability/Feature;
- whether the full encounter can run or should use its reduced form;
- exact PTU/Caelo rules if a mechanic is required.

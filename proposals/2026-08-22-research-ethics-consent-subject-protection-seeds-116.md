# Ouros research ethics, consent & subject-protection seeds — Pass 116

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Every entry requires continuity, originality, PTU/Caelo and implementation review before promotion.

These candidates assume the proposed Research Ethics, Consent & Subject Protection layer. They deliberately avoid inventing mechanical consent rolls, Researcher Features, handling bonuses, medical effects or universal research law.

## Candidate 01 — The Study That Changed Its Question

A long-running migration project begins with one question. Midway through the third season, its strongest evidence points somewhere else.

The team can continue the original protocol, open a new question, or amend the study. Old observations remain valid for the purpose under which they were collected. The new question does not automatically inherit every permission.

Persistent consequence: later publications must distinguish which dataset versions support which claim.

## Candidate 02 — The Nest Survey That Stops Early

A seasonal field survey reaches a known site and discovers nesting started earlier than predicted.

The correct research outcome may be to stop approaching, record the phenology shift and return later. The site becomes more scientifically interesting precisely because the team did less that day.

No combat is required.

## Candidate 03 — Consent to Observe, Not to Sample

A Trainer volunteers themselves and a long-term partner for a movement study. They agree to observation and photography. Physical samples are outside the agreed scope.

A visiting researcher later proposes a sample because it would answer a valuable secondary question.

The hook is whether to seek new permission and change the protocol, not how to get the sample anyway.

## Candidate 04 — The Former Research Partner Returns

A wild Pokémon that cooperated with a survey team years ago returns to the same station after the study ended.

Its prior cooperation is historical state. It does not reactivate the old protocol or create permanent researcher authority.

The team can observe the return, attempt a new low-impact study, or simply leave it alone.

## Candidate 05 — The Missing Second Signature

An archival protocol record shows two required institutional approvals, but only one surviving authorization document.

The research itself may have been valid. The missing record may be clerical loss, incomplete digitization, a real authorization gap or something else.

The investigation should distinguish the status of the old study from the truth of its scientific results.

## Candidate 06 — The Camera Trap Became the Attractant

A camera installation begins receiving unusually close Pokémon visits.

A researcher suspects curiosity about the equipment itself is biasing the study.

The response is a method question: relocate, redesign, compare against passive sites or accept the limitation. It is not automatically a “smart Pokémon sabotages research” story.

## Candidate 07 — The Psychic Study With Two Views

Two participants report different experiences during the same shared psychic phenomenon.

Both reports remain first-person data. Neither participant gains access to the other's private mental content by default.

A research team can compare overlap without declaring one participant wrong simply because the accounts diverge.

## Candidate 08 — The Sample Nobody May Destroy

A unique historical specimen can answer a new technical question, but the proposed analysis consumes part of it.

The institution holding the object permits non-destructive study only.

The research hook becomes finding another method, comparable material or stronger justification instead of treating possession as permission.

## Candidate 09 — The Successful Null Study

A team spends a season testing a widely repeated local explanation for a Pokémon behavior pattern.

The expected relationship does not appear.

The null result closes several bad quest hooks and prevents future institutions from repeatedly sending people to “confirm” the same rumor.

## Candidate 10 — The Rare Site Hidden From the Paper

A field team confirms a sensitive breeding site.

The publication can describe habitat, timing and methods while redacting exact coordinates. Conservation records retain the precise location for authorized use.

Later, someone criticizes the paper for being incomplete. The disagreement is about disclosure, not truth.

## Candidate 11 — The Pokémon That Withdraws From Handling

A previously cooperative Pokémon begins withdrawing from a routine research setup.

The protocol stops handling and switches to observation-only while Care checks whether anything else is going on.

Do not infer fear, betrayal or trauma without authored evidence.

## Candidate 12 — The Volunteer Who Leaves Midseason

A PC or NPC participant withdraws from a long-term observational study after several visits.

The Chronicle records participation and withdrawal. The data-use permission determines what earlier records may still be analyzed.

The researcher does not become an antagonist for asking what is permitted, and the participant does not become unreliable for leaving.

## Candidate 13 — The Study Built From Old Records

A new question can be answered largely through old museum notebooks, weather logs and archived photographs.

The low-impact approach avoids a new field season entirely.

This is a full research storyline with almost no direct interaction with wild Pokémon.

## Candidate 14 — The Control Device in the Museum

A museum receives a deactivated historical device once used to control or constrain Pokémon.

The artifact can be studied as history, engineering and institutional memory. Restoring its original function is not automatically acceptable or necessary.

Its provenance matters more than turning it into a dungeon key.

## Candidate 15 — The Modified Pokémon Archive

An archive contains fragmented records about an old artificial-modification program.

The surviving subject, if any, is treated as a persistent Pokémon with agency and history, not as proof-of-concept equipment.

The research problem is reconstructing what was done, what was claimed, and who knew what at each stage.

No new modification procedure is generated.

## Candidate 16 — The Protocol Written Before Evolution

A Pokémon enrolled in a non-invasive study evolves between visits.

Its persistent identity remains the same, but equipment fit, movement profile, handling assumptions or legal PTU options may have changed.

The protocol needs compatibility review rather than pretending the old physical procedure still applies unchanged.

## Candidate 17 — The Trainer Agrees, the Pokémon Does Not Approach

A Trainer is willing to participate in a study involving their Pokémon. At the setup, the Pokémon repeatedly remains outside the handling area.

The team records the behavior and stops the procedure.

No Charm/Command roll is generated to compel participation.

## Candidate 18 — The Wild Pokémon Cooperates With One Researcher Only

A wild Pokémon repeatedly approaches one member of a field team but ignores or avoids the others.

That pattern is an observation, not proof of friendship, ownership or exclusive “chosen one” status.

The team must decide whether the observer effect makes the study more useful or more biased.

## Candidate 19 — The Clinic Dataset With No Names

A clinic notices a pattern across many cases and wants to support a regional study.

The research team receives deidentified aggregate records instead of full case files.

The story can still uncover an environmental signal without exposing private treatment histories.

## Candidate 20 — The Festival Survey Too Close to Roosts

A festival organizer invites researchers to run a public wildlife survey during a major event.

The proposed observation point overlaps a sensitive roost. The event can proceed while the study changes location or method.

This creates cooperation between Festivals, Conservation, Soundscapes and Science without turning either side into an obstacle faction.

## Candidate 21 — The Rehabilitation Study Becomes Care First

A rehabilitation program also tracks recovery outcomes for research.

One subject's condition changes. Care takes priority and the research schedule pauses.

Later analysis can record the interruption instead of pretending every subject followed an ideal protocol.

## Candidate 22 — The Fossil Sample With Limited Destructive Analysis

A fossil specimen has enough material for one destructive test, but several research teams want different analyses.

Custody, scientific value, destructive-use permission and funding pressure are all separate states.

Possible resolution includes deferral, alternative material, a shared analysis or no destructive test at all.

## Candidate 23 — The Dream Reports Participants Can Retract

A voluntary dream study stores participant-written reports.

Participants can later restrict future use of particular reports without rewriting the fact that the session occurred.

The system must keep the audit event while hiding content no longer authorized for research/publication.

## Candidate 24 — The Survey That Finds Nothing and Closes a Rumor

A high-quality survey finds no evidence for a much-repeated rare-Pokémon claim under the conditions it can actually test.

The correct conclusion is scoped: the claim was not supported by this survey. It does not prove the Pokémon can never occur there.

Public communication must preserve that nuance.

## Candidate 25 — The Sponsor Wants Faster Results

A funding source asks a field program to add more handling sessions to accelerate a deadline.

The existing protocol does not authorize the expanded schedule.

The institution can amend, refuse, negotiate scope or seek other funding. The sponsor's request never becomes automatic authority.

## Candidate 26 — The Instrument Is Safe, the Route Is Not

A planned field method remains acceptable, but a storm damages the only safe route to the site.

Research stops because Travel/Crisis state changed, not because the science or instrument failed.

This prevents mission generators from treating every pause as a research problem.

## Candidate 27 — The Permit Covers the Site, Not the New Cave

A survey discovers an unmapped cave extension beyond the authorized field area.

The finding is real and can be mapped as an observation. Entering, sampling or installing equipment there requires a new site decision.

The discovery itself is already meaningful progress.

## Candidate 28 — The Observation That Became Public Too Soon

A preliminary observation leaks or is accidentally published before review and before sensitive coordinates are removed.

The incident can produce correction, access review, conservation pressure and public-memory residue.

Do not assume hacking or malicious intent without evidence.

## Candidate 29 — The Protocol Deviation With No Harm

A field team moves one sensor after floodwater reaches its planned mounting point.

The change violates the literal placement plan but causes no observed harm and may have been the safest choice.

The deviation is recorded, reviewed and incorporated into the next protocol version. No misconduct tag is inferred.

## Candidate 30 — The Archive of Withdrawn Permission

A university archive contains decades of research records created under different permission models.

Some data remain open, some are restricted, some can be used only in aggregate and some are preserved only as historical metadata.

The archive itself becomes a long-term institutional story about changing norms.

# Long arc A — Five Versions of the Same Study

Year 1: a small field team starts a low-impact migration survey.

Year 2: a new instrument improves measurement but requires new site permissions.

Year 3: earlier nesting changes the safe observation window.

Year 4: a community group requests stronger coordinate protections after visitor pressure increases.

Year 5: a secondary team successfully replicates part of the work using a different method.

The campaign preserves all five protocol versions, permissions, null results, amendments and disagreements. The institution becomes more capable because it learns how to revise responsibly, not because it unlocks a generic “research tier.”

# Long arc B — The Research Station Learns to Say No

An old field station begins the campaign with a culture of collecting whatever can be collected because that is how its archives were built.

Over several years, new staff, changing habitats, a damaged specimen, a participant withdrawal and better non-contact methods force the station to distinguish curiosity from necessity.

Possible end states:

- the station formalizes scoped protocols;
- it creates a low-impact field team;
- it returns or restricts some samples;
- it opens older decisions to review;
- it remains internally divided;
- it decides some forms of research are outside its mission.

None require declaring earlier researchers universally evil.

# Long arc C — The Subject With a History

A persistent Pokémon appears in several research programs across many years.

First it is a wild observation.

Later it receives care after an unrelated incident.

A later survey observes it again after release.

A photography project tentatively identifies the same individual.

Years later, a new team requests more direct study.

The arc tests whether Ouros can preserve one Pokémon's identity, prior interactions, current agency, care history and research permissions without converting long observation history into ownership or guaranteed cooperation.

# Encounter contract C — Consent Withdrawal During Handling

Narrative premise:

A planned handling procedure loses valid participation conditions because a PC declines, a Pokémon withdraws from the setup, or a previously valid scope no longer covers the procedure.

Default version:

No battle.

The procedure stops. The player can document the event, contact the lead/caretaker, switch to observation-only, request amendment or end the session.

No social Skill check can force a PC to participate. No Command/Charm/Guile roll is invented to force a Pokémon into research.

If an unrelated threat appears afterward, it receives a separate battle contract.

# Canon questions raised by these candidates

- Which Ouros institutions can authorize field research, clinical-linked studies, destructive sampling or psychic/private-information research?
- Which permissions belong to site operators, custodians, Trainers, participants or institutions?
- How can an explicitly communicative Pokémon express a research decision when canon supports that communication?
- Which research records are private, public, restricted or redacted?
- Can participants restrict future use of old data, and how does Chronicle preserve the history without exposing the data?
- What research uses of clinic data are allowed?
- What procedures involving Eggs, juveniles, artificial Pokémon or modified Pokémon are forbidden or heavily restricted?
- How are protected-site coordinates handled in publication?
- Which historical research programs already exist before the player arrives?
- What exactly do PTU/Caelo rules allow around Researcher, Education Skills, Command/Loyalty, psychic information, restraint, handling, collection and experimental devices?

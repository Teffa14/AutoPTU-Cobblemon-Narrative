# Ouros Proposal Seeds — Settlement / Wild Pokémon Coexistence Response — Pass 127

Status: NON-CANON proposals. These are situation seeds for later review. Nothing in this file establishes institutions, law, species behavior, locations or mechanics as Ouros canon.

## Design use

These seeds exercise the proposed settlement/wild-Pokémon coexistence response continuity layer without requiring every interaction to become a battle.

Each seed should be instantiated only after checking current world state, local ecology, settlement routines, authority, persistent Pokémon identity and the exact PTU/AutoPTU capability contract.

## Local situation seeds

### 1. The Same Bin Every Tuesday

A market records a recurring waste-container disturbance at nearly the same hour each week.

Initial belief: one persistent Pokémon has learned the collection schedule.

Evidence possibilities:

- camera records support one identifiable individual;
- three different Pokémon are using the same damaged latch;
- the container opens before any Pokémon arrives;
- a shopkeeper leaves food nearby on collection night;
- collection timing changed and old resident expectations did not.

Durable consequences:

- latch repair;
- schedule change;
- altered shop routine;
- recurring individual becomes known locally;
- public story persists even after evidence changes.

### 2. The Nest Above Door Three

A nesting pair uses a roof ledge above a frequently used entrance.

Competing stakeholder goals:

- safe worker/customer access;
- preserve the nest;
- keep the business operating;
- avoid disturbing an adjacent property;
- maintain emergency access.

Possible outcomes:

- alternate entrance used temporarily;
- awning or barrier change through Facilities;
- timed access arrangement;
- Conservation review;
- no action because the feared impact is unsupported;
- later recurrence after building renovation changes the ledge.

The pair does not become aggressive by default. Nesting does not create a reaction mechanic without exact rules.

### 3. The Drain Noise Has Three Owners

Residents report a nocturnal sound beneath one street.

The drain belongs operationally to one utility, a nearby road to another owner system, and the suspected Pokémon habitat falls under a third stewardship scope.

The mystery is institutional as well as ecological: who can inspect which section and what evidence is available from each entrance?

The final answer may identify:

- one Pokémon moving through the drainage network;
- several unrelated sources of sound;
- machinery plus a Pokémon sighting;
- an old pipe acting as an acoustic path.

### 4. The Pokémon Everyone Calls Back

Residents insist that the "same" Pokémon has returned after a prior intervention.

Identity evidence is weak.

Players can compare:

- visual marks;
- camera angles;
- time windows;
- telemetry if a lawful prior monitoring program exists;
- individual behavior observations;
- prior case records.

Possible result: same individual, sibling/collective member, unrelated Pokémon, or still uncertain.

### 5. The Store Fixed the Hole, the Alley Got Busier

A facility repair successfully blocks one entry point.

Within days, reports rise in the adjacent alley.

Potential explanations:

- the same subject shifted route;
- a previously hidden population became more visible;
- waste access changed at the same time;
- unrelated seasonal movement began;
- reporting increased because residents are now watching.

The old intervention can be technically successful while the wider coexistence objective remains unresolved.

### 6. The School Has Two Agreements

A school-like institution, only if such a local institution is already canonized, has an informal coexistence routine remembered differently by older staff and newer managers.

One group believes a recurring Pokémon may remain if cleanup is performed. Another believes it was always meant to be excluded from indoor space.

The quest is about reconstructing:

- what was actually agreed;
- which scope it covered;
- whether current facility conditions match the old arrangement;
- whether the Pokémon involved now is the same individual.

No universal school policy is created.

### 7. The Market Opened Earlier, the Pokémon Did Too

A market moves opening time after an unrelated operational change. A familiar wild collective begins appearing earlier as well.

Residents assume the Pokémon "learned the new schedule."

Possible causes:

- food availability changed;
- lighting changed;
- waste pickup moved;
- human foot traffic displaced the collective from a nearby route;
- seasonal dawn shifted;
- the apparent pattern is sampling bias.

### 8. The Garden Nobody Meant to Feed

A public planting area produces fruit/berries or other food accessible to wild Pokémon.

A recurring gathering becomes socially contentious.

Potential responses:

- accept the gathering and adjust maintenance;
- alter planting design;
- restrict one path during a bounded period;
- improve cleanup;
- investigate whether feeding by visitors is the stronger attractant;
- establish a stewardship observation program.

Do not infer that the Pokémon "belong" to the garden or that feeding is legal/illegal without canon.

### 9. The Roof Camera Changed the Story

A resident reports one large aggressive Pokémon every night.

A camera later shows several smaller Pokémon using the roof, with only one close approach to the resident.

The report remains historically valid as testimony about what the resident perceived. The case revises identity and aggression confidence without accusing the reporter of deception.

### 10. The Loading Bay Is Quiet on Sundays

A wild Pokémon repeatedly crosses a loading bay on workdays but not Sundays.

Possible explanation chain:

- delivery traffic displaces it toward one opening;
- food residue appears only after certain deliveries;
- a nearby corridor closes during work hours;
- worker attention produces reporting bias;
- a specific recurring vehicle matters.

The solution can involve schedule/route redesign rather than Pokémon removal.

### 11. The Temporary Barrier Became the New Path

A short-term barrier intended to separate people and wild Pokémon changes pedestrian flow.

Months later:

- residents prefer the new route;
- a shop entrance migrated toward it;
- wild Pokémon continue using the old edge;
- the barrier itself is no longer needed;
- public memory treats the arrangement as permanent.

A later renovation forces the settlement to decide whether to preserve the accidental coexistence pattern.

### 12. Three Reports, Zero Damage

Three households independently report a Pokémon near their fences.

No damage, entry or aggressive behavior is verified.

The meaningful question becomes why the sightings suddenly matter now.

Possible answers:

- a new resident circulated an alarming rumor;
- vegetation removal made an old route visible;
- a rare-looking individual attracts attention;
- a previous unrelated incident changed risk perception;
- the sightings are a normal seasonal movement newly documented.

Case closure with `PRESENCE_SUPPORTED / HARM_NOT_SUPPORTED` is a valid outcome.

### 13. The Damage Is Real, the Culprit Is Not

A storage area has genuine repeated damage.

Tracks or sightings initially point toward one Pokémon species. Later evidence shows:

- the structural fault predates the sightings;
- a different Pokémon uses the opening after it forms;
- human equipment causes most damage;
- two causes are simultaneously true.

The case can hand repair to Maintenance while preserving an ecological observation that the Pokémon opportunistically use the new access.

### 14. The Resident Who Feeds Them Knows Their Names

One resident informally distinguishes several recurring wild Pokémon by markings and habits.

Their identification history may be valuable evidence, but their emotional names and interpretations do not automatically establish scientific identity.

This NPC can become a durable bridge between local testimony and Wildlife Monitoring.

### 15. The Service Closed, the Pokémon Stayed

A facility temporarily stops operating for an unrelated reason. The wild Pokémon associated with repeated complaints remain in the area.

This weakens a theory that active operations alone caused the interaction and opens alternatives:

- shelter;
- residual food/waste;
- water;
- established route;
- adjacent land use;
- incorrect subject attribution.

## Mystery structures

### 16. Five Times the Pokémon "Came Back"

Five records use the phrase "came back," but mean different things:

1. first sighting after a week without reports;
2. first camera detection after a monitoring gap;
3. a similar-looking individual at the old location;
4. a known individual detected in the wider district;
5. the original impact recurring despite no confirmed Pokémon sighting.

The mystery is solved by identity scope, monitoring coverage and timestamps.

### 17. Four Doors, One Broken Screen

Four tenants report Pokémon entry through different doors.

The building inspection discovers one shared service void behind the units and a damaged screen at a fifth location.

The case separates reported entry point from actual physical access mechanism.

### 18. The Quiet Week

No complaints are received for seven days after a mitigation.

Possible interpretations:

- action worked;
- residents were away for an event;
- reporting channel failed;
- monitoring camera was offline;
- the Pokémon shifted activity time;
- weather temporarily changed activity;
- service itself was closed.

The case cannot mark success from silence alone.

### 19. Two Neighborhoods Blame the Same Pokémon

Separate districts report similar impacts and circulate the same photograph.

Chronology proves the image was reused and cannot establish one travelling individual.

The real pattern may still matter: both districts adopted the same container design with the same weakness.

### 20. The Relocation Record Ends Too Early

Only if relocation exists in local canon: historical records say a Pokémon was "removed" from a site years ago.

Missing records leave destination acceptance, placement and follow-up uncertain.

A current sighting forces the player to distinguish:

- return of the same individual;
- incomplete historical transfer;
- inaccurate identity;
- a later unrelated individual.

## Exploration concepts

### 21. The Alley With Three Generations of Gates

An old commercial lane contains three generations of gate hardware, patched wall openings, obsolete signs and a narrow route still used by wild Pokémon.

Players reconstruct the history from:

- maintenance records;
- photographs;
- resident testimony;
- old shop schedules;
- wildlife observations;
- marks on the structures.

The exploration works with static geometry and provenance. No tactical wildlife mechanic is required.

### 22. The Underpass Everyone Stopped Using

A pedestrian underpass was gradually abandoned after repeated Pokémon sightings decades ago, but the official closure happened later for an unrelated structural reason.

The player reconstructs how rumor, routine, maintenance and later policy combined to create current world state.

A reopened underpass need not remove the Pokémon. It can incorporate a new shared-use design if canon later approves it.

## NPC / faction candidates

### 23. The Pattern Keeper

A resident maintains years of sighting notes. Excellent chronology, weak causal interpretation.

Use: reliable timestamps paired with overconfident motive claims.

### 24. The Facility Pragmatist

A manager cares about keeping doors, loading bays or public access operating. They prefer changes with measurable operational outcomes and may resist ecological language they consider vague.

Use: conflict over scope without villainy.

### 25. The Cautious Identifier

A researcher or steward refuses to say "same Pokémon" until individual evidence is strong enough.

Use: teaches the player that uncertainty is part of competent investigation.

### 26. The Neighbor Who Likes the Visitors

An NPC values the wild Pokémon presence and documents its social meaning while acknowledging real access or cleanup problems.

Use: prevents every coexistence story from having only removal-oriented stakeholders.

### 27. The Old Responder

Someone remembers a previous intervention and the unintended consequence that followed.

Use: turns institutional memory into practical quest evidence.

## Longer arc — A District Learns Who Shares Its Edges

Status: NON-CANON.

### Stage 1: ordinary background

Wild Pokémon already appear in alleys, drainage edges, roofs, gardens and service yards. NPC dialogue treats some as familiar and others as unremarkable.

### Stage 2: a visible interaction

One recurring access or service problem produces multiple reports. Evidence initially supports several explanations.

### Stage 3: first response

A facility or schedule change addresses the most obvious mechanism. The response works partially.

### Stage 4: wider pattern

A second location reports a similar interaction. Investigation reveals either a shared built-environment cause, a movement corridor, a different individual, or another bounded explanation.

### Stage 5: disagreement

Residents, businesses and stewards want different outcomes. The player can improve evidence and help owner systems execute selected measures but cannot manufacture institutional powers.

### Stage 6: stable arrangement

The district adopts one or more changes. The wild Pokémon may still be present. Some residents alter routines. Signs, gates, collection times or paths change physically.

### Stage 7: later callback

Months or years later, renovation, seasonality, a new Pokémon individual or a reopened route stresses the old arrangement. The previous case supplies evidence and institutional memory rather than repeating from zero.

## Encounter seed B — Nesting Roof Access Perimeter

Full version:

A hostile encounter occurs near a restricted roof-access perimeter while the nesting scope remains protected.

Potential unsupported complexity includes edge falls, changing exclusion cells, crossing reactions, active withdrawal and AI zone avoidance.

## Encounter seed C — Authorized Relocation Staging Diversion

This seed may only instantiate if canon already establishes a legitimate relocation process.

Full version:

A separate hostile encounter blocks access near a staging route. Escort, Intercept, protected carrier/enclosure semantics, timed departure and objective-aware AI may be required.

Reduced version:

Pause or complete custody staging through world state and move the relocation subject outside BattleSpec. Resolve a conventional static route-access battle. Afterward, the authorized owner resumes the relocation workflow.

Battle outcome cannot create authorization, custody transfer, destination acceptance or placement.

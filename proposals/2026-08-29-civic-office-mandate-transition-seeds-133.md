# Ouros Proposal Seeds — Civic Office, Mandate & Transition Continuity — Pass 133

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS.
Date: 2026-08-29

These seeds require later regional and institutional review. They deliberately avoid defining a universal Ouros political system.

## 1. The Nameplate Changed Before the Keys

A public office door displays the incoming holder's name. The effective transition already occurred, but one restricted room still uses a credential controlled by a separate institutional owner.

The story is about independent clocks, not sabotage.

Useful owners:

- Civic Governance for office state;
- Credentials/Authorization for access;
- Archives for records inside the room;
- Maintenance only if the physical lock itself has a fault.

Player work:

Reconstruct which access is actually missing and route the unresolved credential correctly.

## 2. The Keys Changed Before the Nameplate

The inverse case. Authority and access are already current, but signage replacement is late.

Residents keep sending requests to the former holder because the public directory and door still show the old name.

Persistent consequence:

After correction, the old plate can enter the archive or remain visible in a storage area rather than disappearing from world history.

## 3. The Notice Signed Yesterday

A notice is published after an office transition but bears the outgoing holder's signature.

Possible benign resolution:

- drafted and approved before transition;
- queued for later publication;
- published by the institutional channel after authority changed.

The Evidence Graph preserves draft timestamp, approval timestamp and publication timestamp.

## 4. The Project With Three Administrations

One project is remembered as belonging to three different office-holders.

All memories contain part of the truth:

- first holder approved the concept;
- acting holder authorized a narrow implementation step within existing scope;
- successor was present when construction began.

The project owner retains the actual phase history.

## 5. The Acting Holder Who Says No

An acting office-holder handles routine requests efficiently but refuses one high-profile decision because it lies outside the temporary mandate.

The dramatic tension comes from restraint and institutional limits rather than incompetence.

Possible follow-on:

Players must find the actual decision route instead of persuading the acting holder to exceed it.

## 6. The Former Candidate at the Same Market Stall

A person who sought an office through whatever local canon-approved procedure exists does not receive it. They remain a normal merchant, researcher, teacher, ranger or other established NPC.

The character can continue to care about civic questions without becoming a permanent rival or villain.

## 7. The Outgoing Holder Still Knows the Shortcut

The former holder no longer possesses office authority but remains the person who remembers why an old service route was adopted.

Their testimony becomes evidence. It is not current policy.

This seed supports later archive reconstruction and oral-history continuity.

## 8. Two Calendars on One Wall

An office preserves an old project calendar and a new public-service calendar side by side.

A visitor assumes one replaced the other. In reality they track different owner systems.

Potential mystery:

A deadline that appears missed belongs to the project calendar, while the service deadline was met.

## 9. The Desk Stayed Empty for Eleven Days

A role is vacant, but routine services continue through staff under an authored continuity rule.

What does not continue automatically:

- exceptional approvals;
- new delegations;
- review decisions;
- decisions outside the continuity scope.

This creates a town that keeps functioning while some questions visibly wait.

## 10. The Archive Box Nobody Inherited

A box of records was never part of the formal handoff manifest because it had already been transferred to Archives before the office transition.

The missing box mystery ends with provenance, not theft.

## 11. The Institutional Mailbox Never Moved

People assume the mailbox belongs to the old holder because their name appeared beside it for years.

The mailbox is actually an office channel. The operator changes while the address remains stable.

Potential social consequence:

Some residents continue addressing messages personally until the new public convention settles.

## 12. The Delegate Was Never the Successor

A respected specialist receives a narrow delegation during a crisis or project. Public memory later conflates visibility with succession.

Archive records show the delegation's exact scope and expiry.

The specialist can remain influential without ever having held the office.

## 13. The Decision Survived the Holder

A valid project decision was made under a prior holder and remains active after transition because no authored expiry or review changed it.

The incoming holder may dislike it, support it or remain neutral. Those are separate character facts.

The story can examine implementation obligations without assigning moral alignment.

## 14. The First Meeting Was Not the First Day

Public memory dates a new holder's tenure from their first visible meeting. Office records show authority became effective several days earlier.

Both dates persist because they answer different questions.

## 15. The Last Meeting Was Not the Last Day

The outgoing holder's final public meeting occurred before their mandate ended. Routine office work continued afterward.

An apparently unsigned gap can be explained through staff records and institutional channels.

## 16. The Old Account Still Exists

An institutional communication system preserves an archived snapshot of the prior holder period while the current channel continues under new operators.

Minecraft representation can show archive terminals or books only after Ouros determines what is publicly accessible.

## Mystery — Seven Dates, Two Holders

Evidence set:

- selection/result announcement;
- outgoing final public meeting;
- authority-effective record;
- incoming first public meeting;
- archive custody handoff;
- directory update;
- credential activation.

Players initially expect one transition date. The solution is that each date records a different transition event.

No fraud is required.

## Mystery — The Signature in the Wrong Administration

A project update appears under the new administration but carries the prior holder's authorization reference.

Resolution options consistent with the architecture:

- authorization preceded transition;
- implementation/reporting followed it;
- current owner did not need a new decision;
- public filing grouped the update by publication period rather than decision period.

## Mystery — Three People Called “Acting”

Residents use the same colloquial label for:

- a formal acting office-holder;
- a delegated project lead;
- a long-serving clerk who kept the desk open.

The players reconstruct each actor's actual authority from records and observed actions.

## Exploration — The Office Above the Old Market Hall

An older civic office was relocated during a market reconstruction. The former rooms remain physically accessible as archive/storage space.

Environmental storytelling:

- obsolete room numbers;
- a removed public counter;
- patched cable or notice-board mounts;
- old project maps;
- a route from the office to the former meeting hall;
- one nameplate preserved in storage.

The player reconstructs institutional geography without any supernatural explanation.

Current implementation status:

READY as noncombat exploration using static Minecraft geometry and authoritative provenance records.

## Exploration — The Corridor of Four Nameplates

A public building keeps prior office nameplates in chronological order as ordinary archival material or institutional decoration, if later canon approves that practice.

A fifth mounting point is empty because that holder used another room during renovations.

The apparent missing administration becomes a Construction/Archives continuity question.

Current implementation status:

READY if the regional archival/display practice is canon-approved. No tactical mechanic required.

## Longer arc — A Town Learns What Changes When the Office Does

### Establishment

Players first encounter the office through ordinary service: a route project, market coordination, conservation meeting, festival plan or other mandate that local canon already supports.

The current holder is shown as one actor inside an institution rather than the institution itself.

### Transition approaches

A transition becomes expected through the local authored route.

NPCs discuss practical questions:

- which projects remain active;
- when public meetings move;
- who receives correspondence;
- whether an acting period exists;
- which records must be prepared.

No universal campaign or voting mechanics are assumed.

### Handover

Players may help with a records manifest, project inspection, archive delivery or public notice correction.

One inconsistency appears dramatic but resolves as two valid clocks or scopes.

### First new decision

The new holder eventually makes one decision genuinely attributable to the new holder episode.

Inherited projects remain separately attributable.

### Return months later

Visible consequences can include:

- new nameplate;
- unchanged institutional mailbox;
- one completed inherited project;
- one revised proposal;
- old records archived;
- acting holder returned to previous work;
- losing or alternate candidates still present in community life;
- NPC routines adjusted to the new meeting schedule.

The town changes without being reset.

## Faction seed — Continuity Coalition

NON-CANON role pattern, not a named faction.

Several unrelated operators agree that active services should survive transitions with minimal disruption. They may disagree strongly on policy while cooperating on handoff quality.

This makes procedural continuity a cross-faction interest rather than a political ideology.

## Faction seed — Review Advocates

NON-CANON role pattern.

A civic group wants inherited pending decisions reviewed wherever a valid review route exists.

They cannot create review authority by demanding it. Their actions may instead focus on evidence collection, public consultation or proposing a new decision.

## NPC — The Scope Keeper

Maintains a practical list of which delegations and temporary permissions remain active.

Strength:

Excellent institutional memory.

Limit:

Cannot create authority or resolve an ambiguous governing rule.

## NPC — The Former Holder Who Stayed Local

Returns to an established ordinary role after leaving office.

Narrative value:

Players can meet someone who possesses historical perspective but no longer controls current decisions.

## NPC — The New Holder Who Inherits a Good Idea From a Rival

Discovers that one inherited project is useful and continues it.

This prevents every transition from becoming total policy reversal and creates richer rival relationships.

## NPC — The Clerk Who Worked Under Four Holders

Knows how forms, meeting schedules and archive conventions changed over time.

Their recollections are testimony, not infallible canon.

## Canon questions intentionally unresolved

- Which Ouros settlements have formal civic offices?
- Which use councils, elders, guilds, League-linked bodies, rotating stewards or other structures?
- Do any use elections?
- If so, who participates and under what canon rules?
- Which offices permit acting coverage?
- Which allow delegation?
- How are effective times established?
- Which records belong to an office rather than an individual?
- Which records are public, restricted or private?
- How are credentials activated or retired?
- What happens to pending decisions when a holder changes?
- Which decisions have authored review routes?
- Can a former holder immediately take another institutional role?
- What recurring festivals/services survive transitions by default under their own owner systems?
- Which historical transitions matter enough to be visible in present-day Ouros?

No seed above answers these questions silently.

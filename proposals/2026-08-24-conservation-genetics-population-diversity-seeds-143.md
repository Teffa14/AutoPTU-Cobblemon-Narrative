# Conservation genetics & population diversity seeds — pass 143

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros material derived from high-level research structures. No real conservation program, Pokémon plot or campaign is copied.

## Design intent

These concepts treat genetic evidence as one layer of a persistent ecological history. They avoid purity language, hidden breeding optimization and the assumption that more visible Pokémon automatically means a healthier population.

## 30 candidate concepts

1. The Population That Recovered Too Fast
A protected coastal Pokémon population becomes locally abundant again. Fishers report ecological pressure in one bay while genetic monitoring still suggests a narrow founder history. The management question is no longer simply “protect more” or “protect less.”

2. Seven Founders, Twenty Years Later
An island population descends from a documented release group. Chronicle retains the original identities, but modern surveys find immigrants that were never part of the plan. The old founder story is still true and no longer complete.

3. The Missing Variant in the Museum
A century-old specimen contains evidence of diversity absent from current samples. The discovery changes the historical baseline without proving when or why that variation disappeared.

4. Two Valleys, One Species
Two nearby populations look identical in ordinary field observations. Movement data suggests little exchange between them. Researchers must decide whether more sampling is justified before making any management claim.

5. The Corridor Worked, Maybe
A wildlife overpass begins receiving regular use. Years later, genetic evidence suggests some connectivity between populations, but the study cannot attribute the entire change to the crossing because another route reopened during the same period.

6. The Reintroduction With Good Numbers
A restored population has excellent abundance and juvenile recruitment. A second research team points out that nearly all sampled individuals may descend from a small subset of the original release group. Both reports can be correct.

7. The Source Population Matters Too
A conservation program considers moving more individuals to a restored site. The source population has its own conservation needs, forcing the institution to track donor impact instead of treating source animals as unlimited supply.

8. Three Releases, Three Histories
Three populations of the same species were re-established decades apart. One received later immigrants, one remained isolated, and one mixed naturally with a neighboring population. Modern surveys reveal that identical-looking populations can have very different histories.

9. The Family Tree That Was Too Neat
A nursery archive appears to provide a perfect pedigree for a restoration program. Later evidence shows that one wild adult entered the breeding population without being recorded correctly. The correction improves history rather than creating scandal by default.

10. The Sample Was Not Representative
A dramatic report of low diversity came from a small accessible shoreline section. A broader survey later finds a different pattern inland. The first analysis remains valid for the sample it actually studied.

11. The Immigrant Nobody Expected
A tagged individual appears beyond the corridor thought to contain its population. Several years later, descendants or genetic evidence may show whether that movement mattered demographically.

12. The Quiet Gene-Flow Year
Nothing dramatic happens. Routine sampling records another ordinary year of exchange between two populations. Decades later, this quiet record becomes critical for reconstructing connectivity before a road expansion.

13. The Island Population Is Small, Not Doomed
Public rumor treats low headcount as proof that an island population is genetically collapsing. The available data does not support that conclusion. The quest becomes correcting certainty, not manufacturing a crisis.

14. The Big Population With a Narrow History
A species becomes extremely common around one wetland after a historic recovery program. High abundance masks the fact that most individuals descend from a small founder set. Management still requires evidence before intervention.

15. The Historical Label Was Wrong
A museum sample assigned to one population a century ago is re-examined after Taxonomy and Identity revisions. The specimen was real; its old location label was real; the inferred population assignment may have been wrong.

16. The Second Source
A restoration team proposes adding individuals from another source population. The debate centers on documented compatibility, local adaptation hypotheses and uncertainty. Ouros never turns the decision into a simple “diversity +10” upgrade.

17. The Release That Became a Migration
A reintroduced population eventually begins using a seasonal route nobody planned. Migration becomes part of its current identity. Genetics later helps test whether another population is contributing to that route.

18. The Corridor Closed After Success
A crossing project is decommissioned because authorities think the population is already restored. Five years later, monitoring shows that movement between habitats mattered more than expected. The original decision need not have been malicious or irrational.

19. The Founder Group Was Never Random
Archived meeting notes show that the original release group was selected for practical transport reasons rather than representativeness. Modern researchers must interpret that historical constraint without judging it through knowledge that did not exist at the time.

20. The Population That Split Without Moving
A river channel changes and creates a new barrier between habitats. The same population gradually becomes two management units even though no Pokémon were deliberately relocated.

21. The Ferry Passenger
A persistent wild Pokémon repeatedly appears on an island where its population was thought isolated. Evidence suggests it may be using human transport opportunistically. Biosecurity and Conservation disagree initially about how to classify the movement.

22. The Nursery Contribution Question
Captive-bred individuals were released successfully for years. Later monitoring asks whether those lines contribute equally to the wild population. The answer can be mixed without making the breeding program a failure.

23. The Rare Trait Is Not the Goal
A visually striking characteristic becomes popular with visitors. Researchers warn that selecting release candidates for that visible trait would confuse tourism preference with population recovery goals.

24. The Old Population Was More Diverse
Archived biological material suggests historic diversity was broader before a known bottleneck. The finding creates a stronger historical record, not a mandate to recreate the past at any cost.

25. The Recolonization Without a Program
A population returns naturally after habitat restoration. Conservation must distinguish recolonization from deliberate reintroduction and avoid crediting an institution for moving Pokémon it never moved.

26. The Protected Population Crosses the Boundary
A recovering group expands into land managed by another institution. Genetic and movement evidence shows continuity with the protected population, creating a governance problem without changing ownership of wild Pokémon.

27. The Sample That Should Stay Private
A study could reveal the exact location of the last known breeding group. Research Ethics and Conservation agree that the detailed dataset should remain restricted while a public summary is released.

28. The Released Partner Question
A formerly partnered Pokémon later appears inside a wild population survey. Its persistent identity is known, but its role in the population is not inferred. Presence does not prove breeding contribution, leadership or ownership.

29. The Two Success Metrics
One institution reports restoration success using abundance and recruitment. Another says long-term genetic monitoring should continue. The disagreement is substantive without requiring either group to be corrupt.

30. The Population Atlas With Versions
A regional atlas stores maps, sample windows, founder histories, known corridors and assessment revisions for decades. Old editions remain accessible so future players can see how knowledge changed.

## Long arc 1 — Five Generations at Glasswater Isles

Year 1: a small island population is considered stable based on abundance.
Year 2: historical museum samples reveal a broader former baseline.
Year 3: a storm and new ferry pattern create plausible immigration routes.
Year 4: repeated monitoring finds evidence of limited new gene flow.
Year 5+: management shifts from preserving isolation to understanding which connections are beneficial, accidental or risky.

The arc can end without a single “correct” genetic target. Its payoff is institutional learning and a richer island history.

## Long arc 2 — The Founder Ledger

A restored valley population has an unusually complete release archive. Over decades, lost records, corrected identities, later immigrants and natural recolonization complicate the neat founder story. The same ledger becomes useful to Conservation, Museums, Taxonomy, Identity, Research Ethics and Public Memory.

The story should never become a eugenics or breeding-optimization game. Its tension comes from evidence quality and management decisions.

## Long arc 3 — The Corridor Years

Several habitats are gradually reconnected by road crossings, riparian restoration and removal of obsolete barriers. Movement is observed first. Demographic effects appear later. Genetic evidence takes longer still. Different species respond at different rates.

The arc demonstrates that infrastructure success can be multi-generational and that a crossing used once is not the same thing as restored connectivity.

## Encounter contract A — Corridor Sampling Day

Narrative premise: researchers need to inspect a restored connection while wild Pokémon are actively moving through it.

FULL version:
Wild actors have CROSS/WITHDRAW objectives, researchers must remain protected, and the corridor must not be blocked by combatants. This requires complete movement/interception/forced movement, objective-aware tactical AI and Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics playback. Protected-zone or dynamic-lane rules also depend on the broader terrain/hazards/zones/reactions family.

REDUCED version:
Resolve the passage wave before combat. Sampling is recorded in world state. If an unrelated threat remains, freeze a static arena away from the corridor and use normal AutoPTU battle resolution. The genetic assessment happens later.

## Encounter contract B — Founder Release Perimeter

Narrative premise: an authorized release group enters restored habitat while an independent disturbance threatens staff or access.

FULL version:
Released Pokémon must reach habitat exits and are not normal hostile combatants. Requires complete movement, AI tactical policy and adapter/playback.

REDUCED version:
Complete the release before battle, remove released Pokémon from the tactical grid and resolve only the independent confrontation. Victory protects the operation; it does not prove establishment, recruitment or genetic success.

## Encounter contract C — Archive Sample Retrieval

Narrative premise: a museum specimen may revise the historical baseline of a population.

FULL version:
If transport is contested, moving custody/escort objectives require complete movement and AI tactical policy.

REDUCED version:
The sample remains outside the grid under Museums/Custody state. AutoPTU resolves only combatants in a static encounter. The sample is assessed afterward through Science/Metrology/Research Ethics.

## Hard non-inferences

Do not derive:
- genetic diversity from spawn count;
- inbreeding from a small group;
- health from abundance;
- population value from rarity;
- purity from isolation;
- superiority from ancestry;
- mechanical stats from genetics;
- new regional forms from a bottleneck;
- capture value from founder status;
- breeding bonuses from diversity;
- ownership from conservation records;
- family relationships from co-location;
- gene flow from one observed migrant;
- management success from one release event.

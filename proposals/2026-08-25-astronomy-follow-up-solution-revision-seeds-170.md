# Astronomy Follow-Up & Solution Revision Seeds — Pass 170

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS.
Authority: extends Pass 63 `astronomy-celestial-observation-layer.md`; does not replace it.

These candidates deliberately avoid repeating Pass 63’s existing observatory, meteor-recovery, dark-sky and basic celestial-event hooks. They focus on follow-up, source dependence, solution revision, observing coverage and long-term institutional memory.

1. **The Candidate With Four Names.** Four observatories independently assign local labels to what later appears to be one transient. Identity reconciliation becomes the story.

2. **One Alert, Forty Reports.** A public alert is reposted widely; forty submissions initially look like corroboration until source-dependency review shows that most derive from one image.

3. **The Recovery Window Closed Early.** A follow-up request is scientifically sound, but the target becomes unobservable sooner than the provisional solution expected. The result remains unresolved until another season.

4. **The Object Was Already in the Archive.** A “new” target is linked to a neglected observation from decades earlier, dramatically extending its observation arc.

5. **The Two-Night Orbit.** A dramatic early prediction is based on only two nights of observations. A later campaign changes the forecast enough to test how institutions communicate uncertainty.

6. **The Observation With the Wrong Site Code.** A valid record is attached to the wrong observatory during data transfer. Its measurement survives; its geometry must be recalculated after provenance is repaired.

7. **The Duplicate Was the Better Record.** Two submissions describe the same underlying exposure, but the supposedly redundant copy preserved metadata lost from the primary archive.

8. **The Missing Follow-Up Was Weather.** Public memory treats a candidate as “ignored,” but archived session records show three observatories attempted follow-up and were clouded out.

9. **The Candidate That Split.** A long-baseline series that once looked like one variable source is better explained as two distinct targets after improved spatial resolution.

10. **The Two Targets That Merged.** Historical catalog entries are reconciled as one object observed under incompatible naming conventions.

11. **The Prediction Changed, the Festival Poster Didn’t.** A public event advertises an old visibility window after the scientific solution is revised. The operational problem is version control, not astronomy failure.

12. **The Quiet Follow-Up.** An urgent campaign produces no dramatic discovery but sharply narrows uncertainty. Scientific success is reduced uncertainty rather than spectacle.

13. **The Amateur Observation Everyone Needed.** A community observer fills a time gap between professional sites. The datum matters because of coverage, not because amateurs are magically superior.

14. **The Flagship Site Was Looking Elsewhere.** A transient occurs inside the regional sky but outside the primary program’s scheduled field. A smaller survey notices it first.

15. **The Clock Correction Moves the Event.** Timekeeping later reconstructs an offset large enough that two observations once thought unrelated become plausible matches.

16. **The Filter Change Broke Comparability.** An instrument upgrade improves current measurements but creates a discontinuity in a long series. Researchers must derive a cross-calibration rather than pretending nothing changed.

17. **The Public Risk Page Without a Risk.** A provisional uncertainty region is misunderstood as a prediction of disaster. Follow-up removes the concerning solutions while preserving the history of the alert.

18. **The Candidate Returned Three Years Later.** A poorly constrained object becomes observable again. The new campaign finally decides whether an old unresolved detection belonged to it.

19. **The Null Observation With Excellent Coverage.** Multiple sites observe the right field at the right time and detect nothing. The null result materially changes the current solution.

20. **The Null Observation With Terrible Coverage.** The same apparent outcome means almost nothing because clouds, horizon and equipment gaps remove most of the opportunity.

21. **The Archive Used the Wrong Epoch.** An old coordinate table appears inconsistent until the reference epoch is reconstructed; the original observations were sound.

22. **The Observer Retired, the Series Continued.** A community observer’s decades-long measurements remain useful after retirement while successors inherit method documentation rather than identity or authority.

23. **The Follow-Up Request Nobody Owned.** Several institutions assume another site will observe a target. The missed window exposes coordination gaps without implying misconduct.

24. **The Observatory That Always Confirmed Itself.** Review reveals that supposedly independent corroborations were processed through the same upstream detector. The network redesigns its validation policy.

25. **The Old Plate Changes a Modern Solution.** A digitized historical plate adds one early position and materially narrows a present-day prediction.

26. **The Better Model Makes Yesterday Less Certain.** A new processing method reveals that an older apparently precise result understated its uncertainty. The decision made at the time remains part of Chronicle.

27. **Minior During Follow-Up.** Falling Minior are observed in the same sky sector as a candidate event. The observations become separate linked records rather than evidence that the candidate is a Pokémon event.

28. **Lunatone Study, Year Five.** Repeated full-moon observations show a local pattern weaker than the first two seasons implied. The project narrows a claim instead of forcing species lore to be universally true.

29. **The Alert That Prevented a Quest.** Better follow-up quickly resolves an alarming candidate as a known object. Competent science removes the need for player intervention.

30. **Nothing Happened During Follow-Up.** The campaign runs normally, the target appears where expected and the solution changes only slightly. Years later the clean series is an important baseline.

## Long arc — Five Solutions of Meridian Candidate 47

A candidate begins with a short, uncertain observation arc. Five solution revisions accumulate over several years as new sites, old plates and community observations extend coverage. Public interpretation changes more dramatically than the physical object. The arc can end with a mundane, well-constrained orbit; the story is institutional learning and uncertainty communication.

## Long arc — The Follow-Up Network

Three observatories and several community sites start with incompatible methods and ad hoc requests. Over a decade they develop source-dependency tracking, standardized coverage records and explicit handoffs without merging into one institution. A later rare event demonstrates that the network now resolves uncertainty faster and generates fewer emergency quests.

## Long arc — The Candidate That Would Not Settle

A recurring faint transient remains unresolved through multiple seasons because of poor visibility and sparse coverage. New evidence alternately favors an artifact, known source and distinct target. The campaign may legitimately end with `UNRESOLVED` while still producing valuable infrastructure, archives and research relationships.

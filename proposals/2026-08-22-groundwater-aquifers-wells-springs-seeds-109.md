## 30 candidate seeds

### 1. The Spring That Arrives Late
A hillside spring normally strengthens shortly after the wet season. This year the rain was ordinary, but the spring responds weeks later than the historical record suggests. The investigation can compare recharge lag, new construction, pumping, sensor error, and a changed subsurface pathway.

### 2. Two Wells, One Summer
A settlement well remains stable while an orchard well falls sharply. Both were assumed to use the same groundwater body. The useful question is whether that assumption was ever verified.

### 3. The New Well That Changed the Old One
A newly commissioned institutional well works exactly as designed. Months later, a nearby older well recovers more slowly after pumping. No actor needs to be malicious; the conflict comes from a newly visible connection.

### 4. The Dry Spring With Wet Ground
A culturally important spring stops producing a visible channel, but vegetation below the site remains unusually green. The water may have shifted outlet, moved underground, or be supported by another source.

### 5. The Monitoring Gap
A groundwater network has seven years of records except for a six-month gap around a major redevelopment project. The gap may be mundane equipment failure, poor staffing, or something more significant. Missing data is not evidence of wrongdoing.

### 6. The Well That Tastes Different
Residents report that a familiar well tastes or smells different. The system records the reports without deciding whether chemistry changed. Sampling may reveal a natural mineral shift, infrastructure issue, recharge change, contamination, or no measurable change.

### 7. Rain in the Wrong Place
Heavy rain falls over a town, but the aquifer that supplies it primarily recharges farther upslope. Public discussion initially treats the local storm as the end of the shortage.

### 8. The Old Recharge Map
An archive contains a decades-old map showing the recharge area for a municipal well field. New pumping and new monitoring suggest the contributing area has changed. The old map was not necessarily wrong when it was made.

### 9. The Spring Below the Burn Scar
Years after a wildfire, a spring begins carrying different sediment or chemistry signals during the first strong rains. Wildfire, Soil, Freshwater, and Groundwater all contribute evidence without any one layer owning the full explanation.

### 10. The Cave Pool That Is Not the Aquifer
Explorers discover a large underground pool beneath a drought-affected district. Public enthusiasm immediately treats it as a new regional water supply. Geological and hydrological work later shows the relationship is more limited or uncertain.

### 11. Three Springs, Three Causes
Three springs weaken during the same dry year. One responds to drought, one to a nearby pumping change, and one to a collapsed old diversion. Similar symptoms should not force a shared cause.

### 12. The Orchard Above the Aquifer
An orchard has been productive for generations. A new groundwater model suggests part of the regional recharge zone lies beneath it. That fact alone creates no automatic land restriction, ownership claim, or blame.

### 13. The Well Nobody Uses Anymore
A closed public well still has a functioning monitoring tube and decades of records. It becomes scientifically valuable after its original service role ends.

### 14. The New Spring After the Road Cut
A road project exposes a seep that was not previously visible. The water could represent a newly opened pathway rather than a newly created resource.

### 15. The Pump That Is Innocent
A pump fails on the same week groundwater levels decline. Repairs restore the pump but not the water level, revealing two separate problems that happened together.

### 16. The Recharge Basin Wildlife Window
A managed recharge basin operates only during certain periods. Wild Pokémon begin using it when water is present. Operations, habitat value, and safety become linked without turning the basin into permanent ownership of those Pokémon.

### 17. The Sample That Arrives First
A laboratory receives an unusual groundwater sample before the field team’s full notes and chain-of-custody record arrive. The result is real but cannot yet support a strong regional claim.

### 18. The Well Field Under the Festival
A recurring festival temporarily raises local water demand. The institution has managed this for years, but a particularly dry season exposes how much the event depends on groundwater storage and emergency reserves.

### 19. The Salt That Came Underground
A coastal settlement sees increasing salinity in a well while the nearby surface channel looks normal. Estuaries and Groundwater must compare coastal intrusion, pumping, geology, and sampling history.

### 20. The Spring That Became a Landmark
A spring begins as an ordinary water source, then gains a shelter, path, memorial plaque, monitoring station, and eventually visitor pressure. Its physical and cultural histories remain separate.

### 21. The Water Under the Station
Rail reconstruction discovers old well infrastructure beneath a station district. It may reveal a former supply system, abandoned monitoring point, or subsurface pathway relevant to current drainage.

### 22. The Recharge Project That Worked Differently
A pilot project raises groundwater levels in its target zone, but recovered water chemistry differs from expectations. “Successful recharge” and “successful usable supply” remain separate findings.

### 23. The Spring the Pokémon Stopped Visiting
A spring still flows, but a previously observed wild population no longer uses it. The change triggers ecology work without assuming the water is contaminated or the population is gone.

### 24. Former Partner at the Monitoring Well
A released former partner is photographed near a remote monitoring site during several surveys. The same Pokémon may be using the location repeatedly, but the system does not invent a motive or restored ownership.

### 25. The Household Well Archive
A family has hand-written well-depth and pump-repair records spanning several generations. The records are valuable observations even though they were not collected as a formal scientific program.

### 26. The Public Map Shows One Aquifer
A simplified educational map shows one regional aquifer. Later work identifies several linked units with different behavior. The simple map can remain useful for public orientation while becoming scientifically outdated.

### 27. The Wetland That Depends on Below
A wetland remains connected longer into the dry season than rainfall alone explains. Investigation gradually establishes a groundwater contribution, linking Freshwater and Conservation.

### 28. The Industrial Site With a Long Delay
A historical facility closed decades ago. A new monitoring network identifies a groundwater-quality question whose travel time may connect to that older land use. The old site becomes relevant without automatically proving liability or intentional dumping.

### 29. The Emergency Well
A seldom-used emergency well becomes important after a surface-water outage. Its old maintenance, quality, access, and capacity records suddenly matter. Emergency designation does not guarantee readiness.

### 30. The Aquifer Atlas
Several institutions build a versioned regional atlas of wells, springs, recharge areas, historical levels, uncertain flow paths, old maps, and revised interpretations. Every new edition preserves what was known at the time.

## Long arc A — Five Dry Years at Highplain Basin

Year 1: a dry summer lowers several shallow wells, but the main public supply remains stable.

Year 2: a new monitoring network shows that different districts respond at different rates.

Year 3: an agricultural well expansion becomes a plausible contributor to local drawdown, but drought and geology remain competing explanations.

Year 4: an unusually wet winter produces recharge, but the response is delayed and uneven.

Year 5: institutions revise the basin model and adopt a more resilient mix of surface water, groundwater, reserves, and monitoring.

The arc works even if nobody acted wrongly. Its payoff is improved understanding, institutional memory, and a world that behaves differently because earlier observations mattered.

## Long arc B — The Spring Road

A chain of settlements grew around springs along an old route. Modern transport reduced reliance on the road, but the springs remain ecological, cultural, archival, and emergency assets.

Across several years:
- one spring weakens;
- one is found to be fed by a different aquifer than assumed;
- one becomes a visitor destination;
- one gains a monitoring station;
- one old route reopens during a crisis;
- archived oral/local records help reconstruct which springs were historically perennial versus seasonal.

The arc links Travel, Public Memory, Archives, Groundwater, Conservation, Tourism, and Land Tenure.

## Long arc C — What Moved Underground

A groundwater-quality signal appears near a former industrial district. The campaign does not begin with a culprit.

Phase 1: sparse monitoring establishes a real signal but uncertain extent.

Phase 2: old infrastructure maps, geology, stormwater records, and historical land uses produce several plausible pathways.

Phase 3: new wells narrow the flow model; some early assumptions are discarded.

Phase 4: a delayed downstream detection changes the estimated travel-time model.

Phase 5: response focuses on actual current risks, source control where evidence supports it, and long-term monitoring.

The story can produce institutional conflict, public pressure, scientific disagreement, and remediation without requiring sabotage or conspiracy.

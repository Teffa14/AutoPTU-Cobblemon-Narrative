# Botanical Gardens, Living Collections & Seed Banks — Ouros Seeds — Pass 152

Status: NON-CANON CANDIDATES. Nothing below becomes established lore without explicit approval.
Date: 2026-08-24

These concepts assume `design/botanical-gardens-living-collections-seed-banks-layer.md` as the proposed state model and preserve the authority boundaries of Flora, Conservation, Biosecurity, Taxonomy, Museums, Science, Technology, Supply Chains and Pokémon Agency.

## Thirty candidate hooks

1. The Accession With No Provenance — A famous old plant predates modern recordkeeping. Three labels survive, each suggesting a different origin. The plant is real; its source remains unresolved.

2. The Glasshouse That Changed Climate — A greenhouse was built for one collection decades ago. Later retrofits changed humidity and light enough that the same accessions now need different care. The institution must decide whether to preserve the old environment, move specimens or revise the collection purpose.

3. The Duplicate That Wasn't — Two gardens believe they hold duplicates of the same accession. Taxonomy and provenance review reveal that the records diverged long ago. Neither institution acted improperly.

4. Seed Bank Year Twelve — A stored lot reaches a scheduled viability review. The result is lower than the previous test, but there is no crisis until curators compare duplicate holdings and restoration plans.

5. The Living Collection Outlived the Label — A specimen has survived several taxonomic revisions. Visitors know the old name better than the current one.

6. The Garden That Became Habitat — A research garden gradually becomes important to urban wildlife. Removing an obsolete collection bed now has ecological consequences that did not exist when it was planted.

7. One Plant, Four Institutions — A wild-origin accession was propagated over decades and now exists in four gardens. Each institution has a different local history, condition and public meaning.

8. The Rare Plant Nobody May Propagate Yet — Curators want a backup specimen, but provenance restrictions and uncertain authorization require review before any material is moved.

9. The Wild-Origin Claim Under Review — A specimen has always been advertised as wild-collected. A newly digitized notebook suggests it may have come from another garden instead.

10. The Greenhouse Worker Remembers a Different Label — A retired horticulturist recalls that two benches were swapped during an old renovation. Their memory is useful evidence, not automatic proof.

11. The Comfey Flower Row — A persistent Comfey repeatedly visits one bed and changes which flowers it carries over the season. The garden records the behavior without treating those flowers as medicinal inventory.

12. The Florges Garden Became Famous — A wild Florges establishes a garden near an institutional boundary. Visitors begin attributing healing properties to the site. Staff must separate folklore, public access and ecological observation.

13. Eldegoss Over the Seed House — Eldegoss dispersal is observed across a collection perimeter. New seedlings appear later. Their source cannot be assigned from appearance alone.

14. The Emergency Backup Collection — A storm damages one institution. A duplicate holding elsewhere prevents total loss, but the surviving material has a different propagation history and cannot simply replace every former specimen one-for-one.

15. The Pest-Free House Is Not Empty — A greenhouse has fewer obvious pests after an intervention, but beneficial visitors also changed. IPM and living-collection staff review the whole system rather than declaring victory from one observation.

16. The Storm Breaks One Glasshouse — Most accessions survive because staff used an old emergency procedure written after a previous storm. Chronicle makes routine institutional memory consequential.

17. The Seed Bank Alarm — A storage alarm triggers. The technical system returns to normal quickly; the actual question is which lots experienced meaningful condition changes and which did not.

18. The Accession That Germinates After Thirty Years — A small historical seed sample produces a living specimen after decades. That success does not prove the remaining lot has the same viability.

19. The Lost Duplicate — A partner institution closes. The records show an accession was once duplicated there, but current custody is unknown.

20. The Garden Map With Old Beds — Visitors use a beautiful historical plan whose paths and collections no longer match the current garden. Both maps remain useful for different questions.

21. Public Display, Research Collection — The most scientifically important plants are kept off the visitor route while an ordinary but visually dramatic bed becomes the public symbol of the institution.

22. The Collection That Cannot Be Moved — A very old specimen is tied to a site physically, historically and ecologically. A building project cannot treat relocation as a normal inventory transfer.

23. The Wrong Locality Tag — A typo copied through several databases makes one accession appear to come from a different valley. The original field record survives and allows correction without erasing the bad historical versions.

24. The Garden After Wildfire — A regional fire never reaches the garden, but the institution suddenly becomes more important as a backup for plant material from affected landscapes.

25. The Greenhouse Water Restriction — Drought forces an institution to prioritize water use. Not every collection can receive its normal regime, and curatorial purpose becomes part of the decision.

26. The Garden Stops Being a Garden — A research plot is intentionally closed after its objective ends. Some living material is transferred, some remains as ordinary landscape and some is preserved as vouchers. Closure is not failure.

27. The Visitor Pressure Season — A rare flowering event becomes popular online. Tourism pressure rises faster than the accession's biological condition changes.

28. The Living Specimen Becomes an Archive Trail — A historically important plant dies. Its accession record remains linked to photographs, labels, seed descendants, a preserved voucher and oral histories.

29. The Propagation That Failed — A careful propagation attempt produces nothing. The institution records the result and changes no world-state claim beyond that attempt.

30. Nothing Happened in House Three — A greenhouse has an entirely normal year. Its stable observations later become the baseline that makes a real change detectable.

## Long arc — Five Generations at Meridian Conservatory

Year 1: Meridian Conservatory reviews an old collection with uneven provenance and identifies a few high-value accessions with no duplicate holdings.

Year 2: Staff create backups for material that can legally and practically be propagated. One attempt fails; another succeeds at a partner institution.

Year 3: A utility disruption tests the emergency plan. Most specimens are unaffected, but one historic accession declines later and the cause remains uncertain.

Year 4: Taxonomy revises part of the collection. Public labels, research records and old photographs now use different names without becoming invalid.

Year 5+: The garden changes its collection strategy. Some beds become public education areas, some research material moves off display and a former greenhouse becomes habitat/heritage space.

The arc can continue across decades without a central villain. Institutional learning, staff turnover, climate, public memory and Pokémon observations provide the continuity.

## Long arc — The Seed That Waited Thirty Years

A seed lot collected before the campaign remains in storage with strong provenance but limited material.

A later viability test produces a small number of seedlings. One remains in the institution; one becomes a duplicate holding elsewhere. Conservation considers future restoration use but does not assume success.

Years later, descendants may be used in research or restoration only after the relevant handoffs. The original lot can eventually be depleted while the accession history remains complete.

The story is about provenance, uncertainty, patience and institutional continuity rather than a magical seed awakening.

## Long arc — Three Gardens, One Population

Three institutions hold material derived from the same historical wild population.

One prioritizes research, one public education and one conservation backup. Their local specimens accumulate different histories.

A regional disturbance later makes the network important. The institutions compare provenance, viability and duplicate holdings before deciding what material can support restoration.

No garden becomes the single owner of the wild population. Conservation/Flora remains authoritative for field outcomes.

## Non-combat — Accession Provenance Review

A historic accession has conflicting source labels.

Players may inspect collection records, old maps, photographs, oral histories and taxonomic revisions. Valid outcomes include `CONFIRMED`, `NARROWED_UNCERTAIN`, `MULTIPLE_PLAUSIBLE`, or `UNRESOLVED`.

The accession may remain valuable even if provenance is never fully reconstructed.

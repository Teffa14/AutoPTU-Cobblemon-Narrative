# Ouros Land Tenure, Boundaries, Commons & Access Seeds — Pass 108

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Every entry below is an original Ouros candidate. None establishes regional law, ownership, inheritance, criminal status or PTU mechanics.

## Design constraints

These candidates use the Pass 108 land layer as world-state structure.

They must preserve:
- physical place identity;
- boundary revision history;
- scoped permissions;
- wild Pokémon agency;
- evidence/claim separation;
- player consent for irreversible shared-space changes;
- PTU/Caelo authority over mechanics.

## 30 original candidates

### 1. The Fence That Was Never the Boundary

A working orchard has used the same old fence for decades. A new survey finds that the archived boundary description follows an older creek bed several meters away.

Hooks:
- two map editions can both be historically valid;
- no actor needs to be lying;
- a public walking path may have been using the in-between strip for years;
- the solution can preserve practical use without deciding universal ownership.

### 2. The Shortcut Everyone Assumed Was Public

A trail between two neighborhoods crosses the edge of a workshop yard. Generations of residents use it daily, but no current record explains why.

The interesting question is not “who owns it?” first. It is whether there is an active passage arrangement, a forgotten agreement, a changed boundary or simply tolerated use.

### 3. Three Rights, One Meadow

The same meadow is used by:
- a managed herd during part of the year;
- a community festival for one week;
- researchers monitoring a flowering window.

A seasonal shift makes all three uses overlap for the first time.

### 4. The Orchard After the Diglett

A farm begins receiving displaced Diglett after construction changes a nearby hillside. The farmer has a valid agricultural use; the Pokémon have a traceable ecological reason for being there.

The arc can lead to habitat restoration, fencing changes, crop adaptation or a new corridor instead of an extermination quest.

### 5. The Boundary Marker in the Museum

A museum realizes that one of its old carved stones may once have served as a field marker. Returning it would alter an exhibit, but leaving it in the museum may keep a useful survey clue out of place.

The object’s provenance and the land claim stay separate.

### 6. The River Moved, the Maps Did Not

Two farms share an old boundary description based on a river channel. The river shifted after a flood years ago.

The physical watercourse, historic description, current use and modern survey can all disagree legitimately.

### 7. The Gate Is Locked, the Permission Still Exists

A utility team has authored emergency access across a field, but the physical gate has not been used in years and is jammed shut.

Minecraft obstruction must not erase the underlying permission.

### 8. The Gate Is Open, the Permission Does Not Exist

The inverse case. A damaged fence leaves a private-use orchard open after a storm. Physical entry is possible; authorization has not changed.

### 9. The Common Orchard

Several households maintain old fruit trees together. No single household controls every tree, and different groups handle pruning, harvest, irrigation and path maintenance.

The shared-use relationship predates current residents.

### 10. The Last Grazing Week

A pasture arrangement ends on a known seasonal date. A delayed migration and route closure cause managed herds and wild Pokémon to arrive at the waterpoint at the same time during the final week.

The expiry itself is not betrayal or eviction drama; it is a predictable world-state transition.

### 11. The Old Road Under the New Farm

An abandoned road remains visible as a line through fields. Archives show it was once public transit, but the modern route moved decades ago.

A crisis raises the question of whether the old alignment can be reopened temporarily.

### 12. The Shared Well

A well sits physically inside one land unit but has been used by several nearby households and workers for generations.

A repair project forces the world to distinguish physical location from use access and maintenance responsibility.

### 13. The Spring Nobody Owns

Several actors claim responsibility for maintaining a spring, but none has a verified transfer or ownership claim. The spring feeds a public trough and a wetland.

The world can function through stewardship and use agreements without first solving universal title.

### 14. The Research Plot Inside the Working Forest

A long-running study plot sits inside a forest scheduled for a treatment project. Forestry and Science both have legitimate planned uses.

The conflict becomes scheduling, buffer design and data continuity rather than “researchers versus loggers.”

### 15. The Public Stairs Through a Private Courtyard

A hillside settlement grew around an old stairway. The lower landing is now inside a residence courtyard while the stairs remain the safest route for older residents.

Accessibility, Homes and Travel all intersect.

### 16. The Nesting Edge

A farm boundary is stable, but a nesting site expands across one corner for the first time in recorded history.

Conservation may propose a temporary buffer without changing the underlying tenure relationship.

### 17. The Utility Line No One Put on the New Map

A redevelopment project discovers an old service corridor still supplying a relay. The current parcel map omitted it because the corridor was never digitized.

The map is incomplete; the infrastructure is real.

### 18. The Farmhouse Without the Field

A family continues living in an old farmhouse after agricultural operations move elsewhere. Residence, workplace and production land separate over time.

This creates demographic and architectural history without requiring a property dispute.

### 19. The Field Without the Farmer

A seasonal farming cooperative maintains a field while no member lives on site. The settlement mistakenly treats “nobody lives there” as “unused.”

### 20. The Path That Became Habitat

A former passage corridor closes after a new bridge opens. Over several years vegetation returns and wild Pokémon begin using it.

A later infrastructure proposal must decide whether reopening the old corridor is still low-impact.

### 21. The Temporary Market That Stayed

A market receives a six-week event-use grant after a fire damages its normal venue. Years later, vendors still return to the temporary square because customers prefer it.

Repeated use does not silently create permanent authority.

### 22. The Surveyor Was Right in 1986

A famous map is mocked as inaccurate until archives show the shoreline genuinely had a different shape when surveyed.

The map becomes valuable historical evidence rather than a mistake.

### 23. The Former Quarry Commons

A closed quarry gradually becomes a shared walking, climbing-observation and wildlife area. Different groups use different sections, but no universal access policy has ever been authored.

This is a governance gap, not permission to invent a legal regime.

### 24. The Workshop Garden

A small strip beside a repair workshop is cultivated by employees during breaks. The workshop operator allows it, but the garden is not part of anyone’s residence.

When the workshop changes hands, the continuation of the garden needs an explicit decision.

### 25. The Pokémon-Made Dam Crosses the Line

A persistent Bibarel-built dam spans two human land units and changes water reaching a third location.

The structure’s builder attribution, ecological function, water consequences and human use relationships remain separate.

### 26. The Parcel That Exists Only in Records

An old administrative map contains a tiny enclosed land unit that nobody can locate on the ground. Newer maps omit it without explanation.

Possible explanations include survey error, consolidation, changed landmarks or a lost revision. No fraud is assumed.

### 27. The Accessible Route Is Longer

A public route crosses a hill using stairs. A new accessible path needs a gentle alignment across several existing use zones.

The public-works problem is to negotiate scoped passage and construction use, not simply overwrite every affected relationship.

### 28. The Released Partner at the Boundary Pond

A previously released Pokémon is observed repeatedly at a pond beside a farm boundary.

Its presence does not prove it has returned to its former Trainer, belongs to the farmer, leads the local wild population or is asking to be recaptured.

### 29. The Emergency Shelter Field

A field normally used for seasonal agriculture is designated for temporary shelter during a flood under an authored emergency arrangement.

When the crisis ends, shelter use expires. Cleanup, soil condition and crop timing remain real consequences.

### 30. The Boundary Atlas

A regional archive begins preserving every verified boundary revision instead of only the newest map.

Decades later it becomes essential for:
- river migration studies;
- old route reconstruction;
- settlement history;
- archaeology;
- public works;
- dispute resolution;
- understanding why neighboring families remember the same field differently.

## Long arc 1 — Five Uses of Alder Field

Year 1: Alder Field is primarily an orchard with a public passage on its northern edge.

Year 2: a pollinator survey begins during spring flowering.

Year 3: storm damage closes the normal road and a temporary utility/service corridor crosses the field.

Year 4: a nesting population starts using the unused southern edge, prompting a seasonal buffer.

Year 5: the orchard operation relocates, but residents want to keep the walking route, researchers want continuity and the nesting edge has become ecologically significant.

The arc asks what a place becomes when successive valid uses accumulate. It does not need an ownership reveal or villain.

## Long arc 2 — The River Boundary Years

A river forms the historical reference for multiple farms and a public path.

Over several years:
1. a flood changes the active channel;
2. old maps disagree with new surveys;
3. a wetland develops on the abandoned channel;
4. a bridge moves the walking route;
5. groundwater access remains tied to infrastructure on the old side;
6. a future public-works proposal must use the full history rather than one current line.

The conclusion may be a negotiated patchwork of passage, maintenance and stewardship relationships instead of one clean border.

## Long arc 3 — The Commons That Had No Charter

A hillside meadow has been used for generations by residents for gatherings, seasonal grazing, berry gathering and access to a spring. No surviving institution can point to one founding document.

The campaign gradually reconstructs practice from:
- oral histories;
- photographs;
- old schedules;
- maintenance records;
- archived maps;
- ecological evidence;
- current users.

The final result can be an authored modern agreement that preserves several uses without pretending historical uncertainty was solved perfectly.

## Guardrails for future writers

Do not use these seeds to establish:
- a region-wide property code;
- automatic inheritance;
- adverse possession;
- taxation;
- mortgages;
- eviction procedures;
- eminent domain;
- police authority;
- criminal trespass;
- ownership of wild Pokémon;
- ownership through block placement;
- resource ownership from mere proximity;
- permanent rights from repeated use without authored authority;
- PTU bonuses from land status.

Any of those concepts require separate canon review if Ouros ever needs them.

# Pokémon Shelter, Sanctuary & Placement Seeds — Pass 94

Status: NON-CANON OUROS CANDIDATES. These concepts are proposals only.

Date: 2026-08-28

These seeds assume the proposed shelter/sanctuary continuity extension. They do not establish that Ouros has any specific shelter, adoption law, ownership system or sanctuary network.

## The Intake Record With No Arrival

A shelter has a complete intake form for a Pokémon that nobody currently remembers arriving. The identifier matches a later transfer record, but the timestamp predates the facility's current intake procedure.

Possible explanations include a migrated legacy record, an intake performed at a temporary site, a clerical duplication, or two individuals being confused. The story should remain evidence-driven and may end with uncertainty.

## The Resident Everyone Thinks Is Available

A long-term sanctuary resident is well known to visitors. Repeated public familiarity has created the assumption that the Pokémon is waiting for a Trainer.

The actual shelter record says no such thing. The conflict is about public expectation, privacy and program communication, not about making the Pokémon a reward.

## The Foster Return That Was Planned

A temporary placement ends on its scheduled review date and the Pokémon returns to the shelter. Other residents interpret the return as a failed adoption.

The shelter's own records show that the arrangement was always temporary. The playable thread is correcting an inaccurate public story while preserving private details.

## The Previous Caretaker Appears After Placement Review Begins

A prospective new placement is already under review when a person arrives with evidence of a prior legitimate association.

The shelter pauses the transition. Players may help verify provenance, timelines and identity, but they cannot settle ownership merely by choosing which NPC they like more.

## The Quiet Wing Is Full, the Shelter Is Not

Overall occupancy appears manageable, yet intake is restricted because the only suitable quiet-care spaces are occupied.

The operational issue may be solved through referral, temporary reconfiguration, staffing or another facility. A raw total-capacity number would miss the real constraint.

## The Transfer That Changes Nothing About Ownership

A resident moves to a specialist rehabilitation facility for several weeks. Public rumor describes the move as an adoption or permanent transfer.

The actual change concerns residence and custody scope only. The case is useful for teaching the world that physical location, care responsibility and ownership claim are separate facts.

## The Release Candidate That Stays

A Pokémon passes one review for release, but later habitat evidence changes the suitability of the intended site. The shelter keeps the Pokémon temporarily while Conservation reassesses options.

This does not mean rehabilitation failed. The environment changed.

## The Released Resident Returns to the Gate

A previously released Pokémon appears again near the facility months later.

The observation creates questions rather than answers. It may be using a familiar route, seeking a resource, passing through seasonally, responding to another Pokémon, or returning for an authored reason. The system should record the return without declaring regret, attachment or a desire for recapture.

## The Prospective Household Changes Schedule

A placement looked practical when review began. Then the candidate's work or travel schedule changes substantially.

The placement can pause, shrink to foster, move to another candidate, or remain viable after a different support plan. Nobody needs to become a villain for the thread to matter.

## The Shelter That Refers Almost Everything

A small local program has excellent intake and community knowledge but limited specialist capacity. Its strength is its referral network.

Players initially assume the facility is under-resourced because residents frequently leave. Over time they learn that deliberate transfer is the institution's operating model.

## The Sanctuary Resident Becomes a Local Landmark

A long-term resident has lived at the same sanctuary for years and appears in photographs, visitor memories and local stories.

Later construction, tourism growth or a facility redesign must account for the resident's established routine without turning the Pokémon into a mascot job automatically.

## The Public Board Is Too Detailed

A volunteer updates the shelter's public board with more case history than policy allows. Nothing malicious occurred, but private intake information is now visible.

The response can involve correction, documentation review and better separation between public status and restricted case files.

## The Handoff Kit Belongs to the Wrong Case

A carrier, blanket or documented belonging prepared for one transfer is found with another resident's paperwork.

The item itself does not identify the Pokémon. Resolving the mismatch requires provenance before either handoff proceeds.

## The Resident With Two Names

An intake name, a previous caretaker's name and a wildlife-monitoring identifier may all refer to the same Pokémon.

The shelter must preserve aliases without treating one label as a different entity or assuming a claimant is correct merely because they know one name.

## The Emergency Intake Becomes a Long Stay

A crisis sends several Pokémon into temporary shelter residence. Most leave quickly through reunification or release. One cannot use the original transition route when the crisis ends.

That individual remains part of the institution's history and may eventually become a long-term sanctuary resident, transfer elsewhere or follow a later reviewed path.

## The Old Resident Returns With Someone Else

A former resident visits the facility alongside a new legitimate caretaker or institution.

Staff remember the Pokémon. The system records the callback and current association separately. Historical care does not grant staff or prior volunteers battle-command authority.

## Mystery — Three Intake Names, Two Individuals

Three archival intake entries appear to describe three different Pokémon.

Evidence sources:

- one blurred intake photograph;
- a distinctive marking recorded inconsistently;
- two transfer timestamps;
- staff testimony years later;
- one identifier that was reassigned or rewritten;
- a later wildlife-monitoring sighting.

Possible resolution:

Two entries may describe the same individual at different points in its history. The third may be genuinely separate. Evidence can also remain insufficient.

The mystery should never generate a hidden correct answer and then rewrite physical identity to match it.

## Mystery — Four Placements, Three Actual Moves

A public history claims a Pokémon was moved four times. Facility records show four placement events, but one may be a trial extension, a custody-scope change without physical relocation, or a corrected duplicate.

The investigation teaches that administrative transition and geographic movement are not synonymous.

## Long arc — A Shelter Learns Its Transitions

The same institution remains playable over an extended campaign.

Early visits establish ordinary routines: intake, feeding, care referrals, meetings, foster handoffs and quiet long-term residents.

A later ambiguous intake exposes weaknesses in identity and claim tracking. The shelter changes one procedure rather than receiving a generic upgrade.

A placement then pauses for a mundane but meaningful reason. Players see that returning to shelter residence can be responsible rather than shameful.

A regional incident creates a capacity spike. The facility uses referral partners, temporary zones and transport rather than disposing of residents to clear space.

Months later, a former resident returns through a re-sighting, care referral, visit or new association. Earlier records now matter again.

The final state is a shelter with deeper institutional memory, clearer boundaries and visible procedural changes. There is no `shelter_level` and no requirement that every resident be placed.

## Encounter candidate — Shelter Yard Withdrawal

Narrative premise:

A separate external disturbance reaches the edge of an outdoor shelter yard while staff are moving residents into safer areas.

Full version:

Staff withdraw through multiple routes while explicitly selected combatants hold access. Intercept and forced movement can matter. Tactical zones or gates may matter if the engine supports them. Opponent AI can prioritize route pressure rather than pure KO.

Required permanent families:

- targeting/footprints/range/LoS — VERIFIED;
- base movement legality — VERIFIED;
- complete movement including push/pull/knockback/interception/forced movement — PARTIAL;
- core calculations — VERIFIED;
- action economy/initiative — VERIFIED;
- full turn/round lifecycle — PARTIAL;
- full stateful damage pipeline — PARTIAL;
- status lifecycle — PARTIAL;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions — BLOCKING;
- move-specific behavior — PARTIAL;
- abilities — PARTIAL;
- items — PARTIAL;
- Trainer Features/perks — PARTIAL;
- AI legal-action infrastructure — VERIFIED;
- AI tactical policy — BLOCKING;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback support — BLOCKING.

Reduced version:

Staff and resident Pokémon complete evacuation staging outside tactical resolution. Shelter residents remain outside the grid unless Ouros deliberately selects one as a combatant under valid mechanics. AutoPTU runs a static legal encounter at the outer approach. Gates and yard props remain non-targetable scenery. After battle, Shelter/Care systems decide whether outdoor use resumes.

## Encounter candidate — Transfer Handoff Interruption

Narrative premise:

A legitimate inter-facility transfer is interrupted by an unrelated route conflict.

Full version:

The scene can eventually support WITHDRAW/CLEAR_ROUTE or PROTECT-like objectives, a moving transfer party, Intercept/reaction behavior and objective-aware AI.

Reduced version:

The placement subject and staff never enter the tactical grid. Custody remains with the current institution. Players clear a static obstacle/encounter, then world state decides whether the handoff continues or is rescheduled.

No battle outcome directly changes ownership or placement status.

## Encounter candidate — Release-Site Boundary Conflict

Narrative premise:

A reviewed release reaches the last operational step, but the access area is occupied by another disturbance.

Full version:

The release target remains outside ordinary targeting. CLEAR_ROUTE/WITHDRAW, territory-aware AI, possible zones and Intercept can matter.

Reduced version:

Release waits. The Pokémon remains outside combat. Ouros selects only the actual opposing/defending participants. AutoPTU resolves a static battle. Conservation then reassesses whether the site and timing are still appropriate.

Victory cannot perform the release automatically.

## Noncombat candidate — Placement File Reconciliation

A case has contradictory residence, foster and transfer dates. Players reconstruct the sequence through records, staff interviews, transport logs and photographs.

The payoff is a cleaner provenance chain and potentially a corrected current decision. No battle is necessary, and the system can retain unresolved gaps.

## Canon questions deliberately left open

- Do shelters or sanctuaries exist in Ouros?
- Are they municipal, private, charitable, community-run, Ranger-linked or something else?
- What terms does Ouros use for surrender, foster, placement and sanctuary residence?
- What evidence establishes prior legitimate custody or ownership?
- Can a shelter refuse or delay intake, and under what policy?
- Does a formal foster practice exist?
- Who authorizes release or relocation?
- What case information can prospective caretakers see?
- Can institutions ever permanently own Pokémon, or is another legal concept used?
- How are disputed claims handled?
- What services are free, funded or paid?
- What happens when a facility closes permanently?

No seed in this file answers those questions by default.

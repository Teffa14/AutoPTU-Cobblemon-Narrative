# Ouros Retail Markets, Auctions & Merchant Networks — Seeds — Pass 106

Status: NON-CANON proposals. These are original candidates for review, not established Ouros lore.

Mechanical guardrail: none of these seeds defines prices, item effects, Pokémon ownership rules, bargaining DCs, merchant Features, carrying rules or battle-object mechanics. Any exact mechanic must come from PTU/Caelo plus implemented AutoPTU behavior.

## 1. The Lot No One Can Value

A regional auction house announces an old instrument with strong provenance but no clear functional purpose. Three institutions want it for different reasons: preservation, technical study and public display.

The interesting question is not who has the most money. It is whether the item should be sold before its condition and stewardship obligations are understood.

Possible outputs:

- auction paused;
- museum loan instead of sale;
- technical study agreement;
- provenance review;
- later relisting with better information.

## 2. Saturday Harbor Market

A harbor hosts a weekly market fed by ferries, small fishing boats, farms and traveling merchants. The same place feels different every week because route state changes the vendor roster.

One week a storm removes the island sellers. Another week a rail connection brings a specialist for the first time. Months later the harbor market becomes a useful record of regional connectivity.

## 3. Three Sellers, One Item Name

Three merchants advertise what looks like the same mechanical item.

Their physical instances have different histories:

- one is ordinary new stock;
- one is repaired secondhand stock;
- one was commissioned years ago and returned for resale.

Mechanical behavior remains identical unless the authoritative item system says otherwise. The difference is provenance, condition and story.

## 4. The Shop That Never Has It

Residents insist a specialist shop “never has” a certain component.

Supply-chain evidence shows the region produces enough. The actual problem is that almost every batch is reserved by hospitals and utilities before it reaches retail.

The shopkeeper is not incompetent. The manufacturer is not necessarily withholding stock. The shortage is an allocation pattern.

## 5. The Vendor Who Arrives at Dusk

A traveling seller appears only on evenings when a particular ferry connection works. Players initially treat the schedule as eccentricity.

Later they discover the timetable, tides and onward route make the pattern operationally rational.

This can become a reliable recurring NPC without requiring a fixed shop.

## 6. Market Closed, Town Still Functions

A famous market hall closes for repairs.

Commerce redistributes temporarily into:

- neighborhood shops;
- a school courtyard on weekends;
- harbor stalls;
- delivery orders;
- a smaller outdoor market.

The closure changes daily life without implying economic collapse.

## 7. The Auction Caption Is Wrong

An auction catalog describes an object as belonging to a famous expedition.

The item is authentic. The expedition attribution is weak.

The auctioneer corrects the catalog before bidding rather than hiding the uncertainty. The correction can reduce interest, increase scholarly interest or leave the sale unchanged.

## 8. The Old Stall Returns

A stall that disappeared years ago reopens under a former apprentice.

The visual identity is familiar, but the inventory and service philosophy are different. Older residents still use the previous owner’s name for the stall.

The new operator may embrace, revise or reject that continuity.

## 9. Two Generations of the Same Counter

A small shop counter is preserved through multiple building renovations.

Its scratches, repair patches and maker marks become a mundane historical object. Decades of transactions occurred there, but the counter has no mechanical bonus.

A later museum request creates a choice between continued everyday use and preservation.

## 10. The Listing Did Not Update

A market board still advertises an item that was put on quality hold several hours earlier.

Customers accuse the vendor of bait-and-switch. The actual cause is an information-sync failure between warehouse and market display.

The story can resolve through correction and process improvement without fraud.

## 11. The Seller Left, the Offer Remained

A digital or physical listing remains active after a merchant has already departed for another settlement.

The object still exists and belongs to the merchant. It is simply no longer at the advertised pickup point.

This creates a small information/logistics problem rather than a theft mystery.

## 12. Institutional Buyer

A clinic, archive or observatory competes at a public auction for a mundane but difficult-to-source object.

Other bidders may be collectors or businesses. No bidder is automatically selfish or virtuous.

If the institution loses, it must find another solution instead of receiving a hidden story discount.

## 13. The Merchant Road Becomes Rail

A merchant has followed the same multi-town route for years.

A new rail line makes part of the route faster but skips two small settlements. The merchant can:

- use rail and stop visiting them;
- split the route;
- coordinate with another seller;
- keep the slower route for relationship reasons;
- relocate stock to a transfer hub.

The route revision becomes durable world history.

## 14. The Stall in the Construction Zone

Public works reroute pedestrian traffic away from a long-standing stall.

The vendor’s problem is not necessarily lost ownership. It is visibility and footfall.

Possible outcomes include temporary relocation, altered signage, schedule changes or a negotiated frontage plan.

## 15. Festival Sellers and Permanent Merchants

A major festival brings temporary sellers into a district with year-round shops.

The temporary vendors increase total visitors but also compete for certain purchases. Local responses can be mixed without becoming a “locals versus outsiders” plot.

The event provides data for future planning.

## 16. The Repurchased Heirloom

A family once sold an ordinary object during difficult circumstances. Decades later a descendant notices the same persistent item in a secondhand market.

The object’s identity is supported by repair marks and records.

The descendant has no automatic ownership claim. They can buy it, negotiate, document it or leave it where it is.

## 17. The Replica Became Famous

A craftsperson openly made a replica of a historic object for educational use.

Years later the replica becomes more recognizable to the public than the original because it traveled through schools and festivals.

There is no fraud. The interesting story concerns public memory and material continuity.

## 18. Auction Catalog Archive

A local archive preserves decades of auction catalogs.

The catalogs reveal:

- businesses that disappeared;
- old material sources;
- changing fashion;
- objects whose provenance was later corrected;
- route disruptions reflected in available goods;
- repeated institutional buyers.

They are valuable historical evidence but not perfect records of actual completed sales.

## 19. The Merchant Retires, the Route Continues

An itinerant merchant retires and transfers the route business to an apprentice.

The successor inherits schedules and supplier contacts where explicitly transferred, but not automatic trust, friendships or personal debts.

Some towns welcome the continuity; others treat the new merchant as a new relationship.

## 20. The Pokémon Helper Opts Out

A merchant regularly works alongside a Pokémon partner during setup and transport.

One week the Pokémon does not participate.

The system records the observable absence. It does not invent injury, resentment, fatigue or disobedience. The merchant adapts staffing and transport until the cause is known or the arrangement changes permanently.

## 21. The Harbor Market After the Storm

A storm damages several normal supply routes but leaves the market venue intact.

The next market session is smaller and more local. A later one contains unusual merchants because relief routes temporarily connect places that rarely trade directly.

The market becomes a visible indicator of regional recovery.

## 22. The Stale Price Board

A public board shows yesterday’s offers and terms after a communications outage.

The vendors honor only currently valid agreements, producing frustration without automatically implying deception.

The incident leads to a clearer timestamp/version practice.

## 23. The Object Under Stewardship Review

A culturally significant object is consigned for sale by someone who appears to possess it.

Before transfer, an institution raises a documented stewardship question.

The market places the lot on hold and preserves custody while the appropriate system reviews authority. The seller is not automatically a thief.

## 24. The Market Becomes a Destination

A formerly ordinary weekly market gains attention after photographs and travel writing make it popular.

Visitor growth changes lodging, waste, public-space pressure and stall mix. The market operator must decide how much temporary expansion to allow without turning every local seller into a tourism business.

## 25. The Swap Table

A club begins an informal table for members to exchange ordinary duplicate items and crafted cosmetics.

As participation grows, the club must decide whether the table remains informal, gets written rules or becomes a separate public event.

Player-to-player transfers remain explicit and consent-based.

## 26. The Seed Exchange Without a Yield Bonus

Gardeners and farmers exchange documented seed batches before planting season.

The event supports provenance, local relationships and crop diversity. It does not grant automatic yield bonuses or define breeding/growth mechanics.

A later research project can compare which batches actually established successfully.

## 27. The Quiet Market Day

A weekly market has an unusually slow session.

Nothing is wrong.

Bad weather was forecast, a tournament pulled visitors elsewhere and two major merchants skipped the week for unrelated reasons.

The game should be allowed to record a quiet day without creating a hidden crisis to justify it.

## 28. Provenance Pause

A highly anticipated auction is cancelled minutes before opening because a document suggests the lot may have been removed from an archaeological site without proper context.

No guilt is established. The cancellation protects the object’s chain of custody while a case reviews evidence.

Months later the lot may be cleared, returned, loaned or remain unresolved.

## 29. The Market That Shrinks After Success

A settlement once depended on one huge weekly market because transport and local retail were unreliable.

After roads, rail and neighborhood shops improve, the market becomes smaller.

Residents debate whether this is decline or simply a different role. The market may survive as a specialist/social institution instead of the town’s only distribution node.

## 30. The Merchant Who Remembers Every Version

An elderly seller has worked through several currencies, route networks, market buildings and generations of customers.

Their memory is useful but fallible. Old receipts, maps and catalog archives can corroborate or correct individual recollections.

The character can become a bridge across many previous Ouros systems without functioning as an omniscient lore NPC.

# Long Arc A — Ten Saturdays at Harbor Exchange

Year 1:

The harbor market is a modest weekly event dependent on two ferries and one road.

Year 2:

A storm season disrupts arrivals. Local sellers occupy more stalls.

Year 3:

A new rail spur changes which merchants can attend and creates a transfer hub.

Year 4:

Tourism increases after public media coverage. Residents disagree about expansion.

Year 5:

A provenance dispute causes the first formal auction hold policy.

Year 6+:

The market remains recognizable but has changed seller mix, transport dependency and public role.

The arc can be experienced through individual sessions rather than a single giant quest.

# Long Arc B — The Merchant Road Becomes Rail

A recurring traveling merchant network connects four small settlements.

Over several years:

- one bridge improves;
- one ferry declines;
- a rail line opens;
- a settlement gains a permanent shop;
- an apprentice takes over one route;
- a former stopping point becomes a seasonal market only.

The final network is more efficient in some ways but carries a visible history of what it replaced.

No infrastructure change is automatically framed as progress or loss; consequences remain concrete.

# Long Arc C — The Provenance Market

A small secondhand market gradually becomes known for objects with documented histories.

Early years:

- sellers record maker marks and repairs informally;
- an archive begins preserving catalogs;
- one embarrassing misattribution produces better correction practices.

Middle years:

- museums and researchers visit;
- replicas are labeled more clearly;
- consignment and custody rules mature;
- provenance gaps become accepted as uncertainty rather than automatic suspicion.

Later years:

The market becomes a regional source for material-history research while remaining an ordinary place where people also buy used tools and household goods.

# Encounter Contract Candidate A — Auction Hall Evacuation

FULL:

A disruption occurs during a live auction. Civilians, lots and exits become meaningful moving objectives.

Dependencies:

- complete movement including push/pull/knockback/interception/forced movement;
- AI tactical policy;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions only if the authored threat actually creates battlefield effects.

Current intended state: BLOCKED at full fidelity.

REDUCED:

The venue evacuates in world state before AutoPTU opens. Lots remain under custody outside BattleSpec. The battle uses a static hall/perimeter. Auction state becomes PAUSED and resumes or cancels after aftermath review.

Narrative premise preserved.

# Encounter Contract Candidate B — Traveling Market Chokepoint

FULL:

Merchants and stock attempt to move through a contested route while combatants protect, clear or disengage around them.

Dependencies:

- complete movement/interception;
- AI tactical policy for CLEAR_ROUTE/WITHDRAW/PROTECT;
- adapter/playback for moving transport and cargo;
- environment family if route conditions are tactically active.

Current intended state: BLOCKED at full fidelity.

REDUCED:

Merchant transport remains off-grid. A conventional static encounter resolves the chokepoint. Travel then calculates whether the route reopened in time for the market session.

# Encounter Contract Candidate C — Night Market Wildlife Spillover

FULL:

A temporary market overlaps a pre-existing wildlife movement window. Wildlife seeks exit while people move away from the route.

Dependencies:

- complete movement;
- AI tactical policy for WITHDRAW/AVOID_CIVILIANS/CLEAR_ZONE;
- adapter/playback;
- terrain/zones only where an authored mechanical environment exists.

Current intended state: BLOCKED at full fidelity.

REDUCED:

The crowd is redirected first. Most wildlife leaves through world state. Only individuals that remain in a genuine confrontation enter a static AutoPTU battle. Market and ecology state update separately afterward.

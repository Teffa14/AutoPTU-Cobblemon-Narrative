# Ouros wildlife rehabilitation, release & monitoring candidates — Pass 162

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Every item requires continuity, originality, PTU/Caelo and implementation review before promotion.
Date: 2026-08-25

These candidates extend Care, Conservation and Pokémon Agency through the Pass 162 rehabilitation/release protocol. None creates ownership, Loyalty, capture rights, veterinary mechanics or regional policy.

## Thirty worldbuilding and adventure candidates

### 1. The Gate Opens, Nothing Happens

A release candidate remains near the acclimation enclosure after the exit is opened. Staff record the behavior and wait rather than forcing departure. The next decision depends on later observations, not a timer.

### 2. The Pokémon Who Came Back

A previously released individual returns to the rehabilitation grounds months later. It looks well in casual observation. Competing explanations include route familiarity, food availability, another Pokémon, shelter or ordinary range use. The return is not labelled failure.

### 3. Two Good Release Sites

Two sites satisfy different operational needs. One has stronger habitat continuity; another has lower human disturbance and better monitoring coverage. The choice becomes an institutional discussion rather than a hidden optimal answer.

### 4. The Storm Shifted the Window

Care review is complete, but a storm changes route access and local habitat conditions. The release is delayed without changing the Pokémon's readiness assessment.

### 5. The Migration Left First

A rehabilitated migratory Pokémon reaches readiness after the expected movement window. The team must decide whether to hold longer, seek a different authored release plan or wait for the next appropriate opportunity. No rule is invented from species flavor.

### 6. The Quiet Feeder

Supplemental feeding is reduced gradually. The Pokémon visits less often, but monitoring is too sparse to determine whether it has shifted to wild resources or simply changed its schedule.

### 7. The Broken Transmitter

A telemetry device stops reporting. Public rumor immediately assumes the Pokémon is dead. Field retrieval shows that the device failed near a known movement corridor. The Pokémon's actual state remains unknown.

### 8. The Signal Without the Pokémon

A detached monitoring tag is recovered in good condition. It proves the device separated from the subject, not what happened afterward.

### 9. The Former Patient at the Ferry

Years after release, a persistent individual is observed near a ferry landing outside its original monitoring area. The sighting can connect Travel, Urban Wildlife or Migration without reopening custody.

### 10. The Release Site Became Popular

A scenic area chosen years ago for low disturbance becomes a tourism destination. The institution must reassess future releases while the old release history remains valid for its time.

### 11. The Yard That Became Habitat

Vegetation and water features around a former soft-release enclosure become useful habitat after the program ends. Conservation decides whether to remove, repurpose or retain the structures.

### 12. The Food Cart Problem

A Pokémon under care approaches humans only when a distinctive feeding cart appears. A different test context produces a different response. The case prevents the system from treating one approach event as global habituation.

### 13. The Familiar Voice

A former caretaker visits during a readiness observation and changes the Pokémon's behavior. The session is preserved as context-dependent evidence rather than treated as the animal's ordinary response to unfamiliar people.

### 14. The Wrong Reunion Story

A released Pokémon is photographed beside a group of the same species. Media call it a family reunion. Wild Collective state only records co-presence until stronger evidence exists.

### 15. The Group Moved Away

The release candidate's former collective is no longer using the original area. Conservation, Migration and Wild Collectives must decide what is actually known before a site is selected.

### 16. The Return After Injury

A released Pokémon later appears injured and requires a second care case. Its first release event remains part of history. The new incident may be unrelated to rehabilitation quality.

### 17. The Second Release

After temporary return to care, the same persistent individual is released again. Chronicle now contains two release attempts, two evidence windows and one continuous Pokémon identity.

### 18. The Visitor Closure Worked Too Well

A temporary release-site closure successfully reduces disturbance. The public then assumes the area should remain permanently closed. Stewardship review must separate a temporary operational measure from long-term policy.

### 19. The Release Without Telemetry

A program lacks suitable monitoring equipment. Reviewers must decide what can realistically be learned from field signs, cameras and community reports instead of pretending precise tracking exists.

### 20. The Camera Trap Saw Everyone Else

A post-release camera records many Pokémon but never the released individual. Coverage and activity timing remain uncertain, so the record stays `NOT_DETECTED` rather than `ABSENT`.

### 21. The Researcher Wants One More Week

A monitoring team wants to extend intensive observation for scientific value. Care and Conservation believe the intervention is no longer necessary. Research Ethics must decide whether continued handling or close tracking is justified.

### 22. The Sanctuary Is the Correct Outcome

A case review concludes that release is not currently appropriate. Long-term sanctuary residence becomes a valid world-state outcome without being framed as narrative defeat.

### 23. The Wild Pokémon That Never Needed Admission

Responders initially prepare to transport an apparently distressed Pokémon. Better observation shows it can remain safely in place with a temporary disturbance buffer. The encounter ends without a care case.

### 24. The Juvenile That Was Not Orphaned

A juvenile is admitted after an emergency because adults were not seen. Later evidence reveals continued parental use of the area. Pass 146 and Conservation drive a cautious reunification review rather than rewriting the original rescue as wrongdoing.

### 25. The Released Partner's Old Route

A Pokémon with a former Trainer history resumes using a landscape it knew before capture. The callback comes from recorded locations, not generated nostalgia or a Loyalty assumption.

### 26. The Facility Stops Taking That Species

Years of cases show that the current enclosures are poorly suited to one recurring patient type. The institution changes referral policy and invests in another service rather than continuing to improvise.

### 27. Three Programs, Three Meanings of Success

A rehabilitation station, a conservation project and a community sanctuary use different outcome indicators. Their records can disagree without one institution being incompetent.

### 28. The Release Atlas Has Blank Years

An archive reconstructs decades of releases, but several years lack monitoring. Those gaps remain visible instead of being filled procedurally.

### 29. The Taper Ended Normally

Supplemental support ends on schedule, monitoring continues at lower intensity and nothing dramatic happens. Years later, this ordinary program becomes a useful baseline.

### 30. Nothing Happened at Cedar Release Site

The planned release proceeds calmly. The Pokémon departs, monitoring records routine observations and no quest appears. Chronicle still records the event because ordinary success is part of world history.

## Long arc A — Five Releases at Cedar Rehabilitation Station

Year 1: A straightforward release establishes the station's first monitoring template.

Year 2: A storm forces a delay and reveals that readiness and site availability are different states.

Year 3: A released individual returns to the yard; staff add better observation context before interpreting return behavior.

Year 4: Tourism expands near the old site, forcing a new release-location review.

Year 5: A case is judged unsuitable for release and transitions to another valid care/residence outcome.

The arc allows institutional maturity without escalating every year into a villain plot. Later staff inherit records from earlier decisions and can solve routine cases with less player intervention.

## Long arc B — The Pokémon Who Came Back

Phase 1: A wild Pokémon enters care after a documented crisis.

Phase 2: It completes an authored recovery process and receives a scoped readiness assessment.

Phase 3: A release occurs with temporary support and limited monitoring.

Phase 4: Monitoring becomes sparse; the individual is not detected for a long period.

Phase 5: It reappears at the original facility or nearby landscape in apparently ordinary condition.

Phase 6: Repeated observations reveal that the area is now part of a wider route. The story may ultimately concern landscape change rather than dependency on humans.

The individual keeps the same `pokemon_entity_id` through the entire arc.

## Long arc C — From Intake to First Migration

A juvenile or displaced migratory Pokémon enters rehabilitation before a seasonal movement period.

The arc follows:

- initial care;
- uncertainty over dependency;
- release-site and migration-window review;
- possible staged support;
- departure;
- later observation at one or more stopovers;
- a future season where the same individual may or may not be detected again.

The arc can end with `OUTCOME_UNKNOWN` and still be complete narrative content.

A formerly released wild Pokémon appears at the facility while visitors are present. No one yet knows whether it needs assistance.

FULL version:

Crowds and the Pokémon can reposition non-hostilely while staff create space. Success is de-escalation and safe separation rather than KO.

Dependencies:

- complete movement: BLOCKING for dynamic crowd and wildlife routing;

- adapter/playback: BLOCKING;

## Non-combat scenario — Release Readiness Review

A multidisciplinary review examines Care summaries, behavior observations, current site conditions, biosecurity information, population context and monitoring feasibility.

Possible outcomes:

- ready for site review;
- ready only as a supported-release candidate;
- needs more evidence;
- not ready;
- release paused because the site/window is unsuitable;
- release currently inappropriate, with another care/residence path considered.

No battle result can substitute for the review.

# Ouros Taxonomy, Species Classification & Nomenclature Seeds — Pass 119

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Every concept requires continuity, originality and PTU/Caelo/mechanical review before promotion.

These candidates assume the proposed taxonomy layer from Pass 119. None creates a new Pokémon species, form, Type, Ability, Move, evolution or mechanical rule.

## 30 original candidates

### 1. The Wrong Name on the Old Map

A forty-year-old route map marks a marsh population under a name no current field guide recognizes. The locations remain useful, but nobody is sure whether the old label refers to a superseded name, a misidentification or a population that has since disappeared.

### 2. Three Museums, Three Labels

Three institutions display specimens from the same coastal lineage under three labels inherited from different catalog revisions. The specimens themselves have never changed.

### 3. The “Regional Form” Headline

A photograph of an unusually colored population goes viral as proof of a new regional form. Repeated surveys confirm the color pattern but nothing yet supports a new mechanical form.

### 4. The Field Guide Nobody Updated

A widely trusted ranger handbook still uses a superseded species concept. Its route advice is excellent, but its labels now merge two populations that current research treats separately.

### 5. The Archive Under the Synonym

A modern research team believes no historic observations exist for a rare population. An archivist finds decades of records filed under an older name.

### 6. The Forest Tentacool Problem

Observers repeatedly report a Tentacool-like body shape in a humid inland forest. The entire investigation is about classification and ecology; the game never assumes the resemblance means shared lineage.

### 7. The Population With Two Calls

One visually uniform species population uses two stable call patterns in different valleys. Researchers disagree over whether the difference is cultural behavior, geography or evidence of deeper differentiation.

### 8. The New Species That Was Already in Storage

A specimen announced as unprecedented matches an uncatalogued museum drawer collected decades earlier. The discovery remains important because the old specimen was never correctly integrated into the collection.

### 9. The Split That Breaks the Conservation Map

A taxonomic split is proposed for a population currently managed under one broad species label. Conservation needs a temporary operational rule while classification remains under review.

### 10. The Merge That Leaves Two Traditions

Two historically distinct names are merged scientifically. Communities continue using both local names for practical and cultural reasons.

### 11. The Sample Without a Match

A lab returns an unresolved reference-library match from a wetland sample. The correct next action is broader sampling and archive comparison, not declaring a new Pokémon.

### 12. The Rare Pokémon That Was Two

Old photographs treated two similar species as one. Reclassification changes historical distribution maps without changing any historical sighting event.

### 13. The Two Pokémon That Were One

Two field names used by different districts are found to describe the same species concept. The separate local ecological records still matter because the populations behave differently.

### 14. The Juvenile Error

A “new species” report turns out to be a poorly documented juvenile life stage of a known Pokémon. The original observers were reasonable given the evidence available.

### 15. The Evolution Error

A museum once treated two stages of the same evolutionary line as unrelated taxa because no one had documented the transformation. The later correction must preserve both catalog histories.

### 16. The Fossil With the Modern Name

A fossil display was labeled decades later using a modern Pokémon name despite uncertain anatomical evidence. A new paleontology review asks whether the label is too confident.

### 17. The Local Expert Was More Precise

A fishing community has long distinguished two populations that the regional Pokédex treated as one. New research finds the practical distinction maps to real ecological differences without yet proving a taxonomic split.

### 18. The Professor Corrects Themself

A respected professor publicly revises a famous classification after better evidence appears. The story is institutional self-correction, not scandal.

### 19. The Rival Labs Share the Raw Data

Two labs publish different taxonomic interpretations from the same observations. The conflict becomes a long-running scientific debate where both keep contributing useful evidence.

### 20. The Photograph With No Scale

An old image appears to show a radically different body size. Metrology review reveals that perspective and missing scale make the size claim weaker than the morphology claim.

### 21. The Trackway With Three Candidates

Field signs fit three Pokémon equally well. The quest can end with a ranked candidate set rather than forced certainty.

### 22. The Signal Downstream

An eDNA-like signal appears downstream of a protected habitat. Sampling upstream eventually shows the evidence may have been transported rather than proving presence at the first site.

### 23. The Name on the Permit

A conservation permit protects a species under a historical name. Taxonomy changes, but nobody is sure whether the old document's intended scope automatically maps to the new concepts.

### 24. The Old Pokédex Edition

Collectors value an early Pokédex edition because several entries preserve classifications that later changed. It becomes an archive object and research source rather than merely outdated trash.

### 25. The Form Nobody Can Authorize

A regional institution wants to publish a new form designation for prestige. The evidence supports local variation but there is no authoritative mechanical content for a new form, so the proposal remains scientific/worldbuilding only.

### 26. The Public Name Wins

A provisional field nickname becomes so popular that the eventual formal classification cannot displace it in everyday speech. Public Memory and Taxonomy keep both names.

### 27. The Same Pokémon, New Label

A persistent released Pokémon is photographed over many years. Taxonomy changes around its population, but its `pokemon_entity_id` remains unchanged through every reclassification.

### 28. The Survey That Found Nothing New

A highly funded expedition concludes that all unusual observations fit known taxa. The null taxonomic result still improves distribution and behavior knowledge.

### 29. The Reference Library Gap

A molecular/reference system repeatedly returns uncertain matches for one remote region because that region is underrepresented in the library. The fix is institution-building and new reference samples, not more aggressive certainty.

### 30. The Taxonomy Atlas

A regional atlas stores every accepted name, superseded label, uncertain record and revision history on transparent chronological layers. It becomes a playable archive of how knowledge changed.

## Three long arcs

### Arc A — Five Names for the Same Shoreline Pokémon

Year 1: fishers use two local names for a coastal Pokémon while the university uses one broad species label.

Year 2: photographs reveal consistent morphological differences between northern and southern populations.

Year 3: a museum specimen shows that the northern population was collected decades earlier under a forgotten third name.

Year 4: two institutions publish different interpretations: regional variation versus separate species.

Year 5: authored/canonical review either resolves the issue or leaves it deliberately open. Regardless, field guides, permits and old datasets must be crosswalked rather than rewritten.

Long-term consequences can touch Fisheries, Conservation, Archives, Tourism, Public Memory and Education without changing battle mechanics unless separate mechanical content is approved.

### Arc B — The Pokédex Revision Years

A regional Pokédex edition launches with known gaps. Each year, field teams, museums, communities and players submit observations. Some entries become more precise; some old entries split; others merge; a famous mistake is corrected; one “new species” proves to be an old synonym; another remains unresolved for the entire campaign.

The story measures institutional maturity through provenance and willingness to preserve uncertainty, not through a completion percentage alone.

### Arc C — The Three-Island Species Complex

Three island populations look similar but differ in calls, timing and habitat use.

Early surveys treat them as one species.

Later island-biogeography work establishes strong population differentiation.

A biosecurity incident moves individuals between islands, complicating future identification.

Conservation must make management decisions before taxonomy is settled.

The end state can remain “one canonical mechanical species, several ecologically distinct populations” indefinitely.

## Hard proposal constraints

None of these candidates may procedurally create:

- Fakemon;
- new regional forms;
- convergent species;
- new Types;
- new Abilities;
- new Moves;
- new stats;
- new evolution methods;
- shiny variants;
- new capture rules;
- Researcher/Pokémon Education bonuses;
- rarity multipliers;
- spawn-rate bonuses;
- ownership claims over discovered Pokémon.

The strongest desired outcome is a world where scientific names and classifications can change while individual Pokémon, observations, populations and historical records retain continuity.

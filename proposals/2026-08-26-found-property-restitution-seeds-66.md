# Found Property & Restitution Seeds — Pass 66

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANON.

These are original Ouros candidates derived from the Pass 66 research patterns. Names, institutions, locations, customs and outcomes remain unapproved until explicitly promoted.

## The Umbrella at Closing

A small service closes for the evening and finds a distinctive umbrella under the last occupied table. Several regulars plausibly used the seat that day.

The object is ordinary in value but personally recognizable because it has a repair stitch and a mismatched handle. Staff hold it behind the counter. The first person to claim “mine” gives only the public description. A later claimant knows the repair detail.

Use:

- establishes a recurring business routine;
- teaches that custody and ownership differ;
- lets a tiny object create a callback without a combat reward;
- can reveal who was actually present at a place without turning the item into forensic certainty.

## Wrong Name, Right Description

A found case has a written name inside, but the person with that name says it is not theirs. Another actor correctly describes a hidden compartment and an old repair.

Possible explanations:

- the case was borrowed;
- the written name belongs to a previous holder;
- the label was never updated;
- the second claimant repaired it but never owned it;
- the first actor is protecting someone else's privacy.

Do not preselect one explanation until world evidence supports it.

## The Finder Comes Back

A traveler finds an object, hands it to a local service desk, then leaves. Weeks later the player meets that finder again and learns whether the return ever succeeded.

The callback can change:

- trust in the service;
- the finder's opinion of the town;
- the recipient's routine;
- whether a later object is entrusted through the same channel.

The finder never needs to become a permanent faction member or quest giver.

## Lost During Relocation

A household has already moved. A neighbor finds a small personal object in a shared courtyard near the old residence.

Residential history supplies the previous address and move date. That evidence makes the former resident a plausible contact but does not prove ownership.

The interesting consequence is temporal: if the player delivers it to the old address, they find a new resident; if they follow the household's updated routine, the object may reconnect two neighborhoods.

## Found in the Old Locker

A workplace opens a locker that has not been used since a staffing change and finds a named object inside.

The former worker may have:

- transferred roles;
- moved away;
- retired;
- intentionally left it;
- lent it to someone else;
- forgotten it.

Staffing state establishes who once used the locker. It does not decide current ownership.

## The Claim Nobody Makes

A distinctive object is found after a public event, yet no one claims it even after notices circulate.

The absence of a claim becomes the story.

Possible non-canon directions:

- the holder never saw the notice;
- the item was being carried for someone else;
- claiming it would reveal attendance the holder prefers to keep private;
- the object was already unwanted;
- the item belongs to an institution whose inventory is incomplete.

Do not interpret silence as abandonment without a canon rule.

## Identical Cases

Two visually identical instrument cases are found after a performance weekend. Both claimants can describe the model and color because those details are common. The cases differ only in internal wear, a maker mark and contents that were not publicly listed.

This seed supports provenance-based verification without requiring a hidden percentage or arbitrary dialogue check.

## Returned to the Wrong Desk

A found object reaches a service desk that has no responsibility for it. The staff member accepts it informally, creating a custody gap between two otherwise competent institutions.

The quest is administrative rather than criminal. The player can reconstruct the handoff, correct the custody record and move the object to the right service without accusing anyone of theft.

This can connect Transit, Event Operations, Workplace and Found Property systems.

## The Gift with an Old Label

A personal object bears a label with the name of someone who no longer lives locally. A claimant says it was meant as a gift and never belonged to the named person in the way everyone assumes.

This seed explores the difference among maker, intended recipient, possessor and owner claim. The label is evidence of association, not a universal title document.

## The Repair Mark

A workshop recognizes a found object's repair as its own work. The repair log identifies the customer from years earlier, but current world state shows the object changed hands later.

The old repair narrows the provenance chain without deciding the final claimant.

This creates a reason to revisit an existing workshop and makes prior maintenance history useful outside crafting.

## The Notebook After the Expedition

A field notebook is found on a route several days after a research group has left the area.

Safe design:

- the exterior may reveal a name or institution if visibly marked;
- the finder does not automatically read every page;
- if the notebook contains research observations, those claims remain attributed to their author;
- returning it can create a future Science/Research callback.

The notebook does not grant Pokédex knowledge, Trainer Feature benefits or Accuracy modifiers simply because the player possesses it.

## Three Claimants, One Camera Case

A camera case is recovered after a crowded destination day. Three people make plausible claims.

Evidence graph:

- Claimant A knows the public color/model and was photographed near the find site.
- Claimant B knows an internal repair patch but learned about it from the same witness as A.
- Claimant C has an older photograph of the exact case, a matching strap replacement record and a timeline showing a legitimate handoff shortly before the loss.

The puzzle is to notice dependency between sources. Two claims that share one origin do not automatically count as two independent confirmations.

Possible resolutions:

- verified return;
- insufficient evidence and continued holding;
- discovery that claimant C is an authorized proxy rather than owner;
- escalation if evidence of deliberate false claiming appears.

No battle determines the result.

## Trail Satchel Recovery

A personally significant satchel is reported missing along an active route. A later inspection locates the exact object in a place that is temporarily unsafe because Pokémon are occupying the area.

Full encounter intent:

- exact persistent object;
- search/access objective;
- possible terrain/weather interaction;
- territorial or withdrawal behavior;
- interception/forced movement if supported;
- objective-aware AI;
- synchronized adapter playback.

Reduced implementation:

The satchel remains outside tactical targeting. The unsafe section closes. A static battle resolves the immediate route threat. Recovery occurs afterwards as a separate world interaction and starts claimant verification.

A battle victory never proves ownership.

## Event Teardown Recovery Sweep

After a temporary festival/event closes, teardown staff find several personal objects in a section that wild Pokémon have begun using again.

Full encounter intent:

- workers withdrawing;
- several recovery zones;
- changing access;
- temporary barriers or fragile scenery;
- environmental hazards if governed;
- territorial/retreat AI;
- persistent playback of which items were actually recovered.

Reduced implementation:

Workers leave before combat. Named objects remain in narrative world state. A static encounter makes access safe. The post-battle sweep creates individual find events for significant objects.

## A Desk Learns the Neighborhood

Long-form recurring arc.

Visit 1:

A small holding point exists informally. Staff know their regulars and return most ordinary objects without incident.

Visit 2:

A public event causes a temporary backlog. One significant object receives an exact persistent record.

Visit 3:

A claimant dispute exposes that public descriptions were being posted too completely, making false claims easy. The service changes how it separates public and private verification details.

Visit 4:

A relocation or transit disruption creates a return that cannot be completed locally. The service creates a formal handoff to another system instead of improvising.

Visit 5:

Months later, the player sees the revised routine operating without needing another crisis. A new NPC refers to the earlier change as ordinary local practice.

Persistence value:

The service improves because of accumulated events. The neighborhood changes through procedure, memory and trust rather than a building upgrade bar.

## Canon review questions

Before any seed is placed in Ouros, confirm:

- the actual settlement/location;
- whether the holding actor/institution exists and has this responsibility;
- available identity/record technology;
- regional privacy customs;
- whether claim verification is formal or social;
- whether finder rewards exist;
- what happens to unclaimed property;
- what property categories require special handling;
- whether the related route/event/workplace state already exists;
- whether any battle version requires unsupported mechanics.

Until then, every seed above remains proposal material only.

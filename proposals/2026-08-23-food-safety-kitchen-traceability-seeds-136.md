# Food Safety, Kitchen Operations & Traceability Seeds — Pass 136

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS.
Date: 2026-08-23

These are original Ouros candidates derived from the Pass 136 research. None are canon-approved.

## 30 candidates

1. The Sauce Batch With Two Parents. A festival sauce combines two ingredient lots from different suppliers. Only one later comes under suspicion, forcing the kitchen to reconstruct which servings actually contained it.

2. The Safe Dish With the Wrong Label. A meal batch passes every available safety observation, but its label points to the wrong ingredient lot. The problem is provenance and service accuracy, not illness.

3. The Refrigerator That Failed Quietly. A storage unit logs a fault overnight. Staff do not know whether the failure lasted minutes or hours because the monitoring clock also drifted.

4. Three Complaints, Three Meals. Three diners report illness after visiting the same café, but their receipts show no shared dish. The venue is still relevant as a shared exposure site, not yet a confirmed source.

5. The Water Outage Menu. A neighborhood water interruption forces several restaurants to cut their menus differently. One closes, one switches to sealed items and one stays open only for drinks from a separate supply.

6. The Hold Nobody Notices. A careful cook places a single ingredient lot on internal hold before service. Nothing bad happens. Months later the record becomes valuable during a supplier review.

7. The Festival Stall That Sold Out First. A popular stall sells out before a supplier withdrawal arrives. Traceforward becomes a records problem because no physical stock remains.

8. The Clean Kitchen, Dirty Delivery Crate. The preparation area is orderly, but an incoming container arrives damaged. The venue must decide whether the problem belongs to freight, packaging, ingredient quality or safety.

9. The Meal That Never Reached the Table. A preparation batch is completed, then discarded after a late warning. Chronicle preserves the batch even though no diner consumed it.

10. The Shared Cutting Board Rumor. A witness reports seeing raw and prepared ingredients on one surface. Later records show the events happened hours apart after cleaning. The original report remains honest but incomplete.

11. The Pokémon Helper Opts Out. A Pokémon that often assists with ingredient carrying refuses to enter the kitchen one morning. Staff reorganize service without assuming illness, danger or supernatural warning.

12. The Wrong Lot at the Right Restaurant. Investigators receive a correct supplier notice but initially link it to the wrong delivery record because two lots arrived on the same day.

13. The Archive Recipe With No Safety Record. A famous historical dish can be reconstructed from an old cookbook, but there is no evidence that the original preparation process meets modern institutional expectations.

14. The Kitchen Thermometer Disagrees. Two instruments produce different readings. Metrology must resolve the measurement issue before Food Safety treats either result as authoritative.

15. The Remote Lodge Stop-Use Notice. A mountain lodge receives notice that one stored ingredient should not be served just as a storm blocks resupply. The choice is logistical and culinary before it is dramatic.

16. The School Canteen Split Batch. One large preparation batch is divided between two buildings. A later hold affects both locations even though only one has reported a problem.

17. The Batch Everyone Remembers Wrong. Staff confidently recall that an ingredient was delivered Tuesday. Postal/freight records show Wednesday. Nothing indicates dishonesty; memory simply lost to routine.

18. The Famous Critic Ate Something Else. Public attention focuses on a restaurant after a food critic becomes ill. Their actual meal records show they did not eat the dish blamed by social media.

19. The Closed Stall With No Outbreak. A vendor voluntarily shuts down after discovering uncertain provenance. No illness follows. The decision still becomes part of the stall’s institutional history.

20. The Supplier Changed the Package. The same ingredient arrives in new packaging with a new code format. Staff briefly treat it as a different product, creating duplicate inventory records but no physical duplication.

21. The Leftover That Became Evidence. A small retained portion from a service event is still available when a later investigation begins. Material custody now matters as much as the recipe.

22. The Night Shift Knows the Cooler. A worker notices a recurring sound change before the storage unit fails. Their observation has operational value but does not itself identify what component is broken.

23. The Festival Without a Kitchen. A cultural event moves toward sealed/prepared foods after its temporary kitchen infrastructure cannot be made reliable. The event survives without treating the change as cultural decline.

24. The Dish With a Safe Substitute. A supplier problem removes a traditional ingredient. A substitute keeps service safe and available but changes taste enough to create a separate cultural dispute.

25. The Recall That Missed One Inn. A route disruption prevents a supplier notice from reaching a remote inn at the same time as urban venues. Communications and transport history explain the timing gap.

26. The Suspected Poison-Type. A Poison-type Pokémon is seen near a kitchen loading area and becomes the subject of rumors. Timeline evidence shows the relevant ingredient lot had already been isolated before the Pokémon arrived.

27. The Reopened Restaurant, Open Investigation. Operational corrections are complete and the venue reopens, while a broader outbreak investigation remains unresolved. Public messaging must explain both states accurately.

28. The Empty Sample Jar. An expected retained sample cannot be found. The absence creates a traceability problem but does not prove tampering.

29. The Same Dish, New Kitchen. A traditional recipe moves from an old family venue to a new community kitchen. Recipe continuity and preparation-system history diverge.

30. Nothing Happened at Dinner. A busy service completes normally. Records are retained, equipment works and no signal appears. Years later this ordinary night becomes a useful baseline when investigators compare a later incident.

## Long arc — Five Incidents at Meridian Market Kitchen

Year 1: a small storage fault produces a precautionary hold with no illness.

Year 2: a supplier withdrawal reaches the market after most stock is sold. Traceforward reveals the market kept better receiving records than expected.

Year 3: several health reports intersect the market, but epidemiology points away from food service entirely.

Year 4: a potable-water outage forces temporary menu redesign and creates a new dependency map between the market, Water Service and Supply Chains.

Year 5: a real source-linked food incident occurs. Staff respond differently because they remember the previous four events. The arc should reward institutional learning, not escalation for its own sake.

Possible end states include stronger traceability, reduced menu complexity, shared cold storage, a new worker committee, or simply better operating records.

## Long arc — The Ingredient With Seven Stops

A single persistent ingredient lot begins at an agricultural site and moves through packing, freight, a regional depot, a wholesale market, two kitchens and a community event.

Each handoff creates records at a different level of detail. Years later, the lot is used as a teaching case because it demonstrates that provenance can remain reconstructable even when no single institution saw the whole chain.

The arc can connect Food, Supply Chains, Postal/Freight, Markets, Manufacturing, Metrology, Finance and Archives without requiring wrongdoing.

## Long arc — The Restaurant That Learned to Close Early

A family venue experiences repeated near-misses rather than disasters: equipment drift, staffing gaps, a late supplier notice, a stormwater interruption and a mislabeled batch.

Over several years, the venue’s culture changes from “stay open at all costs” toward earlier precautionary shutdowns, simplified menus and stronger handoffs.

Success means fewer quests because the institution begins solving routine problems before player intervention is necessary.

## Encounter — Festival Stall Traceback

FULL version:

The party moves through an active festival while investigators compare stall records, ingredient deliveries and health reports. A confrontation or wildlife disturbance may force route clearing while civilians continue to move.

Dependencies:

Most of the investigation is non-combat. Dynamic crowds require complete movement, AI tactical policy and adapter/playback. Any exact battle effects still depend on the relevant partial mechanic families.

REDUCED version:

Pause or reroute the affected festival area in world state. Conduct interviews and record checks outside battle. If a confrontation occurs, use one static cleared location. The battle cannot identify the source.

## Canon questions

These proposals intentionally do not decide:

- whether Ouros has restaurant inspectors;
- whether mandatory recalls exist;
- who can force closure;
- which food hazards are recognized in canon;
- whether serving-level records are routine;
- how much kitchen technology each region has;
- whether allergens/pathogens are explicit systems;
- whether player businesses can be formally inspected;
- whether any Pokémon role is recognized as food-safety work;
- exact PTU/Caelo Chef, Food Buff, Digestion Buff, poison, Medicine or item rules.

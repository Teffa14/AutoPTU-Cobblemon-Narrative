# Ouros Proposal Seeds — Slope Instability, Landslide & Rockfall — Pass 114

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANON. These are worldbuilding and adventure candidates derived from Pass 114 research. They do not establish regional geography, institutions, exact Pokémon roles or battle mechanics.

## Design rule

Every seed preserves three separate layers:

1. what physically happened or was observed;
2. what actors currently believe or have recorded;
3. what downstream authorities have decided.

A slope event is not an excuse to replace the world with a disaster cutscene. The goal is persistent consequences, revisitable places and evidence that remains meaningful after emergency response ends.

## Situation — The Road Is Clear, the Slope Is Not

A crew has removed enough material from a blocked switchback for inspectors to reach the far side. Residents begin saying the road is “open again,” but Road Operations has not reopened it because the source slope assessment is incomplete.

Useful actors:

- road crew;
- local residents who depend on the connection;
- courier or service provider using the detour;
- slope assessor;
- shop or lodging operator affected by redirected travel;
- recurring wild Pokémon that has started using the quiet closed roadway.

Possible continuation:

The central problem can resolve without combat through reconciliation of clearing, assessment and access records. If conflict occurs, use a reduced static perimeter encounter. Victory never reopens the road.

## Situation — The Old Route Still Has an Address

A former regional road was permanently bypassed after a historical slope failure. Decades later, an old property record, letter, delivery log or family memory references an address on the retired alignment.

Adventure value:

- reconcile historical maps with the replacement road;
- identify which structures survived;
- discover that local naming continued after official alignment changed;
- encounter a new habitat or informal use on the closed route;
- determine whether access is legal/safe without assuming the old road can be reopened.

The landslide itself remains background history. The story is about continuity of place.

## Situation — The Pokémon Left Before the Rockfall

Several residents remember a particular wild Pokémon leaving a cliffside area shortly before a rockfall. The pattern becomes local folklore.

Possible interpretations remain open:

- coincidence;
- the individual reacted to something actually observable;
- the animal changed routine for an unrelated ecological reason;
- the remembered timing has drifted in retelling;
- a later observation was incorrectly merged with the older event.

Do not convert this into species-wide landslide prediction. Investigation should compare dated observations, photographs, telemetry if available and witness scope.

## Situation — The Detour Made a Main Street

A slope closure reroutes travelers through a small settlement for months. A temporary stop, food stall, lodging room, courier counter or meeting place appears to serve the new traffic.

When the primary route returns, the settlement does not simply reset.

Possible consequences:

- one business survives because locals adopted it;
- a resident dislikes the loss of quiet;
- a Pokémon begins returning to the former temporary stop;
- a secondary road now needs maintenance from heavier use;
- a friendship/rivalry formed during the detour continues;
- visitors still use an outdated map and arrive by the old temporary route.

## Situation — Three Boulders, Two Events

A road archive lists three major boulder-removal jobs over several years. Local memory treats them as evidence that “the same cliff always fails.”

Investigation may show:

- two jobs belonged to one long event with multiple clearing phases;
- one boulder came from an unrelated roadside excavation;
- two different source sectors share a local place name;
- the records remain insufficient to establish recurrence.

The result should be provenance, not a hidden `recurrence_score`.

## Situation — The Survey Marker Moved

A field marker no longer stands where an old photograph shows it. Several explanations are possible: slope movement, maintenance, vandalism, map datum changes, intentional relocation or a mistaken photo location.

The investigation should preserve uncertainty until evidence distinguishes them.

This seed works as a low-stakes science/local-history quest and can later become relevant if a larger event occurs.

## Situation — The House Is Reachable From the Other Side

A slope failure closes the ordinary road approach to a cluster of homes, but a footpath or another authored connection remains available from a different direction.

Design purpose:

Avoid treating “main road closed” as “settlement inaccessible.” Travel topology and actor-specific access matter.

Possible consequences:

- deliveries change handoff point;
- school/work routines change;
- a local guide becomes unexpectedly important;
- elderly/disabled residents require an Accessibility review rather than an assumed staircase/path solution;
- a Pokémon route overlaps the temporary human path.

## Situation — The Stream Changed After the Slide

A slope event deposits material near a watercourse. Residents observe a changed channel edge or ponding.

Authority boundary:

Slope Instability records the deposition observation and footprint. Water/Stormwater systems decide hydraulic consequences. Conservation interprets ecological change. Crisis handles immediate danger if any.

Do not script flooding directly from the slope event without those owner systems.

## Situation — The Closed Cut Became Habitat

A roadside cut remains closed long enough that vegetation and wild Pokémon establish new routines there.

Years later, a reopening or stabilization proposal creates a genuine world conflict:

- restoring the old route may improve regional access;
- the site is no longer ecologically equivalent to the day it closed;
- heritage or public memory may also have accumulated;
- the original reason for closure may be technically resolved while new reasons for review exist.

This creates a strong civic/conservation story without requiring a villain.

## Situation — The Monitoring Station Saw Nothing

A monitored slope failed outside the instrumented sector. Public discussion frames the event as “the system failed.”

Investigation may show that the equipment worked exactly within its authored coverage while the event began elsewhere.

Narrative use:

- teach scope without exposition dumps;
- create institutional trust questions;
- distinguish monitoring availability from universal prediction;
- motivate a debated expansion of observation coverage.

## Situation — The Quarry and the Rain

A slope failure occurs near an excavation after heavy rain. Competing claims immediately blame either the quarry or the storm.

The world should preserve both as claims until evidence resolves more.

Potential threads:

- old drainage changes;
- recent work records;
- prior cracks in dated photographs;
- weather observations;
- geological context;
- worker testimony;
- wildlife observations unrelated to cause;
- civic pressure for a fast conclusion.

No moral outcome is preselected.

## Situation — The Temporary Barrier Stayed for Years

A slope sector was restricted after an event. The dramatic response ended, but the barrier, warning board or observation hut remains.

Later questions:

- is the restriction still authoritative?
- is the sign stale while access state changed?
- did the temporary structure acquire a new public use?
- does anyone maintain it?
- is the old closure now part of local identity?

Public Notices and Road Operations own current official state. The physical object alone does not answer it.

## Mystery — Four Maps, One Moving Boundary

Evidence set:

- pre-event road map;
- first-response sketch;
- later surveyed footprint;
- current public map;
- photographs with partial location metadata;
- closure and reopening timestamps.

Core question:

Why do all four maps disagree?

Possible resolution:

They describe different moments and different objects. One maps the road, one the first observed blockage, one the later understood footprint and one the replacement route.

No witness has to be lying.

## Mystery — Five Times the Road “Opened”

Five documents use language equivalent to “open” on different dates.

Possible meanings:

- debris cleared enough for inspection;
- emergency/service access allowed;
- one lane or foot route available;
- formal Road Operations reopening;
- public notice finally updated.

Players reconcile timestamps and scopes rather than hunt a fabricated culprit.

## Exploration — The Switchback Beneath the New Road

A replaced mountain alignment remains visible below a newer route. Parts are buried, others are intact and one maintenance path still reaches an old observation point.

Current executable version:

- use historical maps;
- compare old mile/segment names with current coordinates;
- find surviving signs, retaining features and route furniture;
- interview people who remember the former road;
- document wildlife use;
- enter only already-inspected stable sections;
- keep unstable or unknown areas outside accessible geometry.

Future mechanically rich version could add unstable surfaces, falling material or changing access only when exact PTU/Caelo and engine contracts exist.

## Exploration — The Debris Fan Archive

A local museum, road office or community archive preserves photographs and objects from repeated road closures. The nearby physical debris fan is now quiet and partially revegetated.

The quest alternates between archive evidence and present landscape.

Possible discoveries:

- a route name changed twice;
- a household relocated but retained ownership/kinship ties;
- a shop moved to the detour and never returned;
- one famous “slide photo” actually depicts later clearing work;
- a recurring Pokémon is present in photographs years apart, but identity must be verified rather than assumed.

## Faction / institution candidate — Slope Observation Cooperative

Status: PROPOSED archetype, not a canon organization.

A region could support a small cooperative of road workers, guides, researchers, residents and public-works staff who share slope observations.

Useful tension:

- locals have dense practical observations but variable terminology;
- specialists have formal methods but limited geographic coverage;
- route owners need operational decisions;
- businesses want predictability;
- conservation actors care about disturbance from stabilization work.

The cooperative is an information relationship, not a magic hazard detector.

## NPC archetype — The Route Historian

A retired courier, driver, ranger, guide or road worker remembers several alignments of the same corridor.

Strength:

They can connect old place names to current geography.

Limitation:

Memory is provenance, not perfect truth. They may accurately remember travel practice while misremembering engineering cause or exact dates.

## NPC archetype — The Cautious Reopener

An official or local owner repeatedly says that clearing a road is not the same as reopening it.

Narrative role:

This character can initially seem bureaucratic, then become useful when players discover separate slope, road-surface and downstream dependencies. Avoid making them automatically correct; their decisions still need evidence and can be revised.

## NPC archetype — The Detour Entrepreneur

A resident built a temporary service around redirected traffic and now faces the main road's return.

Potential arc:

They can lobby for the detour to remain signed, move the business, pivot toward locals or resent the reopening. Their story turns infrastructure continuity into ordinary personal stakes.

## Long arc — A Valley Learns Its Slopes

Phase A: ordinary life.

Establish roads, farms/homes, workplaces, travel patterns, regular weather reports and recurring Pokémon observations before any disaster.

Phase B: ambiguous observations.

Small rockfall reports, drainage changes or cracks appear. They may be unrelated. Institutions and residents form different interpretations.

Phase C: authored failure event.

A slope failure occurs only if the story/world system decides it. First reports know less than later assessments. Road, Crisis, utility and conservation systems receive separate handoffs.

Phase D: temporary geography.

Detours, closed paths, alternate service points and changed travel routines create new relationships. The slope itself can become inaccessible while nearby life continues.

Phase E: clearing and evidence.

Debris removal, mapping, slope assessment and possible stabilization occur separately. Conflicting documents become playable evidence.

Phase F: partial recovery.

One connection may reopen while another stays restricted. Businesses and households decide whether to return to old routines. Habitat changes remain.

Phase G: legacy.

A later season or generation encounters the abandoned alignment, observation station, changed stream edge, memorial or recurring Pokémon behavior. Earlier player choices and records affect how the new situation is understood.

## Encounter — Switchback Withdrawal

Full intended form:

A conflict happens while staff/travelers withdraw from a recently restricted switchback. Designers may eventually want falling-rock/exclusion zones, route loss, reactions, Intercept, forced movement and AI that values retreat/protection.

Capability dependencies:

- targeting/footprints/range/LoS — VERIFIED;
- base movement legality — VERIFIED;
- complete movement including push/pull/knockback/interception/forced movement — PARTIAL;
- core calculations — VERIFIED;
- action economy/initiative — VERIFIED;
- full turn/round lifecycle — PARTIAL;
- full stateful damage pipeline — PARTIAL if debris can damage;
- status lifecycle — PARTIAL if a legal condition applies;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions — BLOCKING;
- move-specific behavior — PARTIAL;
- abilities — PARTIAL;
- items — PARTIAL;
- Trainer Features/perks — PARTIAL;
- AI legal-action infrastructure — VERIFIED;
- AI tactical policy — BLOCKING;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback — BLOCKING.

Reduced current form:

Finish the noncombat withdrawal first. Exclude the unstable slope and active debris from BattleSpec. Use a fixed inspected turnout. No dynamic rockfall, collapse, sliding or changing cells. Victory secures only the immediate safe edge.

## Encounter — Survey Marker Perimeter

Full intended form:

A survey/monitoring team needs protection or controlled withdrawal near an observation point. Dynamic restricted cells and technical equipment could matter later.

Main blockers:

- complete movement remains PARTIAL;
- generalized environment/zones/reactions remain BLOCKING;
- objective-aware AI policy remains BLOCKING;
- adapter/playback remains BLOCKING.

Reduced current form:

Move staff and instruments outside the battle before resolution. Use a stable static clearing. The encounter result cannot validate measurements, operate equipment, repair monitoring or change the slope assessment.

## Encounter — Debris Fan Diversion

Full intended form:

A later conflict occurs across the deposit left by an old slope event. Designers might eventually want loose footing, dust, mud, rolling material or route-changing flow.

Those additions explicitly require environmental zones/reactions, and may also require full movement, turn lifecycle, damage/status and exact Move/Ability/Item/Trainer Feature interactions.

Reduced current form:

The debris fan is stable ordinary geometry with no special tactical behavior. Its narrative value comes from history, route continuity, ecology and evidence.

## Minecraft / Cobblemon presentation candidates

Safe presentation candidates, subject to API review:

- persistent scars or debris geometry;
- old/new road alignments;
- barriers and signage;
- survey markers and huts;
- crews and NPC routines;
- wild Pokémon reclaiming closed routes;
- photographs/maps/UI;
- particles and sounds that do not imply mechanics.

Never allow native falling blocks, gravel physics, water spread, suffocation, fall damage, knockback or redstone to become PTU authority.

## Canon questions generated by these seeds

The proposals deliberately leave open:

- which Ouros regions actually have meaningful slope-instability history;
- whether major settlements have been relocated or routes permanently replaced;
- which institutions monitor or assess slopes;
- how local and technical terminology differ;
- what access authority exists;
- which stabilization technologies belong in setting;
- how abandoned routes are reused;
- which individual Pokémon have recurring documented relationships with slope sites;
- what exact PTU/Caelo environmental mechanics, if any, should be authored for specific locations.

# Ouros Fluvial Geomorphology & Channel Migration Seeds — Pass 114

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Requires originality, continuity, PTU/Caelo and implementation review before promotion.

Pass: 114.

These candidates assume the proposed Fluvial Geomorphology & Channel Migration layer. They do not establish any named Ouros river, property rule, habitat or battle mechanic.

## 1. The Bridge Over Dry Ground

An old stone bridge is still maintained even though the main river shifted into a newer channel decades ago.

Possible threads:

- heritage value;
- emergency overflow route;
- old property descriptions;
- tourism;
- wildlife using the abandoned channel.

The bridge is not automatically obsolete.

## 2. Two Maps, Two Correct Rivers

A survey map from fifteen years ago and a current atlas show the main channel in different places.

Both are accurate for their dates.

## 3. The Island That Wasn't There

A gravel bar appears after a series of high-flow seasons and gradually becomes vegetated enough to be treated locally as an island.

Its new physical persistence does not establish ownership or public access.

## 4. The Road That Ends in Water

A historical road trace on an old map now terminates at the active bank because the river migrated laterally.

A later expedition can still use the old alignment as evidence of the former landscape.

## 5. The Oxbow Nursery

A former river bend becomes seasonally isolated and starts functioning as a quiet nursery area for an aquatic population.

Conservation significance develops after the channel was abandoned.

## 6. The Oxbow Nobody Recognized

Residents call a crescent wetland “the old pond.” Archive maps reveal that it was once the main river.

The discovery changes local history, not automatically land rights.

## 7. The Ferry Landing That Silted Up

A ferry still appears in institutional schedules, but its traditional landing became too shallow after bar growth.

The service can move to a new landing without erasing the old one from public memory.

## 8. Three Banks in Thirty Years

A riverside property has three survey markers from different decades, each placed beside the bank position of its time.

The correct legal interpretation remains a Land Tenure question.

## 9. The New Channel Started as a Ditch

An overflow swale first documented after a flood is ignored for years. Later surveys show it carrying more and more flow until it becomes a secondary channel.

Players can witness a long transition instead of one dramatic cutscene.

## 10. The Cutoff Night

A high-water event finally links two ends of a meander and shortens the main channel.

The event creates a new travel problem immediately but ecological consequences emerge over following seasons.

## 11. The Bank Eroded, the Shrine Did Not

A culturally important site remains intact while the bank approaches it over several years.

The story is about monitoring, access and choices before a crisis exists.

## 12. The Orchard on the Point Bar

A productive orchard occupies land that formed through older deposition.

New erosion begins on the opposite bank and shifts local assumptions about which side is “stable.”

## 13. The Bar Where Everyone Camps

A seasonal gravel bar becomes a popular informal campsite because it is exposed every dry season.

One year it does not emerge.

No one should infer permanent loss from a single season.

## 14. The Side Channel Returns

A side channel considered abandoned reconnects after several ordinary high-flow events rather than one catastrophic flood.

Its return reopens a fish/Pokémon movement path and closes a footpath.

## 15. The Photograph With No Island

A famous historic photograph shows a river view with no island. Modern visitors accuse the image of being taken elsewhere.

Archive dates prove the island formed later.

## 16. The Island Split in Two

A vegetated island survives for decades, then a flood opens a new thread through its center.

The same persistent island record gains a geometry revision rather than becoming two unrelated locations immediately.

## 17. The Fallen Trees That Built a Bar

Wood accumulation coincides with sediment deposition and a new shallow area.

The exact causal role remains a scientific claim until investigated.

## 18. The Sand Everyone Blamed on the Quarry

A downstream bar grows after quarry expansion upstream.

Sediment provenance evidence later shows multiple possible sources, including a wildfire-affected tributary.

No automatic blame.

## 19. The Ruin Under the Cut Bank

Bank migration exposes masonry that was previously buried in floodplain sediment.

Archaeology gets a new site because of erosion, but exposure also threatens preservation.

## 20. The House That Never Flooded

A riverside house has survived every flood in living memory, but the channel has slowly migrated toward it.

Flood history and erosion risk are not the same thing.

## 21. The Backwater Became a Wetland

An abandoned channel loses regular connection to the main stem and gradually develops wetland vegetation.

Freshwater, Flora and Conservation all gain new state while the former channel history stays visible.

## 22. The Same Pokémon, Different Bank

A persistent wild Pokémon is repeatedly photographed near the river across several years.

Later images place it on what appears to be the “opposite side,” but the river moved between observations.

Do not infer a dramatic crossing event without evidence.

## 23. The Survey Marker in the River

A durable marker that once stood on dry ground is now surrounded by shallow water after bank migration.

Its position is evidence of change, not proof the survey was wrong.

## 24. The New Beach Upstream

A sandy depositional bar becomes locally known as a “river beach.”

Tourism pressure arrives before scientists know whether the feature will persist for more than a few seasons.

## 25. The Abandoned Bridge Pier

One bridge pier remains in the former channel after a replacement bridge follows the new course.

It becomes a landmark, roost site and historical navigation reference.

## 26. The River That Crossed the Boundary

A boundary description follows a named river. Decades later, the active channel is hundreds of meters away from the original surveyed line.

The physical fact is clear. The ownership consequence is intentionally unresolved.

## 27. The Levee Protected Yesterday's River

An old levee remains structurally sound, but the active thread now approaches from a different angle.

Infrastructure inspection must distinguish structure condition from present usefulness.

## 28. The Flood That Created Five Years of Change

A single large event scours an overflow path. Nothing dramatic happens immediately afterward.

Over the next five years, smaller floods enlarge it until it becomes the dominant thread.

## 29. The River Atlas Has Transparent Pages

A regional archive publishes a layered atlas showing channel revisions by decade rather than choosing one “true” historic river line.

It becomes useful to researchers, planners, travelers and storytellers.

## 30. The Quiet Reach Starts Moving Again

A reach considered stable for generations begins migrating after an upstream sediment/flow change.

The cause may involve several systems and should remain open until evidence supports one explanation.

# Long Arc A — Five Bends of Meridian River

Year 1:
A routine survey documents slow erosion at one outside bend and growth of a point bar opposite it.

Year 2:
A high-flow season cuts a shallow overflow path across the floodplain. The main channel remains unchanged.

Year 3:
The overflow path carries water more often. A foot trail is rerouted and new vegetation establishes on the old bar.

Year 4:
A smaller flood enlarges the shortcut. A ferry landing becomes unreliable and an abandoned-channel habitat begins forming.

Year 5:
The main thread finally shifts. Historical maps, property descriptions, transport routes and habitat records all need revision, but none are erased.

Purpose:
Show geomorphic change as accumulated Chronicle state rather than a single scripted disaster.

# Long Arc B — The Oxbow Years

Stage 1:
A dramatic meander remains part of the main river and supports a busy crossing.

Stage 2:
A cutoff forms. The old bend remains connected at one end.

Stage 3:
Sedimentation reduces connection. Fishing, wildlife and visitor patterns change.

Stage 4:
The former channel functions as a seasonal wetland/oxbow system.

Stage 5:
A restoration or flood event partially reconnects it, creating a new ecological question rather than returning to an old snapshot.

Purpose:
Give one former channel a multi-decade identity across Freshwater, Fisheries, Conservation, Cartography and Public Memory.

# Long Arc C — The River and the Town

Year 1:
A town treats the riverbank as a stable edge because all modern buildings were designed around the current channel.

Year 2:
Archive research reveals that older structures once faced a different channel alignment.

Year 3:
Bank migration starts affecting a park, ferry landing and buried utility crossing.

Year 4:
The town debates retreat, reinforcement, alternate crossings and habitat restoration. Different institutions support different legitimate priorities.

Year 5:
A new physical revision tests those decisions. Some work well. Others produce tradeoffs elsewhere.

Purpose:
Make river change part of urban history, governance and ecology without requiring a villain.

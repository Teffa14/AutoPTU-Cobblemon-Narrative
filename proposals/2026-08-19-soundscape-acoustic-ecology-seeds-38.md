# Ouros Soundscape & Acoustic Ecology Seeds — Pass 38

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research patterns. No source plot, dialogue, character or melody is imported.

## 1. The Missing Dawn Chorus

A route with years of early-morning call records suddenly becomes much quieter for several days.

Possible causes remain open:
- migration timing changed;
- weather shifted activity later;
- a food source moved;
- construction disturbance changed where a collective roosts;
- monitoring equipment failed;
- observers simply sampled the wrong window.

The hook begins with absence, not a monster attack.

## 2. The Old Voice Stations

Stone platforms along a mountain route were once used as long-distance signaling points. Modern residents disagree on whether Pokémon were routinely involved, whether the platforms were civic infrastructure or ceremonial spaces, and why the network stopped being used.

The sites can connect archaeology, media history and travel without copying any canon city's communication system.

## 3. Three Calls at the Quarry

Workers report the same three-part call from different parts of an inactive quarry every night.

Researchers disagree whether it is:
- one mobile Pokémon;
- several individuals using call-and-response;
- an echo from a fixed source;
- machinery cycling after shutdown;
- two unrelated sounds being grouped together by witnesses.

The investigation should support multiple observation points before any conclusion.

## 4. Harbor Without Horns

A harbor's acoustic identity changes when a long-running transport operator retires its old signaling equipment.

Nothing is mechanically wrong, but residents, wild Pokémon and visitors all react differently to the changed routine. The story can become about memory, accessibility, navigation or whether a new warning system preserves enough of the old audible cues.

## 5. Night Market Quiet Hour

A market district introduces a late-night quiet period after nearby residents and a seasonal nesting population both become points of concern.

The conflict is not framed as nightlife versus nature. Traders, performers, residents, transport workers and conservation staff all have legitimate interests.

## 6. The Wrong Alarm

A public warning signal sounds during clear weather. Several actors respond correctly to the signal, but the event turns out to be a maintenance fault.

The useful consequence is not embarrassment; it is discovering which institutions, routes and people actually respond when the signal activates.

## 7. Recording 17-B

A research archive contains a recording labeled as a rare species call. The waveform and background noise suggest it was captured in a different location than the label claims.

Possible explanations include clerical error, reused equipment, misdated media or an actual range anomaly. Fraud is not assumed.

## 8. The Bell That Moved

Travelers use a distant bell as an informal route landmark. After public works relocate the bell tower, old directions remain in circulation and begin sending visitors toward the wrong trail junction.

The quest connects public memory, communications and travel knowledge.

## 9. Roost Under the Viaduct

A wild collective establishes a roost beneath a transport structure. Trains or vehicles already existed before the roost arrived, so the question is not simply whether traffic is "disturbing nature."

The long-term story can involve timetable changes, structural inspection, alternative roosting structures and observation of whether the collective adapts.

## 10. The Festival That Lost Its Sound

A regional festival continues after an instrument-making workshop closes. Organizers can still hold the event, but one traditional acoustic element disappears.

Possible responses:
- commission a new local maker;
- document the old practice without pretending it never changed;
- adopt a different tradition;
- invite a neighboring community;
- let the festival evolve naturally.

No magical consequence is attached to preserving the old sound.

## 11. Calls Through the Fog

Coastal travelers recognize a recurring wild call that often coincides with safe shallow-water activity. One season the calls occur farther offshore.

The observation may become an ecological clue, but the game must not claim the Pokémon are intentionally guiding humans without evidence.

## 12. The Machine With a New Rhythm

A workshop machine still functions, but workers notice its cycle sounds different. The technical system records a degraded-state possibility before failure occurs.

The hook can lead to preventive maintenance rather than catastrophe.

## 13. The Listening Bench

A simple bench near a wetland becomes an unofficial place where residents and researchers compare observations. Over time, the location accumulates a community call library and seasonal notes.

The bench can become important without becoming a formal institution.

## 14. False Silence

A conservation team reports an unexpected silence in a forest sector. A later audit shows that strong wind masked the relevant calls during the observation window.

The result becomes a useful null/quality-control record rather than a failed quest.

## 15. The Repeated Knock

Residents of an old apartment building report a rhythmic knocking inside one wall at the same time each evening.

Potential causes include:
- pipes;
- thermal expansion;
- a small Pokémon using a void in the structure;
- a maintenance cycle;
- neighboring activity.

The system must keep "heard in the wall" separate from "Pokémon trapped in the wall" until evidence exists.

## 16. A Pokémon Recognized by Voice

A persistent wild Pokémon is recognized by a distinctive call pattern before anyone can see it clearly.

The identification remains probabilistic until visual, tracking or other corroboration exists. If confirmed, the call becomes part of that individual's persistent profile.

## 17. The Quiet Clinic Wing

A care facility creates a low-noise recovery area. The story is about scheduling, visitors, nearby construction and facility design.

The quiet policy has no automatic healing bonus.

## 18. The Siren Map

An emergency-planning team discovers that the region's warning system was designed around old settlement boundaries. New neighborhoods and a recently expanded harbor may not receive the same audible coverage.

The solution can involve tests, visual alerts, communication channels and infrastructure upgrades rather than simply making the siren louder.

## 19. The Cave That Answers Back

Explorers use short test sounds to understand a cavern's geometry. Different branches produce clearly different echo patterns.

The full version can eventually use spatial audio. The reduced version exposes equivalent visual waveform symbols and route notes so hearing ability never blocks progression.

## 20. The Unknown Evening Caller

A new call appears around sunset near three farms. Residents immediately disagree over whether it signals a predator, a rare migrant or a harmless species.

The generator should produce observations first, claims second and encounters only when world state justifies one.

## 21. Workshop Noise Treaty

Two neighboring workshops share a courtyard. One begins operating later hours to meet a seasonal commission backlog, conflicting with the other's training classes and nearby residences.

The problem can resolve through schedules, acoustic barriers, relocation of specific tasks or acceptance of a temporary busy period.

## 22. The Broken Public Piano

A public instrument in a settlement square becomes physically damaged. Different groups value it as a performance venue, meeting point, memorial object or simply a piece of street furniture.

Repair decisions should use Material Culture, Civic and Public Memory state. The instrument grants no buffs.

## 23. The Call Archive Dispute

Two institutions have recordings of the same species from different decades. Their classification schemes differ, making the datasets appear contradictory.

The real task is reconciliation of metadata and versions, not proving one institution incompetent.

## 24. Night Freight

A new overnight freight schedule changes the soundscape of a residential edge district. Some residents complain; nearby scavenging Pokémon alter their routines; shops benefit from earlier deliveries.

The world can track actual observed responses rather than assigning a universal "noise pollution" stat.

## 25. The Signal Keeper

A retired route worker knows the meaning and failure patterns of several old audible signals that no longer appear in current manuals.

Their knowledge is institutional memory, not supernatural intuition. Training a successor can become a workplace/archives story.

## 26. Echoes in the Memorial Grove

Visitors report hearing a recurring cry in a memorial grove after dark. The Loss/Memorial layer protects the interpretation boundary: a Ghost-type or other Pokémon may be present, but no postmortem identity is inferred from the sound.

## 27. Community Sound Map

Players contribute optional recordings and notes to a regional map of acoustic landmarks.

Possible entries:
- waterfall audible from a ridge;
- seasonal insect chorus;
- ferry signal;
- factory shift bell;
- morning market setup;
- unusual but unverified call.

Contributions retain author, timestamp and location provenance.

## 28. Accessible Ruin Puzzle

A ruin contains resonant stones that produce distinct tones when activated.

The puzzle can be solved through:
- sound;
- visible pulse patterns;
- vibration indicators;
- an inscription that maps symbols to positions.

All modes expose the same sequence logic. No player is required to distinguish pitch by ear.

## 29. The Listening Season

A conservation program temporarily reduces visitor traffic through a valley to compare call activity with prior years.

The result may show no meaningful difference. A null result is still valuable and can influence future policy.

## 30. The Broadcast Nobody Heard

A public audio announcement is correctly transmitted from a station but several remote areas never receive it because local speakers are offline.

This creates a clean example of source event, transmission, physical playback and actor receipt being separate states.

# Long Arc A — The Changing Valley

A valley's soundscape shifts over several seasons.

Arc progression:
1. players help establish a baseline through field observations;
2. a new transport connection changes human activity;
3. one wild collective changes its timing;
4. another expected call disappears temporarily;
5. public debate assumes the new route caused every change;
6. long-term evidence reveals multiple interacting causes;
7. the region chooses targeted mitigation rather than a single dramatic closure.

The arc can finish without proving one villain or one universal ecological cause.

# Long Arc B — The Lost Signal Network

Old acoustic relay sites appear across several regions of Ouros.

Arc progression:
1. one station is discovered during unrelated travel;
2. archives preserve conflicting explanations of its purpose;
3. an old signal pattern survives in local custom;
4. a second station proves the network was larger than expected;
5. players help document or restore selected sites;
6. the final decision is whether the network should remain historical, return as civic infrastructure or be preserved only in parts.

No canon explanation of the original network is set by this proposal.

# Long Arc C — The Region Learns to Listen

A loose set of researchers, residents, route workers and conservation groups gradually build a shared acoustic observation network.

Arc progression:
1. observations begin as disconnected local notes;
2. players discover metadata inconsistencies;
3. standards for recordings and privacy improve;
4. the network catches a genuine seasonal anomaly;
5. a public scare demonstrates the danger of publishing uncertain species locations too early;
6. governance creates a policy for sensitive records;
7. the network becomes a durable world institution whose data can generate future investigations.

The player legacy is an institution and dataset, not a one-time boss victory.

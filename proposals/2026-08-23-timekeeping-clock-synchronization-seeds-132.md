# Ouros Candidates — Pass 132: Timekeeping, Clocks & Synchronization

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Nothing in this file is established Ouros lore.
Date: 2026-08-23

These candidates use the Pass 132 research and systems layer. They are designed to create persistent history through clocks, schedules, records and synchronization without turning ordinary discrepancies into time-travel plots.

## 30 worldbuilding and quest candidates

### 1. The Clock Four Minutes Fast

A station clock is deliberately kept several minutes ahead by local custom, while the railway timetable uses the official operating reference. Visitors assume the train departed early. The investigation is about schedule interpretation and institutional habit, not sabotage.

### 2. Two Correct Midnights

Two archive records assign the same storm to different calendar dates because their settlements used different historical rollover conventions. Both documents are internally consistent.

### 3. The Observatory Drift Year

An old observatory instrument recorded excellent scientific data during a year when its clock slowly drifted. Modern researchers can recover much of the series by comparing it against another station, but not every timestamp can be resolved precisely.

### 4. Last Train by the Old Clock

A rural station preserves a mechanical clock after the regional rail network changes its operating standard. The clock remains a landmark, but its face no longer matches the timetable.

### 5. The Bell at Local Noon

A town bell continues to ring according to an old civic convention even after transport and communications adopt another reference. Residents understand both systems; newcomers do not.

### 6. Three Cameras, One Migration

Three camera traps record the first migration wave at apparently conflicting times. One camera was recently synchronized, one was offline, and one has an uncertain offset. The ecological observation may still be valid even if exact sequence remains uncertain.

### 7. The Ferry Log That Crossed Midnight

A ferry departs late under one local time convention and arrives under another. A later case file appears to show a negative travel duration until the original time references are reconstructed.

### 8. The Clock Tower Without Authority

A famous city clock is the most visible clock in the district but is maintained ceremonially and is not the official reference for transport, science or emergency services.

### 9. The Schedule Printed Before the Change

A batch of public timetables was printed before a standard revision took effect. They are genuine documents, not forgeries, but become stale on a known date.

### 10. The Relay That Kept Bad Time Well

A communications relay loses its upstream timing source and continues running from its local clock. Its logs remain orderly while drifting gradually away from the regional reference.

### 11. The Museum Label With an Impossible Time

A historic photograph’s label gives a time that appears impossible relative to a documented train arrival. The photograph may have been timestamped under an older local convention rather than mislabeled.

### 12. The Tournament Registration Minute

A challenger submits a registration at the deadline. Two systems disagree by less than a minute. Institutional Review must decide what evidence and rule version govern eligibility without treating clock precision as combat skill.

### 13. The Clinic Handoff Gap

Two clinical records describe a transfer in the opposite order because one ward’s clock had not resynchronized after a power interruption. The medical facts remain under Care/Health authorities.

### 14. The Festival That Starts When the Shadow Reaches the Stone

A traditional observance still uses a locally observed marker while transport, vendors and emergency services use clock time. The ceremonial start and operational schedule are related but distinct.

### 15. The Weather Station With Two Clocks

A weather station has one clock for automated instruments and another wall clock for staff. A historical notebook uses whichever was visible to the observer. Later climate work must separate those sources.

### 16. The Missing Seven Minutes

A public incident video and an emergency dispatch log disagree by seven minutes. Neither record is altered. Their clocks were simply synchronized at different times.

### 17. The Railway Standard Reaches the Valley

A valley with several local conventions gradually adopts one transport standard. Old shops, bells, archives and family schedules continue to preserve earlier time language for decades.

### 18. The Quiet Clockmaker

A clock repairer becomes an important recurring NPC because they know which institutions changed standards, which towers were rebuilt and which records should not be compared directly. They do not receive Technology Education or mechanical bonuses automatically.

### 19. The Broken Clock That Became a Memorial

A public clock stopped during a historic flood and was later preserved at that reading. The displayed time is memorial state, not current time and not necessarily the exact moment of the event.

### 20. The Expedition With No Shared Clock

An old expedition used several personal watches without a common synchronization event. Their notebooks can reconstruct broad sequence but not exact minute-by-minute ordering.

### 21. The Radio Time Signal Goes Silent

A region loses a timing broadcast while normal voice communication partly survives. Some institutions switch to fallback references. The world does not stop; confidence in later cross-system timestamps changes.

### 22. The Digital Board Is Right, the Wall Clock Is Right

A station display uses operational railway time. The heritage wall clock shows the town’s local convention. Both are labeled correctly, but an outdated visitor guide explains neither.

### 23. The Migratory Window Moved, the Clock Did Not

A wildlife observation tradition historically began at a fixed hour. Over years, migration timing shifts while the institution keeps the same schedule. The mismatch belongs to Migration/Climate, not clock failure.

### 24. The Sun Dial After the New Building

A historic sundial remains physically intact after a nearby building changes its sunlight exposure. It becomes an architectural and heritage object rather than a reliable everyday clock.

### 25. The Clock That Was Reset During Evacuation

A responder manually resets a station clock after a power outage to support operations. Later investigators must preserve that intervention instead of assuming all post-outage timestamps share one continuous clock history.

### 26. The Market’s Five-Minute Tradition

A recurring market informally opens when the old square clock strikes, while official vendor permits use another reference. The small difference becomes socially meaningful without needing legal drama.

### 27. The League Archive Converts Old Times

A long-running battle institution publishes modernized timestamps for historic matches while preserving the raw original schedule notation. Researchers can view both.

### 28. The Clock Network Modernization

Several settlements gradually replace standalone clocks with a synchronized service. The story is mostly normal infrastructure work: surveys, outages, maintenance and archival conversion.

### 29. The Timestamp That Saved the Wrong Theory

A scientific hypothesis appears strongly supported only because an old sensor’s clock offset was ignored. Correcting the timestamps weakens the theory without proving a replacement explanation.

### 30. Nothing Happened at 03:17

A cluster of systems briefly reported strange timestamp differences. The eventual conclusion is a mundane synchronization fault. This candidate exists to protect Ouros from turning every unexplained clock issue into a temporal anomaly.

## Long-form arc 1 — Five Standards at Meridian Junction

Year 1: rail, ferry, market and civic clocks use partially different conventions.

Year 2: a missed transfer exposes the operational cost of ambiguity.

Year 3: institutions agree on a shared transport reference while public landmarks keep local displays.

Year 4: archives begin publishing conversion notes for older records.

Year 5: a major emergency tests whether dispatch, rail and hospital logs can be reconciled across the new system.

The arc can end with partial standardization rather than one universal clock.

## Long-form arc 2 — The Observatory Clock Archive

A regional observatory preserves generations of instrument logs whose clocks were maintained differently. Each research season improves the mapping between old timestamps and current reference time.

Possible connections:

- Astronomy;
- Meteorology;
- Climate Baselines;
- Metrology;
- Libraries/Archives;
- Photography;
- Migration.

The payoff is better historical sequence, not a hidden prophecy.

## Long-form arc 3 — The Valley That Changed Its Time

Three settlements historically use different local practices. Rail, radio and emergency coordination gradually create pressure for a common operational reference. Local traditions do not disappear; they become explicitly labeled contexts.

The arc can create:

- old/new clock towers;
- guidebook revisions;
- station signage changes;
- oral-history disagreements that become understandable;
- public resistance based on practical or cultural concerns rather than villainy;
- preserved local customs alongside shared operational time.

## Encounter contract 1 — Clocktower Relay Access

Narrative premise:

A timing relay lost synchronization during a communications outage. A technician needs access while a separate wildlife disturbance blocks the route.

FULL version:

- full turn/round lifecycle — PARTIAL;
- full stateful damage pipeline — PARTIAL;
- status lifecycle — PARTIAL if statuses occur;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions — BLOCKING if live environmental/reaction behavior is used;
- move-specific behavior — PARTIAL;
- abilities — PARTIAL;
- items — PARTIAL;
- Trainer Features/perks — PARTIAL;
- AI legal-action infrastructure — VERIFIED;
- AI tactical policy — BLOCKING for `PROTECT_TECHNICIAN` / `CLEAR_ROUTE`;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback — BLOCKING.

REDUCED version:

Resolve technician movement in overworld state. Freeze a static legal arena around the wildlife conflict. After AutoPTU resolves the battle, the technician checks the relay and records a synchronization event. Battle victory never sets the clock automatically.

## Encounter contract 2 — Station Platform Time Dispute

Narrative premise:

Two platform displays disagree. Passengers begin moving toward the wrong boarding point while a separate Pokémon incident creates a safety problem.

FULL version:

Key blockers:

- complete movement/interception for crowds and staff;
- AI tactical policy for `CLEAR_ROUTE`, `WITHDRAW`, `PROTECT`;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics playback for platforms, crowds and route semantics;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions only if an independently validated tactical hazard exists.

REDUCED version:

Rail staff halt boarding and clear the platform before battle. AutoPTU receives only actual combatants on a static map. The timestamp/schedule discrepancy is resolved later from logs and clock state.

## Encounter contract 3 — Observatory Timestamp Reconciliation

Narrative premise:

Three instruments recorded one celestial event under clocks that disagree.

Primary mode:

Non-combat investigation using Astronomy, Metrology, Science and Timekeeping.

Possible outcomes:

- one clock had a known offset;
- two series can be aligned within an uncertainty window;
- exact ordering remains unresolved;
- the apparent anomaly disappears;
- a separate physical anomaly remains after ordinary timing errors are removed.

No battle capability is required unless an independent encounter occurs.

## Mechanical guardrails

These proposals do not create:

- initiative bonuses for synchronized actors;
- extra actions for precise timing;
- Speed bonuses from clocks;
- timing-based Accuracy modifiers;
- automatic Trick Room effects;
- delayed-Move manipulation;
- time-stop or rewind mechanics;
- Legendary time intervention;
- schedule-based capture bonuses;
- day/night combat effects not already validated by PTU/Caelo and AutoPTU.

## Canon questions raised by the proposals

- Which settlements use shared versus local time conventions?
- Who maintains trusted references?
- Which clocks are heritage objects rather than operational standards?
- Does Ouros accelerate world time relative to real time?
- What precision do ordinary institutions actually need?
- How are historical timestamps shown to players?
- Which schedules advance when no players are online?
- How much synchronization technology exists before the campaign begins?

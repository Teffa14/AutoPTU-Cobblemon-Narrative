# Ouros Homes, Housing & Neighborhood Seeds — Pass 23

Status: NON-CANON proposals. Original Ouros candidates derived from structural research themes. No source plot, character or dialogue is imported.

## 1. The Room Above the Workshop

A young artisan lives in a small room above the workshop where they apprentice. The workshop changes ownership, but their residence agreement does not automatically change with it. The resulting story can involve uncertainty, negotiation and continuity without assuming any specific property law.

Useful links:
- material culture;
- mentorship;
- household/access state;
- public memory.

## 2. A Home Everyone Knows but Nobody Owns

A remote field cabin has served generations of explorers. Multiple institutions maintain it informally, and nobody has a clean ownership record. Its practical stewardship matters more than title until a future civic decision requires a formal answer.

## 3. The New Neighbor Is an Old Rival

A recurring rival moves into the same neighborhood for reasons unrelated to the player. Their proximity creates ordinary encounters, shared local issues and opportunities for changed context without forcing friendship or conflict.

## 4. The Empty House Has Become Habitat

A long-vacant residence is now regularly used by wild Pokémon. A proposed renovation creates tension between housing need, neighborhood safety and the new ecological reality.

No side is automatically correct.

## 5. The Guest Room That Became Permanent

A temporary guest remains for months because a research project, recovery period or transport disruption keeps extending. The system records extended residence without inferring adoption, family membership or permanent tenancy.

## 6. The Moving Trophy Wall

A veteran Trainer has displayed trophies, photos and mundane keepsakes across several homes. When they relocate, they choose which objects travel, which remain and which become public donations. The object provenance system carries the history forward.

## 7. A House With Two Public Faces

The front room is a tiny neighborhood clinic or repair service. The rest remains private residence. Visitors can access one part without being granted access to the household.

## 8. The Neighborhood Notice Table

A small shared table near several homes accumulates handwritten notices, lost-item notes, transport changes and local requests. It is a neighborhood-scale information channel, not a universal quest board.

## 9. Return to a Former Home

A character returns years later to a residence now occupied by someone else. The building remembers physical changes and public history, but the new occupant has their own rights, routines and relationship to the place.

## 10. The House That Stayed Closed After the Crisis

A building survived a regional crisis but never reopened. The reason is unknown. Possible causes must emerge from evidence: absent resident, unresolved damage, personal choice, institutional restriction or simple relocation.

## 11. Pokémon at the Window

A specific wild Pokémon begins appearing regularly outside one home. The system records repeated visitation. It does not infer ownership, affection or desire to be captured.

This can evolve through observation, feeding rules if legal, ecology, or later disappearance.

## 12. Shared Garden, Different Priorities

Several households use one courtyard garden. One wants vegetables, one wants native plants, one wants a safe play space, and wild Pokémon already use part of it. The conflict is small, local and persistent.

## 13. The Night-Shift Household

Several residents work at times when most of the neighborhood sleeps. Their routines change which NPCs and businesses are visible at night and create different social opportunities without needing a dedicated night questline.

## 14. Temporary Housing After Route Closure

Travelers become long-term temporary residents when a major route is unusable. Local lodging, spare rooms and institutions absorb some of them. Their presence changes demand, social life and public discussion until transport recovers.

## 15. The Clubhouse Without a Club

An old clubhouse survives after its original organization dissolves. Former members disagree about whether it should become a new club space, museum, residence, workshop or ordinary public building.

## 16. The Home That Grew Around a Pokémon

A resident gradually modifies a property around the legal needs and capabilities of a long-term partner Pokémon. The changes are physical and narrative. They do not grant new PTU capability values.

## 17. A Visiting Performer Circuit

A household regularly hosts traveling performers during a regional circuit. The same guest room accumulates years of signatures, gifts and stories from different visitors.

## 18. The Address Nobody Updated

A player moved months ago, but one institution still sends messages to the old address. This creates believable information delay without assuming magical universal contact synchronization.

## 19. Neighborhood Reputation Without a Score

A street becomes known for researchers, artisans, battlers or food stalls because of who actually lives and works there. The reputation is a public claim built from visible state, not a numeric buff.

## 20. The House Built in Stages

A modest residence expands only when the player's life changes: a workshop corner, a guest room, a small archive, a habitat-safe yard. Each stage comes from actual need rather than a generic housing-level ladder.

## 21. The Door That Used to Be Public

A room once functioned as a club meeting space. The club relocates, and the room becomes private again. Old NPCs may still arrive expecting access until the information propagates.

## 22. The Mobile Home Returns

A long-distance expedition team uses a mobile base. When it returns to town, it temporarily becomes a social hub full of people, equipment, samples and stories, then leaves again.

## 23. Two Homes, One Character

A character spends significant time in two regions and has legitimate residence in both. The system should support multiple residences without forcing one to become the "real" home.

## 24. The Family House Without Invented Family Drama

A multigenerational home exists in canon, but current relationships inside it remain only as authored facts. The generator can use shared history, room changes and household events without inventing estrangement, inheritance disputes or favoritism.

## 25. A Neighborhood Reoriented by Transit

A new transport stop changes foot traffic. Some homes become noisier, a café gains customers, another route becomes quieter, and wild Pokémon respond to the changed human movement. No single quest caused all outcomes.

## 26. The Smallest Safe Place

A character with no permanent home repeatedly returns to the same rented room or bunk between expeditions. Over time, a few persistent objects and relationships make the place narratively meaningful despite its temporary status.

## 27. Housewarming as Social State

A player invites selected NPCs or PCs after moving. Attendance creates witnessed shared events and conversation opportunities. It does not automatically increase friendship or reputation.

## 28. Former Villain, Ordinary Neighbor

A former member of a hostile organization relocates into an ordinary neighborhood after a resolved arc. Some residents know their past, some do not, and others only know rumors. Their new home does not erase prior responsibility or guarantee acceptance.

## 29. The Workshop Fire That Did Not Become a Boss Fight

A small accidental fire damages part of a residence/workshop. The meaningful play is evacuation, repairs, supply disruption and community response. Combat occurs only if an independently valid encounter intersects the event.

## 30. Home Archive

A player can designate a small display/archive area for meaningful objects, field notes, photographs or trophies. Each item remains linked to its provenance and Chronicle events. The archive is for memory, not stat bonuses.

# Longer Arc A — The Changing Block

Several independent state changes accumulate in one neighborhood over a season:

1. a workshop changes operator;
2. a resident moves away;
3. a wild group begins using a vacant yard;
4. a transport stop opens nearby;
5. a new household arrives;
6. an old public room becomes private;
7. a local event brings temporary visitors.

No mastermind exists. The arc is about a place changing because many ordinary systems intersect.

Possible player roles:
- resident;
- visitor;
- researcher;
- artisan;
- club member;
- battler;
- civic participant;
- neighbor.

# Longer Arc B — Coming Home Changed

A player spends a long expedition away from their residence.

During the absence:
- neighbors continue routines;
- publications arrive;
- a local business changes;
- one public project advances;
- a wild population shifts;
- a friend or rival may move nearby if supported by their own state.

On return, the home is recognizable but the neighborhood is not frozen. The system should present a few meaningful changes rather than dumping every elapsed event at once.

# Longer Arc C — The Settlement of Many Homes

A newer Ouros settlement develops from individual residences instead of a top-down town upgrade tree.

Over time:
- temporary cabins become stable homes;
- homes begin hosting services;
- pathways become public routes;
- shared water or power becomes infrastructure;
- informal meeting places become institutions;
- neighborhoods gain distinct identities;
- some early residents leave;
- later arrivals inherit a town with visible history.

The settlement layer owns the city-scale result. The housing layer preserves the personal-scale history underneath it.

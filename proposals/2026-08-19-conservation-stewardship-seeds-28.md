# Pass 28 — Conservation & stewardship seeds

Status: NON-CANON. Original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research patterns. No external plot, character, location or prose is transplanted.

## 1. The Corridor That Became a Road

A newly improved route has become the fastest connection between two settlements. Long-term field reports show that a seasonal wild collective also crosses the same bottleneck. The problem is not choosing "people or Pokémon" but finding a schedule, crossing design, reroute or management compromise that actually works.

## 2. The Urban Refuge

A dense district contains a deliberately preserved strip of habitat used by several wild species. Nearby residents value it, merchants dislike delivery restrictions, researchers want more monitoring access, and some Trainers assume it is simply another catching area. The story grows from incompatible expectations around one small place.

## 3. The Empty Nesting Ledge

A protected nesting ledge is unexpectedly empty during the usual season. The first quest is observation and comparison with older records. Disturbance, food availability, weather, migration timing and simple normal variation remain possible until evidence narrows them.

## 4. The Visitor Rule Nobody Updated

A trailhead sign says one activity is prohibited, but the management plan changed months ago. Some locals follow the old rule, guides teach the new one, and a media article copied the obsolete version. The quest is partly ecological and partly about information propagation.

## 5. A Wild Pokémon Keeps Returning

A wild Pokémon receives treatment after an incident and is released. It repeatedly returns to the same care station. The system records the visits without assuming the Pokémon wants capture, ownership or permanent residency.

## 6. The Newcomer Panic

A species appears in a watershed where no recent records exist. Public discussion immediately labels it invasive. Researchers can confirm presence but not ecological impact. The story tests whether institutions act on uncertainty, monitor first or discover that the population has a much older local history than expected.

## 7. The Shared Waterhole

A restored water source is used by livestock, travelers, wild collectives and a nearby settlement. Each group benefits from the restoration, but peak use overlaps. Management becomes a schedule and infrastructure problem rather than an enemy encounter.

## 8. The Quiet Zone During Festival Week

A recurring festival draws more visitors every year. A nearby roost becomes increasingly disturbed during the same period. The interesting decision is whether the festival shifts location, reduces hours, changes transport, funds mitigation or accepts a smaller footprint.

## 9. The Ranger Who Disagrees With the Lab

A field steward and a research institution interpret the same behavior differently. The steward has years of local observations; the lab has stronger regional comparative data. Both can be partly right. Players gather evidence instead of resolving the disagreement with reputation alone.

## 10. Restoration Attracts the Wrong Crowd

A wetland project succeeds faster than expected and starts attracting rare-looking Pokémon. Public attention arrives before the habitat is stable. The management challenge becomes visitor pressure created by success.

## 11. The Voluntary Closure

A community asks Trainers to avoid a route for two weeks during a sensitive ecological window. No magical barrier exists. The system tracks compliance, alternatives, communication quality and consequences without inventing law-enforcement authority.

## 12. The Habitat Inside the Dungeon

An old ruin previously treated as an adventure site has become an important refuge for a displaced wild population. Returning players must navigate the tension between archaeology, dungeon access and habitat protection rather than simply clearing the location again.

## 13. The Release Site Dispute

A rehabilitated persistent Pokémon is ready for possible release, but three candidate locations have different risks: original home range with ongoing disturbance, a safer managed site with no known social connection, and a corridor where its old collective has recently been observed. The system does not choose automatically.

## 14. The Observation Blind

A research group builds a small observation structure near a migration route. It later becomes clear that the blind itself changes how some Pokémon move. The team must decide whether to relocate it, redesign it or accept that earlier data may be biased.

## 15. The Working Landscape

A productive orchard also functions as seasonal feeding habitat. Growers want predictable harvests; stewards want habitat continuity; Trainers are drawn by unusual encounters. Several modest changes may work better than converting the area into either a farm-only or wildlife-only zone.

## 16. The Stewardship Apprenticeship

A young NPC begins as a visitor guide and gradually learns monitoring, conflict response, cultural history and ecological survey work from several mentors. Their career can evolve across seasons independently of the player's level.

## 17. The Corridor Camera Gap

Monitoring stations show a wild collective entering a corridor but not leaving it. Before assuming a disappearance, players investigate broken equipment, alternate paths and stale maps. The missing data is a problem in its own right.

## 18. The Protected Site With No Rare Pokémon

A site is protected because it preserves common species, water flow, cultural history and a migration link. Players expecting a legendary or rare encounter discover that the place matters precisely because of ordinary ecological continuity.

## 19. The Tourist Favorite

A charismatic wild individual has become famous online. Visitors increasingly approach it, leave food and crowd its normal range. The problem comes from affection rather than hostility. Public-memory and media systems track how the individual's image differs from its observed behavior.

## 20. The Restoration Crew's Shortcut

Workers repeatedly use a shortcut through a recovery zone because the official access route is inefficient. Instead of treating them as villains, the project can redesign staging logistics or accept a different management boundary.

## 21. The Temporary Refuge

A crisis displaces several wild collectives into a managed area never designed for them. Emergency stewardship decisions must balance temporary shelter, resource competition and eventual return without assuming permanent relocation.

## 22. The Community Monitoring Board

Residents, students, travelers and researchers can submit observations to a public board. Reports vary in quality. Repeated independent observations may reveal a real trend, while viral but copied reports do not count as independent evidence.

## 23. The Closed Trail Economy

A seasonal closure protects a sensitive area but harms a small guide service and nearby vendors. The civic problem is not proof that protection is wrong; it is deciding whether alternate routes, timing, compensation, retraining or redesigned visitor access can reduce the tradeoff.

## 24. The Sanctuary That Is Not a Nursery

A habitat refuge houses wild Pokémon temporarily displaced by construction. Visitors keep assuming staff can breed, train, heal or adopt every Pokémon there. The institution must clarify its actual mandate and refer other needs to existing care/nursery systems.

## 25. The Species Everyone Thought Was Solitary

Long-term observation reveals recurring group behavior in a species locals believed was solitary. The wild-collective layer can create a persistent association only after enough evidence accumulates; the discovery should not invent Pack Mon or combat bonuses.

## 26. The City Learns to Share Space — long arc

A rapidly growing settlement initially responds to wild Pokémon through ad hoc complaints and relocation. Over several chapters, players help map movement patterns, identify infrastructure conflicts, test small coexistence measures, create an urban refuge network and review unintended consequences. Success changes how the city develops rather than producing one final boss.

## 27. The Returning River — long arc

A degraded river system is restored in stages. Early jobs document current use and stabilize damaged sites. Middle chapters reveal that restored habitat changes fish, Water Pokémon, agriculture and transport. Later chapters force management choices about visitor access, seasonal flows and the return of a previously absent collective. The arc ends with a management review, not a permanent "100% restored" flag.

## 28. The Corridor Pact — long arc

Several settlements, transport operators, land users and stewardship groups depend on one regional corridor. No single institution controls the whole route. Players gather migration evidence, negotiate local changes, test crossings and respond to failures. The final state can be a workable network of imperfect agreements rather than one universal law.

## Mechanical guardrail summary

Conservation state may explain why players encounter a Pokémon, why a location is temporarily restricted or why a route changes. It cannot itself:

- modify capture odds;
- create a Poké Ball restriction;
- grant escape/withdrawal actions that PTU does not provide;
- add morale or pack bonuses;
- invent habitat damage;
- invent environmental status effects;
- create Trainer Feature bonuses;
- despawn an ecological population because visitors are prohibited;
- decide ownership of a rehabilitated Pokémon;
- turn an observation into a species-level rule.

Every tactical effect must remain owned by PTU/Caelo + AutoPTU, with Minecraft only adapting and rendering verified state.

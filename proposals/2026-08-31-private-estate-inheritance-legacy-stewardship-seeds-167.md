# Private Estate, Inheritance and Legacy Stewardship Seeds

Status: NON-CANON CANDIDATES
Pass: 167

These concepts are proposal material only. They create no canon deaths, heirs, ownership rules or succession customs.

## The House With Three Keys

Three people possess legitimate keys to the same old house: a former resident, the person who maintained it during the owner's final years, and a relative who received a key long before the death. None of the keys proves current ownership. The story is about reconstructing access history, household use and later authority without turning possession into title.

## Five Times Someone Said “It Was Left to Me”

Five claims use the same phrase but mean different things: an explicit gift already completed, a future intention never executed, a stewardship request, a family assumption, and a later distribution decision. The mystery rewards precise provenance rather than exposing four liars.

## Two Correct Inventories

An early inventory records a workshop before a storm and a later one records what survived. Both are accurate. A missing tool becomes suspicious only because later characters treat the two lists as if they were simultaneous.

## The Beneficiary Who Refused the Object

A person was validly selected under an authored local process but declined the physical object or stewardship duty. The refusal creates a new continuity problem rather than forcing acceptance or portraying the person as ungrateful.

## The Workshop Nobody Wanted to Divide

Tools, notebooks, furniture and the building have different ownership and stewardship histories. Several successors agree that splitting everything would destroy the workshop's practical value, but they disagree about the long-term arrangement. This can generate negotiation, apprenticeships and institutional partnerships without requiring a villain.

## The Heirloom That Belonged to the Institution

A famous object has been called a “family heirloom” for decades because one family maintained it. Archive records suggest the institution always retained title while the family held stewardship. Public Memory, Material Culture, Archives and Property/Ownership can all hold different but compatible facts.

## The Letter Found After Distribution

A newly catalogued letter expresses a clear wish after most assets have already been distributed. The letter's existence and authenticity can be established without assuming it automatically reverses earlier transfers. The resulting question depends on the authored succession rule.

## The Debt Everyone Assumed Was Paid

A ledger lists an old obligation and later household memory treats it as settled, but no matching payment record has survived. The story can end with uncertainty, a voluntary settlement, archival discovery or proof that the obligation had already been discharged. Finance remains authoritative for actual payments.

## The Archive Copy With a Missing Page

The only accessible copy of an instruction is incomplete. The missing page becomes a provenance problem rather than an excuse for the generator to invent whichever clause produces the most drama.

## The Pokémon Who Keeps Returning to the Old Home

A deceased Trainer's former companion repeatedly returns to the old property. Its behavior can become an emotional and ecological continuity thread. It does not make the Pokémon property, establish an heir, or prove where the Pokémon wants to live permanently.

## The Estate With No Clear Successor

Canon supplies no applicable rule and no controlling instruction. The estate remains unresolved while trusted people preserve assets from deterioration. The uncertainty itself can persist for years instead of being solved through invented law.

## The Object Everyone Remembers as a Gift

Multiple witnesses remember a person publicly saying an object would belong to someone else someday. Material history shows the object never left the original owner's possession. The distinction between promise, gift, custody and later distribution matters.

## The Role That Did Not Pass With the House

A family residence and an important civic or religious duty were historically associated. When the house changes hands, characters assume the role also transferred. Civic Office or Ritual authority shows that the role uses an independent succession process.

## The Collection With Four Histories

A research collection contains specimens, notebooks, tools and personal keepsakes gathered over decades. Some belong to an institution, some to the deceased researcher, some were loans, and some have uncertain provenance. Resolving access and stewardship creates a long-form archive/research arc without treating the collection as one asset bundle.

## Long arc: The House Outlives the Family

Across several generations, one building changes from household to workshop, then shared community space, then archive. Ownership, occupancy, stewardship and public meaning change at different times. Later characters can investigate why local speech still calls it by the name of a family that has not lived there for decades.

## Long arc: Three Generations, One Workshop

A craft workshop survives its founder through an assistant, a relative and eventually a cooperative or institution. Each period preserves different practices and objects. The campaign can revisit the same room years apart and see continuity through ordinary work rather than constant catastrophe.

## Long arc: The Object With Four Histories

One non-magical object accumulates four distinct narratives: family memory, institutional documentation, public legend and physical repair history. None needs to be wholly false. Its eventual stewardship decision becomes meaningful because the players understand all four contexts.

## NPC and faction patterns

A temporary custodian who is careful precisely because they do not claim ownership.

A relative who wants records preserved more than assets acquired.

An archivist who can authenticate provenance but has no authority to decide distribution.

A property steward trusted by several rival claimants because their mandate is deliberately narrow.

A former business partner whose legitimate financial claim is mistaken by outsiders for greed.

A community institution that wants an important collection to remain locally accessible while acknowledging that access and ownership are separate questions.

## Encounter hooks with reduced contracts

Estate Inventory Storage Perimeter: adversaries block access to a storage location. Reduced output: `IMMEDIATE_INVENTORY_STORAGE_APPROACH_CLEAR` only.

Legacy Transfer Route Interruption: a threatened route must be cleared before a separately authored handoff can occur. Reduced output: `IMMEDIATE_LEGACY_TRANSFER_ROUTE_CLEAR` only.

Vacant Homestead Access Incident: a conventional battle happens near an unresolved property. Reduced output: `IMMEDIATE_PROPERTY_APPROACH_CLEAR` only.

Testament Record Recovery Perimeter: hostile actors occupy the approach to an archive. Reduced output: `IMMEDIATE_RECORD_STORAGE_ACCESS_CLEAR` only.

In every reduced version, estate assets, papers, companions, custodians and protected objects remain outside BattleSpec. Combat never settles ownership, authenticity, inheritance or stewardship.

## Future canon questions

Ouros still needs authored decisions before any specific institution can use this layer: which communities recognize posthumous instructions; what authority reviews them; what happens without an instruction; how stewardship differs from title; how privately held businesses continue; how companion Pokémon are cared for after a Trainer death; how confidential estate records become accessible; and whether particular cultures use family, institutional, communal or other succession practices.

Until those questions are answered, these seeds remain worldbuilding candidates rather than implied law.

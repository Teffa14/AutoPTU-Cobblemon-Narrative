# Ouros Worldbuilding Seeds — Pass 69: Flora, Pollination & Seed Dispersal

Status: NON-CANON proposals. These are original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research patterns. They do not transplant source plots, characters, locations, or mechanics.

## 1. The Missing Midseason Bloom

A valley still has normal spring and late-summer flowers, but the species that usually bridge the two windows barely bloom this year. Researchers first notice the change through a gap in visitor observations rather than visible plant death.

Hooks:
- phenology survey;
- Cutiefly/Butterfree observation;
- drought or soil hypothesis;
- restoration planning;
- orchard concern without automatic crop failure.

## 2. The Orchard Edge Shift

Wild nectar users begin concentrating along cultivated orchard edges after a nearby wildflower corridor declines. Farmers disagree about whether the change is beneficial, disruptive, or merely temporary.

The story can resolve through observation, access management, restoration, or relocation planning without assuming a battle.

## 3. Windborne Seeds After the Fire

A burn scar develops scattered seedlings whose nearest plausible source is far away. Eldegoss activity is observed in the region, but it remains one candidate vector among wind, restoration material, vehicles, and old seed banks.

## 4. The Meadow That Grew but Did Not Flower

A restoration plot establishes dense green cover but produces little floral resource during the target season. The project looked successful visually, yet its ecological objective remains incomplete.

## 5. Combee Route Collapse

A known Combee collective keeps its night roost but stops using one of its daytime foraging routes. The cause could be flowering timing, construction, repeated disturbance, pesticide-like contamination if canon supports it, or a completely unrelated shift.

## 6. The Garden Without a Gardener

An old public garden survives after the institution that maintained it disappears. Flabébé-line Pokémon and residents continue using the site, but nobody knows which plants were deliberate, which arrived later, and which maintenance practices matter.

## 7. The Seed Lot With No Field Notes

A conservation nursery finds an old batch of regionally important seeds with a plausible label but no collection coordinates or date. The material may still be useful, but its provenance is too weak for sensitive restoration without further work.

## 8. The Flowering Ferry Verge

A ferry terminal's neglected verge becomes a strong seasonal flower patch because routine mowing stopped during repairs. Reopening the terminal creates a conflict between transport maintenance, visitor safety, and an unexpected ecological resource.

## 9. First Bud, Wrong Week

Budew in a monitored valley begin opening earlier than the local historical range. The event is logged as a phenology signal, not immediate proof of climate change or a regional anomaly.

## 10. Pollination Without Yield Claims

A research team confirms repeated flower visits and pollen transfer in an orchard but refuses to claim a crop-yield effect because the harvest season has not yet finished and many other variables remain unresolved.

This can create a public-media conflict without requiring fraud or villainy.

## 11. The River Seed Line

After a floodplain reconnects, seedlings appear far downstream in newly deposited sediment. Hydrology can establish where water moved; flora research must still determine which seeds actually travelled with it.

## 12. The Empty Apiary Morning

An apiary is operating normally, but wild flower visitors are unexpectedly absent from nearby hedgerows. The event may involve flowering timing, weather, disturbance, or a collective movement shift rather than hive failure.

## 13. Ribombee Material Pressure

A locally valued Ribombee-produced material becomes fashionable after media coverage. Demand grows faster than any institution's understanding of the wild population, creating a stewardship problem before a legality problem is even defined.

## 14. The Two Restoration Mixes

Two neighboring projects use different seed-source strategies. Both establish, but five years later one has strong early-season flowers and the other strong late-season resources. Neither was necessarily "wrong"; the landscape now depends on both.

## 15. The Roadside Success Nobody Planned

A repaired road shoulder develops a diverse flower patch from a mixture of old soil seed bank, restoration seed, wind dispersal and vehicle movement. The question becomes whether to preserve, manage or simply observe it.

## 16. The Orchard's Old Wild Strip

A narrow uncultivated strip inside a century-old orchard turns out to provide a continuous floral window that newer sections lack. Expansion plans threaten it because nobody previously classified it as infrastructure.

## 17. Flower Color Is Not Species Identity

A survey team has years of records grouped only by flower color. A later taxonomic review reveals that several distinct plants were collapsed into the same category, forcing old ecological conclusions to be re-evaluated without deleting the historical observations.

## 18. The Meadow After the Festival

A large festival temporarily increases trampling, waste, vehicle access and night lighting near a flower-rich site. The following season's changes are monitored rather than blamed on a single cause immediately.

## 19. Former Partner in the Flower Corridor

A released former partner is observed repeatedly along a flowering corridor. The system records location and behavior only. It does not infer that the Pokémon returned because of its former Trainer or wants to rejoin them.

## 20. The Fruit Nobody Harvests

A wild fruiting patch is important to several Pokémon groups but has no established human use. A proposed commercial harvest creates new questions about access, stewardship and ecological dependence without assuming harvesting is forbidden.

## 21. The One Tree Everyone Watches

A single old flowering tree becomes a long-term observation landmark because its bloom dates have been recorded for decades. Damage to the tree threatens a valuable time series as much as the tree itself.

## 22. The Pollinator Corridor Map

Researchers realize that individual flower patches look healthy, but large gaps between them may explain changes in visitor movement. The resulting project is about connectivity rather than adding more total flowers everywhere.

## 23. The Green Roof Experiment

A dense district tests several rooftop planting designs. Some attract visitors, some fail under heat and wind, and others succeed botanically but create maintenance problems. The project becomes urban ecology, architecture and public works rather than a simple upgrade.

## 24. The Old Seed Bank Wakes

After shallow soil disturbance, plants unseen for years appear at a site. Their source may be a persistent soil seed bank rather than recent dispersal. This creates a historical ecology investigation tied to Soil and Archives.

## 25. The Wrong Restoration Target

A project was designed to recreate a remembered landscape state, but archival photographs and soil records later suggest that the remembered condition was itself temporary. The community must decide whether to keep the current target or revise it.

## 26. The Florges Garden Boundary

A persistent Florges is strongly associated with a large flower garden that crosses private and public parcels. Its presence does not establish ownership or authority. The real conflict concerns access, maintenance and the future of the connected plant community.

## 27. The Nectar Gap During Recovery

A post-fire landscape shows excellent seedling recruitment but very little flowering during one part of the season. Pollinator observations reveal that "recovery" has different timelines for cover, flowers and habitat use.

## 28. The Nursery Escape That Was a Plant

A suspected introduced Pokémon case begins after unusual visitor activity around a nursery. Investigation instead finds that a nonlocal ornamental plant escaped cultivation and changed local floral resources. Biosecurity still matters, but the target of the case changes.

## 29. Night Pollination Question

A night-observation project detects repeated visits to flowers after dark. Lightscape changes, nocturnal Pokémon activity and flower timing all become relevant. The system does not assume the visitors are effective pollinators until evidence supports it.

## 30. The Regional Flower Calendar

Researchers, farmers, hikers and photographers jointly build a multi-year calendar of flowering windows. It becomes useful for conservation, tourism, agriculture and field research, but it remains a record of observations rather than a guaranteed schedule.

# Long Arc A — The Seven-Year Meadow

Year 1: a disturbed transport worksite is stabilized with temporary cover.

Year 2: native planting begins after soil assessment.

Year 3: plant cover is strong, but target flowering windows are incomplete.

Year 4: visitor observations show a midseason resource gap.

Year 5: a second planting phase adds complementary flowering periods.

Year 6: a nearby wild collective changes its foraging route through the site.

Year 7: the meadow is considered a functioning landscape element, but monitoring continues because succession is not finished.

The arc can connect Public Works, Soil, Conservation, Science, Wild Collectives, Food/Agriculture and Travel without requiring a central villain.

# Long Arc B — Seeds Across the Basin

A freshwater system connects mountain meadows, farms, riverbanks and a downstream wetland.

Players gradually discover that plant recruitment across the basin comes from several mechanisms:

- wind;
- floods;
- restoration projects;
- transport corridors;
- Pokémon-mediated dispersal;
- persistent local seed banks.

The payoff is a regional ecological map built from evidence rather than one revelation that explains everything.

# Long Arc C — The Bloom Years

A settlement becomes known for a spectacular seasonal bloom.

Over several years:

- tourism grows;
- night lighting expands;
- transport improves;
- some flowering patches are trampled;
- residents create protected routes;
- commercial growers introduce new varieties;
- researchers notice visitor-network changes;
- public memory starts treating one recent bloom pattern as "traditional" despite older records showing variation.

The conclusion can be a new management agreement rather than restoring the landscape to a fictional perfect past.

# Encounter Contract 2 — Seed Bank Recovery

Full version:

Players search a disturbed site for documented seed material while wild Pokémon use the same space and weather may change access.

Key missing dependencies:

- interactable objective state;
- objective-aware tactical AI;
- dynamic weather/terrain if used tactically;
- adapter/playback;
- potentially complete movement if withdrawal or protection matters.

Reduced version:

Search and custody are resolved in overworld. Samples never become destructible battle props by default. If battle occurs, use a static legal map and update the recovery record afterward.

# Encounter Contract 3 — Orchard Edge Foraging Conflict

Full version:

Wild nectar/fruit users move between a declining wild patch and a cultivated orchard edge. The intended design supports observation, deterrence, withdrawal and protection rather than mandatory KO.

Key missing dependencies:

- complete movement/interception — BLOCKING
- terrain/zones/reactions — BLOCKING if orchard areas have tactical meaning
- tactical AI — BLOCKING for forage/withdraw/protect goals
- adapter/playback — BLOCKING

Reduced version:

Determine positions and ecological motivation in world state. Offer noncombat routes first. If combat starts, only combatants enter the grid. Crop damage, pollination success and fruit loss are not inferred from attacks unless an explicit world-state action records them.

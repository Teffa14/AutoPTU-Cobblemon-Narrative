# Pass 166 — Community science and participatory monitoring seeds

Status: PROPOSALS / NON-CANON
Date: 2026-08-25
Authority: subordinate to the Pass 166 community-science protocol and existing domain systems

All concepts below are original Ouros candidates. None are canon-approved. They preserve uncertainty, provenance, privacy, and the distinction between public observations and authoritative ecological conclusions.

## Thirty candidate hooks

1. The Duplicate Rare Sighting

A supposedly explosive cluster of rare reports turns out to be many people reposting one original photograph. The social reaction is real; the independent detection count is not.

2. The Park Nobody Surveys

A neighborhood park looks empty in the regional dashboard because nearly all volunteers use a better-known trail two districts away. A school project creates the first structured weekly record.

3. School Count, New Method

A long-running student survey switches from casual highlights to complete checklists. Apparent species richness changes immediately, forcing the archive to preserve the method boundary.

4. The Night-Shift Observer

A worker walking home after midnight becomes the only regular source for nocturnal observations along an industrial corridor. The pattern may reflect ecology, schedule, or both.

5. Viral Screenshot, Missing Metadata

A screenshot of a rare Pokémon circulates widely but has lost timestamp, original file, and precise provenance. The sighting can remain interesting without becoming validated.

6. The Expert Who Was Wrong

A respected reviewer confidently assigns the wrong species. A later image angle produces a correction without turning the reviewer into a fraud.

7. The New Observer Who Was Right

A beginner submits an unusual record with excellent evidence. Automated filters flag it, but expert review eventually validates it.

8. Accessible Route Bias

The best-sampled wetland sector is the accessible boardwalk. Researchers realize their public dataset says much more about that route than about the marsh interior.

9. Storm Week Coverage Gap

A major storm causes zero volunteer observations for six days. The gap later coincides with a real ecological change, but the exact timing remains unknown.

10. Festival Overcount

A city festival draws hundreds of observers into one plaza. The dashboard explodes with reports even though the wildlife population may not have changed.

11. The Camera Clock Drift

Dozens of volunteer photographs come from the same loaned camera whose clock was wrong. Timekeeping corrects the series without rewriting the image originals.

12. Fifty Records, One Observer

A highly active naturalist submits fifty records during one route. They remain fifty observations but one observer-effort episode, not fifty independent surveys.

13. Translation and Common-Name Collision

Two local names are treated as different Pokémon in a volunteer form until Languages and Taxonomy reconcile the terminology.

14. The Redacted Coordinate

A public researcher assumes a rare observation lacks location data. In fact, its exact nesting site is deliberately obscured under a sensitive-location policy.

15. Telemetry Disagrees

Volunteer sightings suggest a tagged individual stayed near town, while receiver data places its tag elsewhere. The correct explanation could include mistaken identity, tag detachment, or coverage gaps.

16. The Paper Checklists

A library digitizes thirty years of local nature-club notebooks. The observations become useful only after method, dates, place names, and duplicate sources are reconstructed.

17. The Contributor Retires

A monitoring program loses the person who knew every local call and track. The institution discovers that informal expertise was never documented.

18. Rival Naturalist Clubs

Two clubs compete over who records more species. Their rivalry increases coverage but also creates incentives to chase highlights instead of complete efforts.

19. Nothing Happened This Count

An annual count produces no unusual findings. Years later, its ordinary coverage becomes the clean baseline needed to interpret a genuine change.

20. The Observation That Prevented a Quest

A rumor of a missing seasonal Pokémon population reaches a ranger station. Existing complete checklists show the same late arrival happened three times before, so no emergency expedition is needed.

21. Photo Without Time

A strong identification image survives, but nobody can establish when it was taken. It remains useful for morphology and weak for phenology.

22. The Empty Checklist

A volunteer reports a complete fixed-point survey with no target Pokémon detected. The zero becomes valuable because method and effort are explicit.

23. The School Route Becomes Scientific

What began as an education walk accumulates enough consistent protocol history to become part of a formal monitoring program.

24. Ten Uploads From One Group Walk

Every participant uploads the same flock from slightly different angles. Source-dependency links preserve each contribution while preventing artificial inflation.

25. The Observer Changed Method

A veteran switches from a short stationary count to a long traveling route. Their personal time series remains historically useful but no longer directly comparable without method awareness.

26. The Hand-Drawn Field Map

An old volunteer notebook contains a remarkably precise hand-drawn location but no modern coordinates. Cartography can georeference it while preserving uncertainty.

27. The Rare-Species Alert Creates Disturbance

A precise public alert attracts photographers to a sensitive site. The program changes its publication policy without deleting the valid original observation.

28. The Correction Twenty Years Later

A museum specimen and new taxonomic work show that several old community records used the wrong species name. The observations survive under revised determinations.

29. Juvenile Misidentification

A seasonal spike in a supposed rare species is eventually traced to juveniles of a common species that were difficult to identify from distance.

30. The Count Moved Because of Weather

An annual survey shifts by a week after severe weather. The result is valid for that year but must not be compared as if timing were identical.

## Long-form arcs

### Five Counts at Meridian Marsh

Year 1 establishes a popular public BioBlitz concentrated around the main boardwalk. Year 2 adds an accessible secondary route. Year 3 introduces night observations and finds that the earlier dataset was strongly daytime-biased. Year 4 a sensitive nesting discovery forces coordinate redaction and changes public communications. Year 5 a storm cancels half the planned effort, producing a deliberately incomplete year rather than a fabricated trend.

The arc can recur later through archived dashboards, old volunteer notes, retired reviewers, school participants who become professionals, and revised ecological interpretations.

### Atlas Built by Strangers

A regional atlas begins as thousands of unrelated public reports. Over years the institution separates duplicate media, translates local names, adds structured effort protocols, identifies coverage deserts, creates privacy rules, and revises old determinations.

The atlas becomes culturally important while remaining scientifically versioned. Earlier editions are not deleted when later ones improve.

### The Rare Sighting That Wouldn’t Settle

One disputed rare-Pokémon observation persists for years. The original photograph is real but incomplete. Several reposts are duplicates. A second witness is uncertain. A later telemetry record is suggestive but not decisive. Taxonomy changes the expected identification criteria. Eventually the correct outcome may still be `UNRESOLVED`.

The narrative value comes from how different institutions handle uncertainty, not from forcing a final reveal.

## Encounter contracts

### BioBlitz Trail Closure

Full version: a public count is underway when a route closure and wildlife movement create a safety problem. Participants, staff, and wild Pokémon have non-hostile movement goals. Complete movement, tactical AI, adapter/playback, and any real environmental mechanics used by the closure are required.

Reduced version: stop the BioBlitz in world state, evacuate volunteers, preserve all submissions up to interruption, move wildlife outside the grid, then run a conventional static battle only if an independent threat remains.

Narrative premise preserved: the survey was interrupted and its coverage is incomplete.

### Rare Sighting Crowd Surge

Full version: a publicly visible rare observation draws visitors near a sensitive wildlife site. The goal is rerouting people and allowing wildlife withdrawal, not defeating the observed Pokémon.

Reduced version: obscure the public location, resolve crowd rerouting and wildlife movement outside battle, and use a separate static encounter only if a genuine confrontation remains.

Narrative premise preserved: publication policy and public behavior changed because of the sighting.

### Community Sensor Retrieval

Full version: a public monitoring program must recover a simple field logger after severe weather. Technicians have a `REACH_DEVICE` objective and may need protection or withdrawal.

Reduced version: device custody, technician movement, storm assessment, and retrieval occur in world state. A nearby static battle can happen independently.

Narrative premise preserved: the dataset may contain a gap or delayed upload regardless of combat outcome.

### Observation Review Night

No battle required. Reviewers compare submissions, source dependencies, photographs, effort records, translations, and sensitive-location policy. Valid endings include `VALID_FOR_SCOPE`, `DUPLICATE_SOURCE`, `IDENTIFICATION_REVISED`, `INSUFFICIENT_FOR_PRODUCT`, and `UNRESOLVED`.

## NPC and institution candidates

A retired local naturalist whose observations are excellent but whose old place names need georeferencing.

A school science coordinator who values complete zero-detection checklists more than flashy rare sightings.

A volunteer reviewer who is highly skilled in one taxonomic group and explicitly refuses to review outside that scope.

A public-program coordinator whose job is mainly protocol design, participant safety, privacy, and data handoff rather than scientific interpretation.

Two rival clubs with different observation cultures: one prioritizes species totals, the other long repeatable transects.

A former participant whose old account was merged incorrectly with another person and whose historical observations need Identity review.

## Faction dynamics

Community-science groups should usually be lightweight associations, clubs, school programs, museum circles, conservation volunteers, or institutional programs rather than combat factions.

Conflict can arise from method, publication, access, privacy, prestige, local knowledge, or interpretation. None of those disagreements require villainy.

## Mechanical guardrails

No concept in Pass 166 grants Researcher, Pokémon Education, Perception, Chronicler, XP, Tutor Points, capture modifiers, encounter modifiers, initiative, Accuracy, or item rewards.

Observation counts never modify Cobblemon spawn rates directly.

A validated public sighting never guarantees the same Pokémon remains present when players arrive.

Battle victory never validates an observation, resolves taxonomy, produces survey effort, or authorizes publication.

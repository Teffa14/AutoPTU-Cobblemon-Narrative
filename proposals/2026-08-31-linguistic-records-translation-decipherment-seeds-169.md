# Pass 169 Ouros candidates — linguistic records, translation, and decipherment

Status: NON-CANON / REVIEW REQUIRED
Date: 2026-08-31

These candidates use the proposed linguistic continuity extension. None establishes a language, script, historical civilization, magical inscription, universal translator, or PTU mechanic as canon.

## The Door Was Never Locked

A frequently cited translation of an old route marker has been treated for decades as an instruction to keep a passage sealed. A newly documented parallel text suggests the key verb may instead concern keeping a path clear or unobstructed. The adventure is about comparing witnesses, finding the physical route, and understanding who built later policy around the old reading. The old translators need not have been incompetent or malicious.

Full encounter possibility: the newly accessible route contains positional hazards and a contested chamber. Reduced version: translation work and route opening remain Narrative; AutoPTU handles only a conventional conflict encountered after access is established.

## Five Translations of the Same Marker

Five surviving translations circulate: literal, ceremonial, antiquarian, schoolbook, and modern institutional. Each preserves something useful and loses something else. Players can reconstruct why the versions diverged by tracing their source witnesses and audiences.

The payoff is provenance rather than a single dramatic reveal.

## The Inscription With a Missing Line

A damaged monument has one line reconstructed from an early hand copy made before the surface deteriorated. The copy itself contains an ambiguous character. A later archive discovery provides a second independent copy but not a perfect match.

Potential consequences include revised route history, a changed date estimate, or a narrower historical claim. The missing line must not be used to manufacture cosmology without further evidence.

## The Interpreter Who Was Right for the Wrong Era

An interpreter correctly understands the modern descendant language but applies one current sense to an older text. Another specialist identifies a semantic shift. Both remain competent; the disagreement produces a richer timeline of language change.

## The Living Word in a Dead Lexicon

An academic glossary marks a term as obsolete. A remote community still uses a related form in everyday speech with a slightly different meaning. The discovery updates the record and changes how several old inscriptions are read.

This candidate deliberately prevents archaeology from treating living speakers as mere puzzle keys.

## The Cave Where Directions Depend on Variety

Several route labels use a directional term whose meaning differs between two documented regional varieties. Travelers relying on an old standardized guide repeatedly choose the longer path.

The scene can support exploration and social history without creating a universal language barrier.

## The False Cognate Route

Two inscriptions appear to share a familiar word. The similarity led an earlier expedition to connect two sites. A larger corpus shows the forms are unrelated. Players can retrace the old expedition, discovering useful observations even though its linguistic premise was wrong.

## The Two-Language Station

A long-running transport or research station accumulated signage in two languages across several renovation periods. Later signs shorten or standardize older terms. A service disruption forces staff to identify which historical sign system corresponds to which physical route.

No language is automatically assigned to any current Ouros settlement until canon review.

## The Glossary That Outlived the Expedition

An expedition ended badly or simply dissolved, but its working glossary remained in an archive. Later researchers discover that entries preserve where each word was heard, who supplied it, and which readings were uncertain. The glossary becomes more valuable than the expedition's published conclusions.

## The Name Everyone Translated

A place name has been translated into a descriptive phrase for generations. New evidence suggests it may have functioned primarily as a proper name, while the descriptive association emerged later. Human Identity retains the name lineage; the linguistic layer retains the competing analyses.

## The Bilingual Fragment

A small fragment contains parallel material in two established language varieties. It helps decipher a narrow set of terms but does not unlock every inscription in the corpus. Future discoveries can expand or overturn the mapping.

## The Unreadable Half Is the Important Half

A public monument has a well-preserved ceremonial introduction and a badly damaged practical section. Popular memory quotes the first half; researchers care about the second. Better imaging or a duplicate witness may eventually improve the reading.

## The Copyist Preserved the Error

Several later manuscripts agree because they descend from one earlier copy. A separate physical witness shows that the shared wording is a copy error rather than independent corroboration. Archive lineage matters as much as raw count of agreeing sources.

## The Translation Nobody Received

A corrected translation was produced years ago but circulated only inside one institution. Another community continues acting on the older version because Communications never delivered the correction through a route they use. Both knowledge states remain historically valid.

## Long arc — A Dictionary Across Generations

A modest field glossary begins with one expedition. Later speakers, researchers, travelers, archivists, and students add senses, examples, regional labels, obsolete forms, and corrections. Player characters in different eras may contribute observations without ever “finishing” the language.

The arc makes accumulation itself meaningful. A future route puzzle may be solvable because an earlier party carefully recorded a mundane phrase decades before.

## Long arc — The Ruins Become Readable

At campaign start, a ruin corpus is mostly undeciphered. Progress comes through repeated witnesses: labels, parallel formulae, damaged copies, bilingual material, living linguistic relatives, dated objects, and corrections to earlier assumptions. Each breakthrough opens questions rather than one final lore dump.

Full tactical set pieces remain optional. Reduced implementations keep decipherment in Narrative and use AutoPTU only for conventional encounters around access, protection, or withdrawal.

## Long arc — The Language Nobody Actually Lost

Researchers initially describe a historical language as extinct because their archive contains only old texts. Later fieldwork reveals a living descendant variety in a community that institutional research had ignored. The arc concerns relationships, documentation, access, changing terminology, and institutional correction rather than “discovering” people who already knew their own language.

## Review questions

Canon review must decide whether Ouros has multiple established languages or varieties at all; which scripts and communities actually exist; whether any old corpora are already known; whether current speakers connect to those corpora; what translation institutions exist; how player-language barriers should be presented; and whether any PTU/Caelo Feature creates explicit linguistic capability.

No candidate should be promoted merely because Minecraft can display text in a sign, book, subtitle, chat bubble, UI locale, or resource-pack font.

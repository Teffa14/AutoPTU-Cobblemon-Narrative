# Coastal Navigation Aids, Lighthouse & Beacon Seeds — Pass 92

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANON.
Date: 2026-08-28

These are original Ouros candidates derived from the Pass 92 research structures. No lighthouse, buoyage system, keeper institution, navigation standard, Pokémon work practice or coastal technology described here is established canon.

## The Light Is On, the Pattern Is Wrong

A harbor approach remains visually lit, but regular users notice that the signal no longer matches the expected rhythm recorded in their current chart/notice set.

The initial problem is not “the lighthouse is broken.” It is a discrepancy among expected characteristic, direct observation and monitoring state.

Possible causes include:
- incorrect configuration after maintenance;
- observers using a superseded characteristic;
- a temporary replacement being mistaken for the permanent aid;
- intermittent supply or controller fault;
- viewing geometry producing a misleading observation;
- evidence still insufficient.

Consequences can include a temporary notice, route limitation, inspection and later public correction.

No hidden sabotage is assumed.

## The Buoy That Moved Before the Chart

A floating mark is repeatedly observed outside the position shown on a widely used chart.

Several groups disagree over what is wrong:
- the buoy moved;
- the chart is stale;
- the observer plotted the position incorrectly;
- the aid was intentionally relocated and a notice failed to reach everyone.

The quest reconstructs position observations, chart edition history, maintenance visits and notice delivery.

If the aid is actually off station, Maritime/Travel owns any route consequence. Cartography owns the chart correction. Maintenance owns physical recovery/redeployment.

## The Daylight Route During a Dark Beacon

A major light goes out, but the affected lane does not automatically close.

A reviewed local policy may permit limited daylight movement, a temporary marker, local guide support or another mitigation if canon later establishes those options.

The narrative value comes from several systems changing at different times:
- the light fails;
- a temporary operating restriction begins;
- public notices propagate unevenly;
- businesses and passenger services adjust;
- the repair is completed;
- verification occurs;
- normal operation resumes later.

Pass 92 does not invent the actual operating policy.

## Two Harbors, One Late Notice

Two communities use the same coastal aid but receive its change notice at different times.

A vessel operator from one harbor acts on current information while a resident from the other insists the old characteristic is still correct.

Both statements may be honest and correctly sourced from the information each actor actually received.

This hook connects:
- Communications delivery;
- Public Notices;
- actor knowledge;
- Cartography editions;
- Maritime service decisions;
- local rumor correction.

## The Old Lighthouse Still Matters

A historic lighthouse no longer performs the primary technical navigation function. A newer automated aid has taken over offshore.

The old building remains important as:
- a public viewpoint;
- archive/workshop space;
- local landmark;
- memorial or heritage site if canon supports it;
- nesting/roosting structure;
- emergency visual landmark;
- place where former workers and residents retain local knowledge.

A proposal to renovate the building creates conflict among public access, preservation, ecology and technical reuse without requiring anyone to be a villain.

## The Keeper Who No Longer Lives There

Local speech still calls someone “the lighthouse keeper,” but the aid has been automated for years.

The former resident now performs periodic inspections, heritage interpretation or no operational work at all.

A new fault reveals that visitors, officials and local residents have different assumptions about who actually monitors the aid.

The mystery concerns institutional responsibility and information flow, not nostalgia as hidden authority.

## The Temporary Mark Became Familiar

A temporary navigation aid deployed during a long repair remains in place for months.

Regular users begin treating it as part of the ordinary landscape. Public directions, informal route descriptions and photographs start referencing it.

When the permanent aid returns, withdrawing the temporary mark creates a small wave of stale local knowledge.

This is useful for Public Memory, Cartography and actor knowledge because “temporary” can still accumulate history.

## The Beacon Everyone Blames the Weather For

A remote beacon disappears from view during several bad-weather windows. Everyone initially assumes the weather explains it.

Later, observations taken during clear conditions reveal that the asset is also intermittently failing.

The inverse is equally possible: monitoring data suggests a fault, but direct technical inspection shows the signal operating normally and the real problem is atmospheric/line-of-sight obstruction.

The story requires comparing independent state families instead of allowing Weather or Maintenance to swallow the entire explanation.

## The Nest on the Service Platform

A navigation aid remains technically healthy, but seasonal Pokémon use the maintenance platform or surrounding structure.

A scheduled inspection now intersects with an ecological window.

Possible responses include:
- rescheduling work;
- using a different access method;
- installing a temporary mitigation;
- performing only urgent inspection;
- conservation review;
- leaving the site alone if safe and authorized.

The presence of Pokémon does not create ownership, hostility or a mandatory battle.

## The Public Viewpoint Is Open, the Lantern Room Is Not

A lighthouse grounds/viewpoint remain open while the technical lantern/service area is restricted.

Visitors misread visible activity as either “the lighthouse is closed” or “everything is fine.”

This seed creates a clean accessibility/information design problem:
- separate public and technical access state;
- update signs correctly;
- preserve tourism where possible;
- prevent public movement from becoming proof of technical operation.

## The Historic Pokémon Signal Story

A local archive describes an older period when a specific Pokémon individual or lineage-associated household reportedly helped provide a visible coastal signal.

The record may be historically valuable without establishing a current practice.

Questions for play:
- does the source describe one individual or a general custom?
- was it a practical aid, emergency measure, ceremony or later embellishment?
- are later retellings exaggerating the original record?
- does any current institution still maintain the tradition?

No species-wide capability, work eligibility or mechanical light range is inferred.

## The Sound Signal Nobody Heard

A sound-based navigation aid is expected to operate during reduced visibility, yet several observers report hearing nothing.

Evidence can conflict because of:
- observer position;
- ambient sound;
- wind/weather presentation;
- hearing range differences that are not mechanically modeled yet;
- intermittent equipment;
- wrong expectation from a stale publication;
- the aid never being scheduled/required during that window.

Soundscapes owns acoustic observations. Technology/Maintenance owns the asset. Pass 92 links them.

## Mystery — Five Reports, Three Aids

Five witness reports appear to describe five separate coastal signals.

Reconstruction reveals that:
- two reports probably describe the same fixed light from opposite viewpoints;
- another report likely refers to a temporary mark;
- one may be a harbor/private light rather than a navigation aid;
- one remains unresolved.

The mystery uses timestamps, bearings/locations where supported, chart editions, photos, notice delivery and source lineage.

A tidy final answer is optional.

## Mystery — The Light That Failed Only From Sea

Shore observers insist a lighthouse operates correctly. Several vessel crews say its characteristic appears incomplete or missing from the approach.

Possible explanations:
- obstruction between the aid and one sector;
- characteristic/orientation configuration issue;
- observation windows differ;
- a public decorative light is being confused with the technical signal;
- atmospheric conditions affect the two viewpoints differently;
- the reports cannot yet be reconciled.

This mystery encourages players to inspect the coast as geometry and information rather than simply entering the tower and fighting something.

## Long arc — A Coast Learns Its Marks

Stage 1: Baseline

Players experience a normal coastal route. The important aids, landmarks, publications and local users are introduced before anything breaks.

Stage 2: Small discrepancy

One aid produces a minor mismatch: delayed light, uncertain position, inconsistent sound or stale chart label.

Stage 3: Investigation and mitigation

Players or local institutions reconstruct evidence. A limited operational response is introduced instead of an immediate dramatic closure.

Stage 4: Visible procedure change

The coast gains a new temporary marker, revised notice board, monitoring device, access restriction, maintenance schedule or updated chart.

Stage 5: Secondary consequence

The change affects ferry routines, fisheries, tourism, courier routes, wildlife use or community knowledge.

Stage 6: Later revisit

The original fault is gone, but the temporary measure, old chart, former operator, revised habitat or public memory becomes relevant again.

The coast accumulates history rather than resetting after repair.

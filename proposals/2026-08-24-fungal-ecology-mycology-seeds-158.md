## Thirty worldbuilding candidates

1. The Fruiting Season That Never Came — a grove famous for autumn mushrooms has no visible fruiting for two consecutive years. The underlying fungal system may still be present, and the cause remains open.
2. The Same Fungus in Three Groves — three distant patches look identical, but researchers disagree whether they represent one taxon, one clonal system or several similar organisms.
3. The Orchard Blight That Wasn't — fruiting bodies appear beside declining trees and receive immediate blame; irrigation and root damage remain equally plausible.
4. The Cave Bloom After Flood — a chamber produces an unusual fruiting episode several weeks after groundwater changed. Timing is evidence, not proof of cause.
5. The Deadwood Plot Became Important — retained logs that were once criticized as untidy become the only long-running substrate series in the region.
6. The Mushroom Harvest Closed for a Year — a community pauses collection after weak fruiting even though nobody has proven population decline.
7. Parasect at the Old Stump — the same persistent Parasect is repeatedly observed at one decaying stump; the association is real, but the ecological reason remains uncertain.
8. The Network Nobody Could See — years of surface surveys miss a fungal system later detected through root/substrate sampling.
9. The Spore Sample Was Misidentified — a lab revision changes the taxonomic determination while the original sample and collection record remain valid.
10. Nothing Happened This Fruiting Season — the monitoring team records a normal quiet year that becomes useful only after a later anomaly.
11. The Famous Ring Moved — a tourist landmark of visible mushrooms appears several meters away from the historical patch after two silent years.
12. Three Mushrooms, Two Organisms, Maybe — visually adjacent fruiting bodies produce conflicting identification results and remain unresolved.
13. The Conk on the Heritage Beam — a visible fungus triggers an inspection, but Architecture must determine whether the beam is structurally compromised.
14. The Mycorrhiza Headline — a popular article claims a tree grove is 'sharing power through mushrooms'; the scientific record supports only narrower root-fungus associations.
15. The Wrong Decomposer — a fungus on deadwood is blamed for killing the tree even though records show the tree died before the fruiting bodies appeared.
16. The Shiinotic Lights Return — lights reappear on a forest route after years of absence. Whether the same Shiinotic group, different individuals or another source is involved remains an observation problem.
17. The Amoonguss Growth Trail — mushroom-like growth appears after a documented Amoonguss spore release, but only part of the later pattern can be confidently linked.
18. The Hidden Harvest Route — local foragers know a sequence of small sites that never appears on public maps because harvest pressure and sensitive habitat are concerns.
19. The Market Has More Mushrooms Than the Forest Survey — Supply Chains reveals that sellers are sourcing from several regions, correcting a false local-abundance inference.
20. The Old Fungarium Drawer — a preserved specimen from decades ago changes the known historical range of a fungal taxon.
21. The Burn Year Fruiting — wildfire ends above ground, then several fungal fruiting patterns change over subsequent seasons without a single simple direction.
22. The Restored Forest Is Still Different — tree cover has recovered, but fungal surveys continue to differ from an older remnant woodland.
23. The Pokémon That Eats the Evidence — a local fungivore repeatedly removes fruiting bodies before survey teams arrive, creating a detection problem rather than a population collapse.
24. The Former Partner Knows the Patch — a released former partner is observed visiting the same seasonal patch years later; the record does not assume memory, ownership or teaching.
25. The Nursery Inoculation Question — a botanical nursery considers adding a fungal associate to plant stock, triggering Research Ethics, Biosecurity and provenance review before any action.
26. The Lumber Crew Found the Rare Patch — Forest Management pauses one treatment zone while identification is reviewed; the whole project does not automatically stop.
27. The Mushroom Everyone Calls Edible — a local tradition and a PTU Item identification are treated as separate authorities until the exact rules and specimen identity are verified.
28. The Root Sample From the Wrong Tree — a labeling mistake creates an apparently dramatic mycorrhizal result; Metrology/Science catches the provenance problem.
29. The Fruiting Body Became a Landmark — a large perennial conk becomes a route reference even though the biological structure changes slowly over time.
30. The Fungus Outlived the Building — a demolished workshop's damp foundation area keeps producing the same proposed fungal-system observations after the human use disappears.

## Long-form arcs

### Five Fruiting Seasons at Cloudroot Grove

Year one establishes ordinary fruiting locations and community harvest. Year two is unusually quiet. Year three produces a large visible flush after a wet period, but surveys also show changed deadwood and visitor pressure. Year four introduces a temporary harvest restriction and more systematic sampling. Year five may show strong fruiting, weak fruiting or a spatial shift depending on authored world state.

The arc never requires the revelation that one actor caused everything. Its payoff is a better Chronicle: survey effort, public memory, market pressure, forest management and seasonal ecology become legible over time.

### The Organism Nobody Saw Whole

A fungal system is first known from one visible patch. Later samples appear under nearby roots, then an apparently separate patch fruits beyond a trail. Researchers debate whether the records belong to one organism, several organisms or only the same taxon.

The campaign can preserve uncertainty indefinitely. New technology or sampling may narrow the question without requiring a final omniscient map of underground mycelium.

### From Rot Log to Research Reserve

A fallen legacy tree begins as ordinary deadwood. Over years it accumulates fungal observations, fungivore records, seedlings, school visits and a rare identification claim. The site gradually becomes valuable enough to influence a later forestry plan.

The log itself continues decomposing and may eventually cease to exist physically. Its research history persists in Chronicle, preventing conservation from requiring permanent physical stasis.

### Deadwood Plot Disturbance

FULL premise: visitors and wild Pokémon occupy a monitored deadwood plot during a short fruiting window, and staff need the route cleared without treating every Pokémon as an enemy.

FULL blockers are primarily complete movement, AI tactical policy and adapter/playback. Environmental mechanics are only added when individually verified.

REDUCED version: visitors leave and wildlife movement is resolved in world state. AutoPTU handles only an independent static confrontation if one remains.

### Orchard Root Association Survey

This is primarily a non-combat investigation. Competing explanations include fungal association change, irrigation, soil condition, crop pressure, root injury and seasonal visibility. Flora, Soil, Irrigation, IPM, Science and Fungal Ecology provide evidence. A battle cannot determine causation.

### Harvest Closure Dispute

This is primarily institutional/social. Community harvesters, conservation staff and market buyers compare fruiting observations, harvest records and uncertainty. The result may be open harvest, temporary limits, a monitoring-only year or no decision. No combat is required.

## Factions and NPC archetypes

Candidate archetypes, all non-canon:

- a field mycologist who distrusts single-season conclusions;
- a long-time forager whose locality knowledge is excellent but taxonomic terminology is outdated;
- a forestry planner balancing deadwood retention and other operational goals;
- a market buyer who knows supply provenance better than local ecology;
- a cave surveyor who records fruiting as route evidence rather than as a hazard;
- a botanical curator maintaining root-associated samples and living plant accessions;
- a community harvest steward who wants the tradition to survive by accepting occasional closure;
- a researcher whose famous early identification is later revised without scandal;
- a ranger who can recognize a recurring patch but refuses to claim the unseen organism boundary;
- a Trainer partnered with a mushroom-associated Pokémon who must not be treated as an automatic fungal expert.

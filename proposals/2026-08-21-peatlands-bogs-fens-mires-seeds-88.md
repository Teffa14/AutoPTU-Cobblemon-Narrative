# Peatland, Bog, Fen & Mire Worldbuilding Candidates — Pass 88

Status: PROPOSED / NON-CANON.

These seeds are original Ouros candidates derived from the research in `research/2026-08-21-peatlands-bogs-fens-mires-scan-88.md` and constrained by `design/peatlands-bogs-fens-mires-layer.md`.

None establishes PTU terrain, damage, statuses, spawn bonuses, legal ownership or regional canon.

## 01. The Fen That Was Called a Bog

For decades, maps and tourism brochures call the site Blackglass Bog. New groundwater surveys suggest much of it may actually be fen-like. The old name can remain culturally important while the scientific classification changes.

Useful hooks: cartography correction, museum exhibit, scientific disagreement, local identity, education.

## 02. The Boardwalk That Keeps Getting Lower

A boardwalk built decades ago appears closer to the water every survey year. Possible causes include peat subsidence, higher water, construction settling or measurement inconsistency. Players recover old benchmarks and compare them with current data.

Useful hooks: survey, infrastructure, archives, restoration planning.

## 03. Wooper Route Year Seven

A known group route through a wetland has been recorded for six consecutive seasons. In year seven, the movement stops at one ditch crossing. The problem is to determine whether the route changed, the group changed, survey timing failed or the crossing became unsuitable.

No population decline is assumed.

## 04. The Missing Bog Moss Patch

Ducklett are still observed in the region, but a moss patch used during previous foraging surveys is greatly reduced. Several nearby patches remain normal.

The investigation can connect water level, visitor trampling, shading, drought history or simple patch turnover without guaranteeing one cause.

## 05. The Ditch Nobody Remembers Building

A narrow straight channel appears on no modern infrastructure map. Old photographs show it existed before the current road but after the earliest settlement survey.

The question is what it once served, whether it still affects hydrology and whether blocking it would cause new problems elsewhere.

## 06. The Weir That Worked Too Well

A restoration weir successfully raises water in its target sector. A neighboring path becomes unusable much more often than expected.

The conflict is not “restoration versus bad locals.” The design problem is whether access, timing, structure height or route placement can be adjusted without erasing the hydrologic gain.

## 07. The Dry Hummock Archive

A small elevated patch remains dry through years when the surrounding peatland is wet. Old field notebooks show the same pattern decades earlier.

The site can become a useful reference point, shelter for some species or simply a persistent physical feature. It does not gain combat cover by default.

## 08. The Smoke After the Fire

A peatland burned during a regional wildfire weeks ago. Smoke is reported again after rain.

Players must determine whether peat still smolders, another source exists, or the report is obsolete. Crisis, Air Quality and Wildfire provide the external states; peatland evidence determines local involvement.

## 09. The Old Extraction Pools

Shallow pools form a geometric pattern unlike surrounding natural water. Archive work suggests historic cutting or extraction, but records are incomplete.

Today the pools support wildlife and may have heritage value. Restoring a “natural” surface is not automatically the correct decision.

## 10. Three Wells, Three Stories

Three monitoring wells only a short distance apart show different seasonal patterns. One appears rainfall-driven, another more stable, and the third responds strongly to a nearby channel.

The story rewards comparing data rather than finding a single magic reading.

## 11. The Footpath That Became a Drain

Years of repeated use lowered a route enough that storm water now follows it. The path remains popular because it is the shortest crossing.

Possible responses include rerouting, raised boardwalk, seasonal closure or accepting the new drainage role. No option is universally correct.

## 12. Dark Water, Wrong Accusation

Visitors report “polluted black water” and blame a nearby workshop. Historical water records show similar coloration before the workshop existed.

A real pollution issue may still exist, but color alone is not evidence. Water Quality and Science layers handle the investigation.

## 13. The Research Platform That Drifted Out of Use

A raised platform once sat beside the wettest survey plots. Hydrology shifted and the active monitoring line is now hundreds of meters away.

The old structure can become a heritage marker, birdwatching point, storage site or be removed. It keeps its own history either way.

## 14. The Frozen Fen Window

A high-elevation fen becomes easier to access during a winter freeze, but the research target is a hydrologic observation, not “safe ice combat.” Cryosphere and Travel determine access before any encounter begins.

## 15. The First Dry Summer in the Record

A peatland with decades of near-surface summer water records one sustained low-water season. Nothing catastrophic happens immediately.

The important content comes from repeated monitoring, cautious interpretation and decisions about whether to change management before another year confirms the trend.

## 16. The Road That Divided the Water

An old raised road crosses a peat wetland. Surveys suggest the two sides now respond differently to storms and drought.

The road may still be critical transport infrastructure. The long-term arc can examine culverts, hydrologic connectivity and wildlife movement without reducing the road to a villain.

## 17. Stunfisk in the Wrong Mud

A Stunfisk sighting is used publicly as “proof” that a dark wet patch contains a particular mineral-rich substrate. The scientific team points out that species presence alone cannot establish soil chemistry.

The resulting story can involve field sampling, media correction and habitat observation.

## 18. The Subsiding Shed

A small maintenance shed was built at the edge of drained organic soil decades ago. The foundation now sits unevenly.

Possible causes include peat subsidence, ordinary foundation failure, water movement or several factors together. Architecture and Infrastructure handle the building; Peatland stores the ground-history evidence.

## 19. The Fen Without Its Spring

A groundwater-fed wetland shows declining water levels even though rainfall remains normal. A spring historically associated with the site is weaker.

Freshwater/Groundwater investigation can expand beyond the wetland boundary and find causes far upslope.

## 20. The Restoration Plot Nobody Photographed

A small restoration experiment has five years of measurements but almost no visual documentation. A nearby untreated plot has excellent photographs but poor sensor records.

Players and researchers must combine incomplete evidence rather than treating one dataset as complete truth.

## 21. The Floating Mat Rumor

Travelers claim a vegetation mat “swallows people.” Actual survey notes only show that the edge position changed after high water.

The scenario is about rumor, route verification and accessibility. No sinking mechanic is assumed.

## 22. Firebreak Through the Mire

After a severe regional fire year, a proposal would create a maintained access/firebreak route through a peatland margin. The same route could improve emergency response and alter drainage or visitor pressure.

Civic Governance, Wildfire, Road Ecology and Conservation all have legitimate interests.

## 23. The Abandoned Beaver Pond Legacy

A peat-forming wet area began around an old pond complex long after the original builders disappeared from local observation records. The landscape persists even though the initiating actors are gone.

The hook demonstrates ecological legacy without requiring a currently active Pokémon engineer.

## 24. The Well Beside the Festival Route

A popular seasonal event repeatedly compacts the ground around one monitoring well. Data quality begins to change while the larger peatland seems stable.

The correct response may be moving the instrument rather than changing the ecosystem.

## 25. The Artifact Below the Water Table

Routine scientific coring encounters an unexpected cultural object. Work stops and the site transfers into an Archaeology/Custody process.

The object is not automatically ancient, valuable or magically preserved. Context and provenance matter more than spectacle.

## 26. The Restoration Species That Did Not Return

Water levels improve after a multi-year project, but one previously common Pokémon is still not observed.

Possible explanations include slow recolonization, changed regional population, altered food resources, sampling method, a new route or a restoration outcome that is good in some ways but incomplete in others.

## 27. The Former Partner at the Peat Pool

A released former partner is photographed near a peat pool years later. The observation updates that Pokémon’s Chronicle and location history.

It does not prove the Pokémon returned to its old Trainer, owns the pool or leads the local wild group.

## 28. The Historic Fire Layer

A research core contains evidence interpreted as an old burn layer. Local oral history has no matching fire story.

The mismatch can produce a science/history investigation without forcing either record to be false. Dating and interpretation remain reviewable.

## 29. The Rewetting Year With Too Much Rain

A pilot restoration happens in an unusually wet year. The first results look excellent, but researchers warn that weather and intervention effects are hard to separate.

The project deliberately delays a victory declaration until later monitoring.

## 30. The Mire Atlas

Several institutions maintain different maps of the same large wetland: public trails, groundwater, wildlife observations, fire history, archaeology and restoration works.

A multi-year atlas project does not collapse them into one “true map.” It builds linked layers with dates, confidence and provenance.

# Long Arc A — The Bog That Sank Slowly

Year 1: Old survey benchmarks suggest the peat surface is lower than historic records.

Year 2: Investigators connect part of the change to a legacy drainage network, but several zones behave differently.

Year 3: A drought lowers the water table further. Access improves temporarily while scientific concern rises.

Year 4: A wildfire affects part of the landscape. Peat involvement is verified only in selected patches.

Year 5: Rewetting begins with several control structures and a new monitoring plan.

Years 6–8: Water levels, vegetation, road access, fire susceptibility and subsidence respond at different rates. The final state is a changed landscape with a documented history, not a reset to a pristine snapshot.

# Long Arc B — Three Fens, Three Waters

Three visually similar peatlands sit within the same region.

The first is strongly groundwater-supported.

The second depends heavily on seasonal surface flow.

The third was historically modified and now receives water from both a ditch network and natural sources.

Across several years, players build monitoring networks, compare drought response and discover that management successful at one site can be harmful or irrelevant at another.

The arc teaches that visual biome labels are insufficient for causal world simulation.

# Long Arc C — The Long Rewetting

A settlement, conservation institution and research team agree to restore a drained peatland margin.

The physical intervention is only the opening act.

Later chapters cover boardwalk access, monitoring wells, altered visitor routes, Pokémon observation changes, an unusually dry summer, maintenance failure, a public dispute over whether the project “worked,” and eventual revision of the management plan.

The satisfying endpoint is a region that learned how its own peatland works.

Full version:

The arena contains a water-control point whose operation changes zone accessibility while a field team must be protected. Pokémon and opponents can alter their routes as the state changes.

- full turn/round lifecycle — PARTIAL;
- full stateful damage pipeline — PARTIAL;
- status lifecycle — PARTIAL;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions — BLOCKING;
- move-specific behavior — PARTIAL;
- abilities — PARTIAL;
- items — PARTIAL;
- Trainer Features/perks — PARTIAL;
- AI legal-action infrastructure — VERIFIED;

- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics adapter/playback — BLOCKING.

Reduced version:

# Encounter Contract B — Smoldering Peat Edge

Full version:

Players survey a previously burned site while uncertain unsafe patches and smoke conditions change. The encounter may require withdrawal, rescue and AI avoidance of hazards.

Blocking dependencies:

- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions;
- AI tactical policy;
- Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics playback;
- complete movement if interception/rescue/displacement is used.

Partial dependencies remain lifecycle, damage, statuses, Moves, Abilities, Items and Trainer Features where individual mechanics are invoked.

Reduced version:

Wildfire/Crisis determines the verified safe perimeter first. Any combat uses only that safe static patch. Smoke and peat do not apply damage, Burned, Poisoned or Accuracy changes.

# Encounter Contract C — Floating Mat Survey

Full version:

Surveyors verify a route across a shifting wetland surface while wildlife uses different patches. The final form could include unstable zones, route changes and withdrawal behavior.

Blocking dependencies:

- complete movement including forced movement/interception;
- terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions;
- AI tactical policy;
- Minecraft adapter/playback.

Reduced version:

Route verification is an exploration/world-state sequence. Once a stable area is identified, any battle occurs on a frozen conventional arena with ordinary movement and static blockers.

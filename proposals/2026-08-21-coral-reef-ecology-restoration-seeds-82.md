# Ouros Coral Reef Ecology & Restoration Seeds — Pass 82

Status: NON-CANON proposals. These are original Ouros candidates derived from high-level research patterns. They do not copy external characters, locations, dialogue or plots.

## 01 — The Reef That Still Looks Fine

A popular reef remains colorful in tourist photographs, but repeated survey stations show declining recruitment in one zone. The problem is not visible enough to create immediate public concern. Players can help compare historical photography, current transects and water-quality data before anyone claims the reef is collapsing.

## 02 — The Broken Crest

A storm removes structural complexity from one reef crest while the lagoon remains largely unchanged. The same named reef now has two very different recovery problems. Research, navigation and visitor access all need to use zone-specific state.

## 03 — Three Restoration Plots

Three restoration plots installed in the same year produce different outcomes because their exposure, substrate and disturbance histories differ. The institution has to decide whether to repeat, modify or stop one method without pretending the original work never happened.

## 04 — Warm-Current Boundary

Toxapex and Bruxish are repeatedly observed contesting one current edge. The behavior becomes more frequent after the boundary shifts. The story begins as observation; whether intervention is needed remains open.

## 05 — The Empty Survey Frame

A permanent photo station records several weeks with almost no visible Pokémon. Camera failure, altered routes, visitor pressure, seasonal timing and real ecological change are all plausible. An empty frame does not become an absence claim until coverage is checked.

## 06 — The Anchor Scar

A reef site contains a physical scar attributed publicly to an old anchoring incident. Archival records are incomplete. Players can separate what is structurally visible now from what can actually be proven about the original cause.

## 07 — Nursery Transfer Day

A restoration cohort has a documented transfer window between a nursery facility and an outplant site. A route problem threatens the schedule. The challenge is logistics and provenance; the cohort is not a battle item and cannot be duplicated by restarting the encounter.

## 08 — The Reef After the Festival

A coastal festival sharply increases boat and snorkel traffic for one week. Researchers observe new disturbance patterns afterward, but the evidence does not support blaming every change on visitors. Tourism records, weather and pre-event observations all matter.

## 09 — The Rubble Nursery

A rubble field once considered low-value begins receiving repeated juvenile Pokémon observations. A proposed cleanup would make navigation easier but may remove emerging habitat. The design question is whether the zone should be stabilized, partially cleared or left alone.

## 10 — Corsola Station Seven

Corsola were historically recorded at one survey station but have not been observed there recently. Players compare effort, season, water condition and neighboring stations before declaring a population loss. A returning individual can be a callback without proving recovery.

## 11 — The Wrong Healthy Reef

A guidebook labels one reef as the region's healthiest because its imagery is visually impressive. Scientific records show the label is outdated and overly broad. Correcting the guidebook creates a media/tourism problem, not a battle.

## 12 — Storm Repair Before Science

A damaged dive platform must be repaired quickly for safety, but construction access overlaps a monitoring plot. Workers and scientists agree on the need for repairs while disagreeing on sequencing. There is no villain; the conflict is timing and evidence preservation.

## 13 — The Pale Patch

A pale reef patch appears after a heat-stress alert. Some observers call it bleaching immediately; others want repeated observations. The system preserves the observation first and allows later evidence to establish what happened.

## 14 — The Survivor Cohort

Most of one restoration cohort is lost during a disturbance, but a small group persists. The surviving cohort becomes scientifically important without gaining mystical resilience or a mechanical bonus.

## 15 — After the Grounding

A grounded vessel is removed quickly, but the reef remains structurally altered. Years later, the site is simultaneously a restoration project, navigation case study, photography archive and habitat.

## 16 — The Reef Nobody Visits

A remote reef receives little tourism and little monitoring. Public discourse describes it as pristine mainly because nobody has much data. An expedition can discover either strong condition, serious stress, or simply uncertainty.

## 17 — Two Good Maps

A dive operator and a research institute use different reef maps. Both are correct for the dates when they were surveyed. A recent structural change makes neither fully current.

## 18 — The Outplant Tags

Several restoration tags are found far from their recorded positions after a storm. The material may have moved, the map may be wrong or records may have been entered incorrectly. The tags are evidence, not proof of fraud.

## 19 — Reef School

A local training program teaches visitors how to move through a reef site without contacting sensitive structures. The program becomes part of local culture and employment. It grants no PTU movement bonus by itself.

## 20 — The Quiet Current

A current edge that usually concentrates visible activity becomes unusually quiet. The change produces a research opportunity tied to ocean condition, seasonality and behavior history rather than an automatic encounter.

## 21 — The Reef and the New Harbor

A harbor expansion alters traffic, lighting and sediment exposure near a reef over several years. Some changes are positive for access and emergency response; others need mitigation. The project evolves through monitoring rather than one binary approval quest.

## 22 — Restoration Anniversary

A town celebrates ten years since a major reef project. Public memory focuses on the launch event, while archived monitoring shows several revisions and setbacks. Players can help create a more accurate exhibition without invalidating community pride.

## 23 — The Missing Recruitment Year

A scientific archive has a gap during a year when several major environmental changes occurred. Later researchers must avoid filling the blank with assumptions. Old diver logs, photographs and unrelated institutional records may provide partial evidence.

## 24 — The Reef That Became a Wreck Habitat

A small wreck gradually becomes incorporated into reef habitat. Heritage staff, divers and conservation actors all value the same object for different reasons. Salvage, removal and preservation claims remain separate from ecological observations.

## 25 — The False Restoration Success

A project reports excellent immediate survival after outplanting and gains public praise. Later monitoring shows the result did not persist. The story rewards transparent revision rather than exposing a secret villain.

## 26 — The Unplanned Refuge

One sheltered reef pocket experiences less stress than surrounding zones. Researchers want to know why before using it as a restoration source or refuge. The game never assumes that protection comes from a Legendary or special hidden mechanic.

## 27 — Reef Volunteers

A volunteer monitoring program expands observation coverage dramatically, but methods vary between groups. The challenge becomes calibration, training and provenance. High participation is useful without turning every observation into equal-quality evidence.

## 28 — The Boundary Dispute

Two institutions draw different boundaries around the same reef system because one maps geomorphology and the other manages visitor access. The disagreement is partly semantic. Players can help create cross-references without forcing a single map to replace all others.

## 29 — The Reef at Low Visibility

A sediment plume reduces underwater visibility for several days. Tourism declines and rumors spread that the reef has died. The actual ecological response remains unknown until later observations.

## 30 — The Long Transect

One research team maintains the same transect for decades. Player actions, storms, heat events, restoration work and shifting populations gradually turn it into one of the best historical records in the region. Its value comes from continuity rather than a final discovery.

# Long arc A — Five Summers at Blueglass Reef

Year one establishes baseline zones, visitor routes and a popular public image. Year two brings a heat-stress event with uneven bleaching observations. Year three begins several restoration trials. Year four adds a major storm that damages some restored structure while leaving another zone intact. Year five forces institutions to compare what actually persisted against the stories people tell about the reef.

Possible outcomes include a revised monitoring network, changed tourism access, a successful restoration method limited to certain zones, discontinued interventions, stronger public science communication or a decision to prioritize water-quality work outside the reef itself.

The arc does not require a villain or a boss.

# Long arc B — Harbor and Reef

A small harbor and nearby reef both become more important as the region grows. Better transport increases employment, research access and emergency response, while vessel traffic, construction and visitor pressure create new exposures. Over several seasons, players can participate in survey, public works, route design, reef education, restoration and crisis response.

The core question is not whether growth is good or bad. It is which physical changes occur, who observes them, what mitigation works and how the institutions adapt.

# Long arc C — The Restoration Archive

A regional institution inherits decades of reef projects with uneven documentation. Some were successful, some failed, some cannot be evaluated and some used methods no longer preferred. Players help recover provenance, revisit sites and build a versioned archive that influences future restoration decisions.

The final value is institutional memory. No project is retroactively rewritten to fit a clean success story.

# Encounter contract B — Nursery Transfer Interruption

FULL intent:

Players protect a restoration transfer while staff move a documented cohort between facilities. A future implementation could support route clearing, escorts, interception and withdrawal while preserving noncombatant and cohort state.

Key blocking families are complete movement/forced movement/interception, terrain/weather/hazards/zones/reactions, AI tactical policy and Minecraft/Cobblemon/Craftics playback.

REDUCED version:

Staff and the restoration cohort remain outside the tactical grid. Players resolve one static chokepoint battle. The narrative server then advances, delays or aborts the transfer and retains the same cohort/provenance IDs.

# Encounter contract C — Warm-Current Boundary

FULL intent:

A local Toxapex/Bruxish conflict occurs around a current boundary. The full version could model a verified current zone and distinct territorial/withdrawal goals if those systems are eventually authoritative.

REDUCED version:

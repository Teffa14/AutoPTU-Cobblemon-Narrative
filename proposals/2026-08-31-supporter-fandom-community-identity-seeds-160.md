# Ouros Worldbuilding Seeds — Supporters, Fandom & Community Identity

Status: NON-CANON PROPOSALS. Nothing in this file is established Ouros history.
Date: 2026-08-31
Pass: 160
Depends on: `design/supporter-fandom-community-identity-continuity-extension.md`

## Design intent

These seeds create social continuity around public figures and institutions without converting popularity into mechanics, faction control or universal opinion. Each concept is written so it can be promoted, rejected or adapted independently.

## The Scarf Nobody Officially Designed

A locally recognizable scarf appears at several public events. No Gym, performer or institution commissioned it. The first surviving examples seem homemade, later copies differ in pattern, and shops eventually sell lookalikes.

Useful questions:

- who made the earliest documented version;
- whether the current pattern descends from one object or several similar ones;
- when the supported subject first acknowledged it;
- which objects are supporter-made, commercial copies or later commemorative editions.

Material Culture owns every physical instance. Supporter continuity owns group meaning. A popular pattern does not become an official uniform.

## Five Times Someone Said “Everyone Supports Her”

Five sources use essentially the same phrase while referring to different populations:

- the people in one venue section;
- a local fan club;
- a neighborhood where the subject grew up;
- readers responding to one article;
- a travelling group arriving for a final.

The mystery is solved by reconstructing scope. Nobody needs to have lied.

Output lesson: `LOUD_VISIBLE_SAMPLE != WHOLE_COMMUNITY`.

## The Away Group That Never Arrived

A supporter association announces a large trip to an event. A route disruption, booking failure or unrelated local problem prevents most of the group from reaching the destination. Contemporary reporting continues to repeat the announced number.

Players can later reconcile announcement, ticket allocation, travel records, lodging records and actual arrival evidence.

No tactical content is required.

## The Banner With Two Owners

An old banner is physically held by one successor group while another claims cultural inheritance from the organization that originally created it.

Possible outcomes:

- physical custody stays where it is;
- both groups receive archival reproductions;
- provenance reveals that the banner predated the split and belonged to neither successor exclusively;
- the dispute remains unresolved by mutual choice.

A battle can never decide ownership.

## The Champion Nobody Cheers for Loudly

A consistently successful competitor has strong recognition but little organized supporter culture. Their matches still draw interest because of competitive importance.

This seed exists to preserve a basic distinction:

`RECOGNITION != ORGANIZED_SUPPORT`.

It also prevents the generator from assigning every top-ranked NPC a fan club.

## The Local Favorite Who Rarely Wins

A Trainer, performer or team has mediocre formal results but deep local attachment built through years of public presence, work, mentorship or regional identity.

The following can all coexist:

- low competitive rank;
- high local recognition;
- durable event attendance;
- critical supporter discussion;
- no mechanical bonus.

## The Group That Supports the Gym, Not the Leader

A supporter organization predates the current Gym Leader. When leadership changes, the group welcomes the new Leader but retains rituals associated with the institution rather than the individual.

Later, a smaller internal group continues to follow the former Leader personally.

This creates two valid continuity paths without forcing a hostile split.

## The Leader Who Left but the Songs Stayed

A public figure retires. Some supporter practices remain attached to the venue and are gradually reinterpreted by younger participants who never saw the original subject perform or battle.

If the practice eventually becomes a real tradition, Ritual/Tradition must promote it explicitly. This proposal alone does not.

## The Fan Archive Under the Stairs

A small room contains decades of programs, homemade newsletters, photos, ticket stubs, banners and hand-written event notes. The archive has gaps because volunteers preserved what mattered to them rather than what an institution would have catalogued.

The archive is valuable for provenance precisely because it is partial.

Potential hooks:

- identify an unnamed person appearing repeatedly in photos;
- reconcile an event date that official records got wrong;
- find the earliest known use of a nickname;
- discover that a supposedly official slogan began as fan material;
- preserve the archive during a venue renovation without declaring every note historically true.

## The Photo Album That Became More Famous Than the Season

Supporters publish a photo collection around a public figure. Years later, most people remember the images more clearly than the actual competitive results from that period.

Public Memory may record the cultural effect. Chronicle still preserves the season facts separately.

## The Subject Who Publicly Distances Themself

A supporter subgroup obstructs access or harasses a rival despite no instruction from the person they claim to support. The subject publicly asks them to stop.

Possible consequences:

- the subgroup changes behavior;
- some members leave;
- another group forms around a different norm;
- media continues to associate the behavior with the subject for a while;
- the subject's statement becomes part of later provenance.

`GROUP_ACTION != SUBJECT_ORDER` remains mandatory.

## The Welcome Committee That Became a Club

A one-time group forms to welcome a returning Trainer or expedition. The people enjoy organizing together and later formalize into a supporter association.

The formal group therefore has an origin event that was not itself a fan club event.

This is useful for organic organization growth.

## The Club That Became a Volunteer Network

A supporter group begins helping with event cleanup, visitor directions or archival work. Over time, its volunteer role matters as much as the original attachment to a competitor or performer.

The group can develop relationships with Event, Hospitality or Civic systems without gaining formal authority automatically.

## The Supporter Liaison Who Disagrees With Both Sides

An NPC trusted by both an institution and several supporter groups often has to communicate positions they personally dislike.

Useful character tension:

- credibility comes from accurate transmission rather than agreement;
- private belief differs from public role;
- a mistake can damage trust without proving bad faith;
- leaving the role does not erase prior relationships.

This NPC is not an exposition machine and only knows information actually received through valid channels.

## The Rival Clubs Sharing a Train

Two supporter groups travel to the same event through one transport route. They have different customs and old competitive tension but no current hostility.

A route disruption creates practical cooperation around lodging, food, schedule changes and information.

The interesting play is logistical and social. No battle is required.

## The Quiet Section

A venue area known for long-time supporters becomes less visibly expressive than newer groups. Outsiders assume enthusiasm has disappeared, while interviews and attendance records show continued attachment expressed differently.

This prevents a single animation vocabulary from becoming the truth model for support.

## The New Chant Nobody Agreed On

Several variations of a chant or call emerge around the same season. Media later describes one as “the” chant.

Provenance can preserve:

- earliest observed versions;
- local variants;
- the version used at a famous event;
- later standardized wording;
- communities that never adopted it.

No protected real-world chant should be copied into canon.

## The Merchandise That Outsold the Club

A fashionable item associated with a public figure spreads to people with no meaningful supporter relationship.

This creates a useful false inference:

`MERCHANDISE_OWNED != SUPPORT_PROVEN`.

A case or social mystery may initially overestimate a group's size by counting visible items.

## The Former Rival in the Front Row

A long-running rival retires from formal competition and later attends major events involving their former opponent. Media frames this as friendship, hostility, nostalgia or strategic interest depending on source.

Ouros stores attendance and any validated interaction. It does not infer the private relationship without evidence.

## The Fan Who Became Staff

A long-time supporter later takes a legitimate job with the venue, Gym, performer or institution.

Historical supporter membership remains true, but current employee duties require a separate Workplace relationship.

`FORMER_SUPPORTER_HISTORY + CURRENT_EMPLOYMENT` does not equal conflicted loyalty automatically.

## The Staff Member Who Became a Fan

The reverse path also exists. An employee leaves a job and later participates in supporter activity as a private person.

The old access level, credentials and institutional authority do not travel with them.

## The Split Over a Venue Move

A group agrees that it supports the subject but divides over a proposed venue relocation. One subgroup values accessibility and capacity. Another values local history and neighborhood identity.

Civic Governance may receive their testimony if an authored process permits it. This layer records their positions and participation, not the decision outcome.

## The Boycott With Normal Attendance

A highly visible subgroup announces a boycott. Overall attendance changes little because other groups and casual spectators fill the capacity.

Possible truthful statements include:

- the boycott occurred among the announcing group;
- total attendance remained stable;
- the target institution felt little immediate revenue/attendance effect;
- media overstated or understated the campaign depending on source.

This seed trains the system to separate participation from aggregate outcomes.

## The Boycott Nobody Can Measure

A different campaign has no reliable baseline. Attendance falls, but weather, transport disruption and a competing event also changed.

Correct result: causal attribution remains unresolved.

## The Supporters Who Adopted the Successor Slowly

After leadership succession, older supporters remain polite but emotionally distant. Newer members attach rapidly to the successor. Over several seasons the group identity changes without a formal split.

This supports generational continuity without hidden loyalty arithmetic.

## The Subject Who Never Acknowledges the Group

A legitimate supporter community exists around a reclusive or uninterested public figure. The figure never endorses or meets it.

The community remains real because its identity is evidenced independently.

`SUBJECT_ACKNOWLEDGEMENT != GROUP_EXISTENCE`.

## The Group With No Name

A recurring set of people travels together, uses the same meeting place and creates shared artifacts but rejects formal naming or membership.

Ouros can preserve the network as an informal supporter community without forcing institutionalization.

## The Name Everyone Else Uses

Outsiders give an informal group a nickname the participants themselves rarely use. Both labels can exist with provenance.

The public-facing UI should distinguish self-name from exonym or media nickname when known.

## The First Member Is Disputed

Years after a group formalizes, several people are called its “first supporter.” Evidence shows different meanings:

- first person documented cheering for the subject;
- first organizer of travel;
- first registered member;
- first elected coordinator;
- first person named in later anniversary material.

The mystery ends by clarifying categories rather than selecting one mythical founder.

## The Group That Outlived Its Subject

A public figure dies, retires permanently or otherwise leaves public activity through separately established canon. The supporter organization becomes an archival, charitable, commemorative or community group.

Death/Loss and Public Memory own the relevant facts. This layer only records the group's transition.

## The Supporter Who Knows Less Than the Tourist

A local fan has deep emotional investment but has never seen a particular old event or document. A visiting researcher has better factual knowledge about that narrow subject.

Attachment and expertise remain separate dimensions.

## The Watch Party That Created a Friendship

A group unable to travel gathers locally to follow a public event through whatever media technology canon supports. Two NPCs meet there and later develop a relationship.

Media owns the broadcast. Social Bonds owns the relationship. Supporter continuity owns the participation context.

## The Match Everybody Remembers Through One Photo

One image becomes the dominant public representation of an event, even though it captures an unrepresentative moment.

Photography/Media/Public Memory preserve image provenance and circulation. Battle truth remains in AutoPTU/Chronicle.

## The Supporter Map

A community archive tries to map where its members or sympathizers live. The map is incomplete and self-reported.

It can support travel planning or event outreach, but it must never become an omniscient population map.

## Long arc — The Colors Change Meaning

Phase one establishes ordinary supporter practices around several public subjects. A scarf color, meeting place and nickname have local meanings.

Phase two introduces leadership changes, a retirement and a new generation of participants. Some practices follow people; others remain attached to places.

Phase three produces a public controversy in which outsiders incorrectly treat one visual symbol as proof of membership in a particular subgroup.

The players can reconstruct provenance through photos, event records, interviews, artifacts and membership evidence.

The payoff is a more precise social map, not a revelation that one hidden faction controlled every group.

## Long arc — The Season Nobody Agrees Was the Best

Several supporter communities remember the same old season differently. One values wins, another travel culture, another the emergence of a beloved roster, another a community response to a crisis that happened around the venue.

The Chronicle result is fixed. The question is why each group remembers it differently.

This arc can connect Public Memory, Travel, Battle Institutions, Media and Material Culture while keeping subjective valuation legitimate.

## Long arc — From Fan Group to Civic Stakeholder

A small supporter club grows over years because it runs useful volunteer projects and maintains a venue archive. When a future public-space proposal affects the venue district, Civic Governance recognizes the group as one stakeholder among several.

The supporter layer records how the group acquired continuity and credibility. Governance still decides consultation scope and formal authority.

The group can lose a consultation and remain important.

## Canon promotion questions

Before any seed becomes canon, establish the subject, location, time period, supporter identity, evidence for group existence, relationship to formal institutions, artifact ownership, media channels, liaison arrangements and any player-character consent boundaries.

If a supporter action is supposed to alter PTU mechanics, stop promotion until the exact PTU/Caelo rule and live AutoPTU-Java contract are verified. Narrative enthusiasm alone never creates a battle modifier.

Status: RESEARCH / PROVENANCE

Scope: narrative/world ecology research only; no canon mutation

## Why this pass exists

The repository already has substantial ecology work, including Pass 235 on temporal niches, migration and stopovers. This pass does not create another migration model. It isolates a different problem: how Ouros can use an acoustic environment as evidence without treating silence as proof that a population disappeared.

The useful narrative distinction is between presence, audibility, communication success and interpretation. A Pokémon can remain physically present while its calls are masked. A recorder can fail. A species can alter calling time. Weather or terrain can change transmission. An observer can simply be listening in the wrong place. Conversely, a quiet site can be a real ecological warning. The investigation should establish which explanation is supported rather than collapse all quiet into absence.

## Public source findings

### Natural sound is ecological information, not atmosphere only

The U.S. National Park Service describes natural sound as part of ecosystem function. Wildlife can use acoustic information for communication, territory, finding habitat and food, detecting predators, courtship and care of young. NPS monitoring also treats changes in soundscape as information about resource conditions.

Sources:

- National Park Service, `Why Sounds Matter`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/sound/effects.htm
- National Park Service, `Soundscapes of Alaska`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/aknatureandscience/sounds.htm

Reusable lesson for Ouros:

- a soundscape can carry discoverable world-state information;
- the player should be able to compare locations and times rather than receive one omniscient ecology verdict;
- hearing a call can support presence or behavior claims only at the specificity justified by the source and observation.

### Anthropogenic noise can mask communication without removing animals

NPS material reports that human-generated noise can mask natural sound and interfere with wildlife communication and behavior. NOAA work on Cook Inlet belugas gives a concrete example in which commercial ship noise can partially or completely mask calls within relevant frequencies. A 2024 NOAA-indexed killer-whale study also found vessel noise associated with impaired foraging behavior and success.

Sources:

- National Park Service, `Noise - Natural Sounds`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://home.nps.gov/subjects/sound/noise.htm
- NOAA Fisheries, `Cook Inlet Beluga Whale Vocal Repertoire Documented for the First Time`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/vocal-repertoire-cook-inlet-beluga-whales-documented-first-time
- NOAA repository, Holt et al. 2024, `Males miss and females forgo: Auditory masking from vessel noise impairs foraging efficiency and success in killer whales`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/66343

Reusable lesson for Ouros:

- `not detected acoustically` must not mean `not present`;
- a new machine, route, pump, vehicle corridor or industrial schedule can change what observers hear while leaving occupancy initially unchanged;
- masking can create a mystery before any direct injury or combat occurs;
- mitigation can be temporal or operational, not necessarily destruction of infrastructure.

No real-world thresholds or decibel values are imported as Pokémon rules.

### Sensory assumptions must remain species-specific

USGS notes that assessing acoustic impact depends on the hearing range and context of the species involved. Different animals can be sensitive to different frequencies and environments. This is directly relevant to Pokémon because the franchise includes species with explicitly unusual acoustic behavior.

Source:

- U.S. Geological Survey, `In-Air and Underwater Hearing Abilities of Seabirds`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.usgs.gov/centers/eesc/science/air-and-underwater-hearing-abilities-seabirds

Reusable lesson for Ouros:

- no generic `all Pokémon hear this` rule;
- species-specific acoustic clues require source-backed behavior or an approved Ouros overlay;
- an acoustic monitor designed for one population may miss another.

### Official Pokémon material supports species-specific communication, including signals humans cannot perceive

The official Pokémon Pokédex describes several species whose behavior depends on sound in materially different ways. Tympole communicates using sound waves that people and other Pokémon species may not hear, with surface ripples acting as an observable secondary sign. Chimecho uses multiple cries to communicate and produces air vibrations. Exploud uses varied sounds to communicate with its own kind. Whismur has extreme vocal output and the Soundproof ability is explicitly associated with immunity to sound-based moves.

Sources:

- The Pokémon Company, official Pokédex, `Tympole`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.pokemon.com/uk/pokedex/tympole
- The Pokémon Company, official Pokédex, `Chimecho`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokedex/chimecho
- The Pokémon Company, official Pokédex, `Exploud`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.pokemon.com/br/pokedex/exploud
- The Pokémon Company, official Pokédex, `Whismur`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokedex/whismur

Reusable lesson for Ouros:

- observable secondary effects can be better clues than literal audible calls;
- species can have communication channels unavailable to a human observer;
- a Trainer or Pokémon companion cannot infer hidden acoustic information merely because some other species has an appropriate trait;
- Soundproof as a battle Ability does not automatically define all ecological hearing behavior outside combat.

None of these species is assigned to the new Ouros proposal by this research note. Species placement remains unapproved until geography/ecology canon supports it.

The official New Pokémon Snap site makes repeated field observation part of progression. Revisited routes can show different behavior, day/night variants expose different activity, alternate paths change observation opportunities, and player tools can provoke observable responses.

Sources:

## Proposed narrative structures

### Silence as a question, not an answer

A ranger reports that a familiar dawn exchange has disappeared from one basin. The first quest objective is to reproduce the observation at multiple sites and times. The player can discover that the silence begins and ends with an infrastructure schedule, or that the calls shifted to another window, or that the population really moved. Each is a different conclusion with different consequences.

### Triangulated listening

Three listening posts provide different evidence. A ridge detects a distant signal; a culvert masks it; a wetland edge shows secondary signs despite apparent silence. This creates spatial reasoning without requiring real-time sound propagation.

### Operational experiment

An infrastructure operator can authorize a short shutdown or schedule change. Comparing before/after observations can support a causal claim. The experiment should never guarantee a single explanation; weather, time and animal behavior remain possible confounders unless independently controlled by authored evidence.

### Acoustic false positive

A repeated mechanical rhythm or echo can resemble an expected signal. A recorder, NPC or inexperienced observer can misclassify it. Provenance must preserve the raw observation separately from the interpretation.

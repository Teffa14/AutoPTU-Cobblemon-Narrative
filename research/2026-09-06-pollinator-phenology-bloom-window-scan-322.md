## Source-backed design lessons

### Flowering is a timed state, not a static visual property

USGS research records show that flowering dates can shift with climate, precipitation, plant-community composition, elevation, soil conditions, and other local factors. Neighboring populations can diverge enough that pooled regional data hides meaningful local timing differences.

Reusable Ouros lesson: a rendered field of flowers is not sufficient evidence that two nearby patches are in the same phenological state. Survey date, patch identity, elevation/microclimate, and provenance matter.

Sources:
- USGS, “Flowering phenology shifts in response to biodiversity loss,” 2017: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/flowering-phenology-shifts-response-biodiversity-loss
- USGS, “Climate change: Flowering time may be shifting in surprising ways,” 2020: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/climate-change-flowering-time-may-be-shifting-surprising-ways
- USGS, “Dominant Sonoran Desert plant species have divergent phenological responses to climate change,” 2021: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/dominant-sonoran-desert-plant-species-have-divergent-phenological-responses-climate

### Synchrony can matter as much as abundance

US Forest Service material on salmonberry records flowering windows that vary geographically and notes correspondence between flowering and arrival of an important pollinator in some areas. The important abstraction is temporal overlap: two populations may both be locally present while their useful interaction window narrows or moves.

Reusable Ouros lesson: low observed visitation does not prove pollinator absence. A problem may arise from timing, location, observer schedule, weather, alternative forage, or management actions that change overlap.

Source:
- USDA Forest Service FEIS, Rubus spectabilis: https://research.fs.usda.gov/feis/species-reviews/rubspe

## Reusable Ouros model

Keep these states separate:

1. physical plant/community state;
2. bloom timing and usable bloom window;
3. pollinator presence or availability;
4. opportunity for visitation;
5. concrete observation at a specific time/place;
6. actor receipt of that observation;
7. interpretation;
8. management or institutional decision;
9. delayed ecological/economic consequence.

A failed observation is not proof of absence. A successful observation is not proof of sufficient pollination. Fruit or seed outcome is delayed evidence and must not be treated as an immediate sensor. A regional average cannot silently replace a patch-specific observation.

## Original Ouros patterns derived from the research

### Asynchronous evidence

One orchard block can be in peak bloom while a cooler pocket is not yet open and a sun-exposed margin is already past peak. Two observers who visited on different dates can both report accurately and still appear to contradict one another.

### Ecological and commercial stakes can coexist

A cooperative may need reliable yield. A habitat steward may want wild margins preserved. A pollinator monitor may resist conclusions based on one survey. A logistics buyer may have commitments based on expected harvest. A water or grounds crew may have changed irrigation, pruning, mowing, or access for legitimate operational reasons. Conflict can emerge without sabotage.

### Revisit is part of the investigation

A first visit establishes patch identity and current bloom state. A later visit can show whether the window moved, visitation appeared, management changed, or a delayed outcome emerged. The location itself carries the story forward.

### Feature-scoped consequences

A resolution can alter one orchard block, one verge, one mowing schedule, one monitoring plot, or one market commitment without globally declaring the district “fixed.” This fits the existing decision-dependency and selective-consequence-repair model.

## Candidate hooks

- One block reports normal flowering but unexpectedly low visitation while a nearby unmanaged margin remains active.
- A cooperative’s historical bloom calendar no longer matches the cold pocket at the bottom of the property.
- A maintenance action changed the only early-season refuge strip, but whether that caused the yield problem is unresolved.
- A monitoring route samples only the sunny edge and systematically misses the later interior window.
- A Pokémon-associated nectar route shifts toward an alternative forage patch after a local timing change.
- A buyer blames “missing pollinators,” while the field evidence points toward a narrower overlap problem and an overconfident forecast.

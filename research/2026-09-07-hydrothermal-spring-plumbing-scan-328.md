## Public-source findings

### Dynamic hydrothermal systems

USGS describes geyser behavior as dependent on a subsurface reservoir and a constricted conduit. Eruption intervals and activity can change when the natural underground plumbing changes, including changes that are not fully understood. This supports a high-level narrative rule: a change in visible output can be real evidence without identifying which underground connection changed.

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, "Yellowstone's Active Hydrothermal System", https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/yellowstone/science/yellowstones-active-hydrothermal-system

NPS describes Norris Geyser Basin as highly dynamic. New hot springs and geysers can appear while others become dormant; even small earthquakes can alter behavior; water levels and chemistry in underground reservoirs fluctuate. The same source explicitly notes that not every disturbance has a fully understood cause.

Source: National Park Service, "Norris Geyser Basin Trailhead", https://www.nps.gov/places/000/norris-geyser-basin-trailhead.htm

NPS also describes Mammoth Hot Springs travertine features as changing rapidly because dissolved calcium carbonate is deposited as water reaches the surface. This provides a second timescale: surface mineral evidence can preserve prior flow patterns while current flow has already moved elsewhere.

Source: National Park Service, "Mammoth Hot Springs - Lower Terraces", https://www.nps.gov/places/000/mammoth-hot-springs-lower-terraces.htm

USGS research on Yellowstone travertine shows that mineral deposits can preserve evidence of earlier hydrothermal conditions and groundwater recharge over long periods. The useful abstraction is temporal provenance, not Yellowstone geology itself.

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, "Travertine records climate-induced transformations of the Yellowstone hydrothermal system from the late Pleistocene to the present", 2024, https://www.usgs.gov/publications/travertine-records-climate-induced-transformations-yellowstone-hydrothermal-system

### Safety and access management

NPS treats hydrothermal access as feature-specific because thin crust, very hot water, runoff, and gases can create hazards. For Ouros, the design lesson is institutional rather than mechanical: an authority can legitimately close one boardwalk or observation sector while keeping another open, and that decision should have evidence, scope, recipients, and revision history.

Sources:
- National Park Service, Yellowstone Safety, https://www.nps.gov/yell/planyourvisit/safety.htm

## Reusable design structures

A hydrothermal mystery should preserve several separate records:

1. Persistent physical feature identity: spring, vent, terrace, boardwalk, runoff channel, monitoring point.
2. Authored current surface output: flow observed, steam observed, dry basin observed, mineral deposition observed, output changed.
3. Historical observations with timestamps and provenance.
4. Hidden or unresolved subsurface explanation, which may remain unknown.
5. Actor-specific receipt: who actually obtained the log, sample, maintenance report, warning, or eyewitness account.
6. Institutional decisions scoped to particular features.
7. Later consequences and monitoring results.

A visible feature can change without changing identity. A dormant outlet can retain useful historical evidence. A newly active vent does not prove direct connection to the spring that weakened. A mineral trace can support earlier flow without proving current flow.

## Original Ouros hooks derived at high level

- A public spring loses heat or flow while a nearby vent becomes newly active.
- An old mineral terrace records a former outlet that does not match the current path.
- A monitoring crew and a maintenance crew submit correct but differently scoped reports.
- A facility relies economically or socially on one feature and pressures an authority to reopen it before the cause is known.
- A closure protects a narrow sector but unintentionally shifts visitors, workers, or Pokémon toward another feature.
- A later return reveals that the first intervention changed access but did not resolve the subsurface process.

These are transformed structures, not copied plots.

## Why this scan exists

The narrative repository already models field search, institutional delegation, communication, closures, persistent aftermath, public memory, custody, recurring events, and ecological change. This scan looks at a different seam: what communities do before an incident, how they rehearse a plan, how they account for people and Pokémon companions during relocation, and how a failed assumption becomes a concrete plan revision instead of disappearing after the scene.

A repository-wide tree and keyword review was performed before writing. Existing emergency-adjacent material is reused rather than replaced. In particular, field-search cases remain the authority for a genuinely unaccounted-for person, institutional delegation remains the authority for role scope, communications remains the authority for distributed notices and corrections, shared-resource access remains the authority for closures and restricted spaces, and persistent aftermath remains the authority once actual physical damage exists.

## Public sources and reusable structures

### FEMA HSEEP improvement planning

Source: FEMA Preparedness Toolkit, “Improvement Planning — HSEEP Resources.”
URL: https://preptoolkit.fema.gov/web/hseep-resources/improvement-planning

Reusable structure: an exercise is controlled evidence about capability. It creates observations, an after-action record, corrective actions, ownership, and later verification. The useful narrative lesson is that completing a drill should not grant a universal READY flag. A drill can reveal a stale route, unclear authority, a communication gap, missing equipment, or an inaccessible muster location. Those findings can become ordinary work for named residents.

Source: FEMA, “Exercise Evaluation Guides — HSEEP Resources.”

Reusable structure: evaluation can compare exercise objectives, observed critical tasks, and capability targets. Ouros can transform this into plan-specific expected actions versus observed actions without importing real-world emergency doctrine wholesale.

### National Park Service trip and contingency planning

Source: U.S. National Park Service, “Trip Plan.”
URL: https://www.nps.gov/articles/gtgtripplan.htm

Reusable structure: intended route, expected return, backup plan, and trusted-contact handoff are useful precisely because they are incomplete and can become stale when plans change. This is compatible with the existing Ouros field-search model. Preparedness can create an expectation record; a missed expectation still requires verification before a missing-person case is opened.

Reusable structure: equipment, contingency supplies, route information, communication limits, and self-rescue assumptions belong to preparation. Ouros can use the structure for mundane readiness jobs without importing NPS jurisdiction, legal standards, or rescue policy.

## Reusable design lessons for Ouros

Preparedness should be specific to a plan version, location, assumptions, participants, communication path, access state, fallback route, and available resources. A visible plan can become stale. A drill can succeed in one objective and expose a different gap. A designated muster point can become unsafe under the actual event. A cache can physically exist while some contents are unavailable, borrowed, expired, or damaged.

The strongest recurring loop is PLAN -> REHEARSE -> OBSERVE -> AFTER-ACTION ITEM -> OWNER -> CORRECTIVE WORK -> PLAN REVISION -> RETEST. This loop creates small jobs for ordinary residents and makes later incidents feel earned because the world remembers what it practiced.

Preparedness should also preserve accessibility and companion-Pokémon needs. A route usable by one resident may not be practical for another resident, a large Pokémon, an injured companion, a cart, or fragile records. That can create a legitimate revision without requiring a disaster or villain.

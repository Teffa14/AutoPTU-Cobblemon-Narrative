## Source A — NOAA Ocean Exploration: maritime heritage

URL: https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/maritime-heritage/
Published: 2024-12-03; updated 2024-12-04.
Retrieved: 2026-09-07.

High-level evidence:

Maritime heritage includes archaeological sites and artifacts, working waterfronts, landscapes, archival knowledge and cultural practices. Shipwrecks can become ecological habitat while remaining finite historical resources. NOAA describes in-situ study as common and identifies storms, currents, corrosion, looting, salvage and irresponsible activity as threats. Some wrecks can also create environmental hazards.

Reusable Ouros lessons:

- A wreck can simultaneously be a historical site, ecological habitat, navigation feature and hazard source.
- Those dimensions require separate observations and decisions.
- `HISTORICAL_VALUE != CURRENT_ECOLOGICAL_VALUE != CURRENT_HAZARD_STATE`.
- Discovering a site creates stewardship questions rather than an automatic loot table.
- A later ecological use of a ruin does not erase the material history that produced it.

Do not import United States law, sanctuary status, real vessels, accidents or management agencies.

## Source B — NOAA National Marine Protected Areas Center: underwater archaeology

URL: https://marineprotectedareas.noaa.gov/toolkit/underwater-archaeology.html
Retrieved: 2026-09-07.

High-level evidence:

The source emphasizes systematic documentation of submerged sites and the information carried by the exact distribution of artifacts. Investigation can be non-intrusive or intrusive. Context, spatial relationships and multidisciplinary evidence matter because removing an artifact can destroy information that existed in its position relative to the rest of the site.

Reusable Ouros lessons:

- Object identity and site context must be modeled separately.
- `OBJECT_RECOVERABLE != OBJECT_SHOULD_BE_RECOVERED`.
- `ARTIFACT_IDENTITY != SITE_INTERPRETATION`.
- A site map can be evidence with provenance, revision history and uncertainty rather than a universal truth layer.
- Recovery should be an authored world decision with custody lineage, not a Minecraft pickup event.

## Source C — National Park Service: underwater sites

URL: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/oceans/underwater-sites.htm
Last updated: 2025-12-11.
Retrieved: 2026-09-07.

High-level evidence:

NPS describes submerged sites as finite archaeological resources often studied through careful documentation while leaving artifacts intact. The useful design principle is that discovery can lead to repeated observation and interpretation instead of immediate extraction.

Reusable Ouros lessons:

- An underwater discovery can support a revisitable location whose progression comes from better evidence rather than depleted treasure containers.
- A successful expedition may end with a survey, map revision or access decision rather than item recovery.
- Later technology, access, weather or expertise can expose new evidence at the same persistent feature.

## Source D — NPS Shipwreck Tagging Archaeological Management Program

URL: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/shipwreck-tagging-archaeological-management-program.htm
Retrieved: 2026-09-07.

High-level evidence:

NPS documents wreck timbers that can detach, wash ashore, become buried or exposed again, return to sea and travel substantial distances under waves and wind. Monitoring those pieces helps researchers understand changing sites.

This is the strongest structural inspiration for Pass 334.

Reusable Ouros lessons:

- A detached component may be authentic while its current location is unrelated to the original site location.
- `DETACHED_OBJECT_LOCATION != ORIGINAL_SITE_LOCATION`.
- Shoreline evidence can point toward a submerged history without revealing it directly.
- Repeated observations of one object can form a movement history.
- A recovered fragment can be useful even when the parent site remains unknown.

This differs from Pass 333 ghost gear. The object here is evidence about a historical material assemblage and site formation, not active equipment whose current function continues after loss.

## Original synthesis for Ouros

A submerged-site system should model at least these distinct facts:

1. persistent site identity, if a coherent site has actually been established;
2. persistent object/component identity;
3. observed object position at a semantic time;
4. relationship claim between an object and a possible parent site;
5. confidence and provenance for that claim;
6. historical archive evidence;
7. ecological occupancy/use observations;
8. hazard or deterioration observations;
9. access/stewardship decisions;
10. recovery authorization, if Ouros later canonizes such authority;
11. custody and conservation state after any recovery;
12. explicit publication and per-NPC receipt.

Core inference boundaries:

`OBJECT_FOUND != WRECK_FOUND`

`OBJECT_AUTHENTICATED != PARENT_SITE_IDENTIFIED`

`DETACHED_OBJECT_LOCATION != ORIGINAL_SITE_LOCATION`

`ARCHIVE_MATCH != COMPLETE_HISTORY`

`SITE_DISCOVERED != SITE_SAFE_TO_ENTER`

`SITE_ACCESSIBLE != RECOVERY_AUTHORIZED`

`RECOVERED_OBJECT != INTERPRETATION_PROVEN`

`MINECRAFT_PICKUP != CANONICAL_RECOVERY`

## Candidate narrative shape

A storm exposes or deposits an old structural timber or fitting near Puerto Bruma. Its construction details appear compatible with one or more Tideglass records, but no current evidence establishes where the parent vessel/site lies or even whether the object came from a vessel rather than old waterfront infrastructure.

The player can document the object, compare archives, interview residents, inspect later drift/exposure observations and support a non-intrusive survey. A coherent submerged debris field may become one possible later branch, but it is not canonized by this research note.

The strongest mystery is provenance reconstruction, not treasure recovery.

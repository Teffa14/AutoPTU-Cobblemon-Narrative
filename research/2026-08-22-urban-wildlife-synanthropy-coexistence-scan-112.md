# Research Scan — Urban Wildlife, Synanthropy & Coexistence — Pass 112

Status: RESEARCH / PROVENANCE ONLY. Not Ouros canon. Not a PTU rules source.

## Why this pass exists

Repository-wide inventory review found strong existing layers for Urban Public Space, Waste/Sanitation, Light, Soundscapes, Road Ecology, Architecture, Tourism, Conservation, Diel Activity and Wild Pokémon ecology. None owns the persistent relationship between wild Pokémon and human-built environments: habitual feeding sites, roosts on structures, attraction to waste or power, learned tolerance of people, repeated conflict sites, and coexistence measures.

This pass therefore studies urban wildlife as a long-lived relationship among individual Pokémon/populations, human resources, structures, routines and management responses. It does not create a generic `nuisance` species tag.

## Sources and reusable structures

### Pokémon official Pokédex — Pidove

Source: https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokedex/pidove

Relevant high-level pattern: Pidove follow human activity and can aggregate in very large numbers when people scatter food. This supports a causal chain where a small repeated human behavior changes local aggregation without requiring a villain, rare spawn or ecological collapse.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- distinguish ordinary urban presence from feeding-driven aggregation;
- record the attractant behavior separately from the population itself;
- a crowd of Pokémon at one plaza does not prove regional population growth;
- mitigation can target the human-side attractant before targeting the Pokémon.

Do not import exact flock sizes as simulation parameters.

### Pokémon official Pokédex — Squawkabilly

Source: https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokedex/squawkabilly

Relevant high-level pattern: Squawkabilly explicitly prefer cities, form groups, search for food around towns and can have territorial relationships among groups. This gives a canon Pokémon precedent for an urban-adapted population with social structure rather than treating every city Pokémon as displaced wilderness fauna.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- some populations may genuinely use city habitat as their normal habitat;
- neighborhood-level group identity can persist across observations;
- territorial conflict can occur among Pokémon groups without implying hostility toward humans;
- different groups of the same species may use different districts.

Do not copy plumage-group politics into original Ouros populations unless the species canon itself requires it.

### Pokémon official Pokédex — Joltik

Source: https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokedex/joltik

Relevant high-level pattern: city-dwelling Joltik can learn to draw electricity from household outlets. This is an unusually clear precedent for behavior learned in response to built infrastructure.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- an urban population can develop a local infrastructure interaction without gaining a universal species power;
- recurring outages near buildings can become an investigation involving Technology + Wildlife observations;
- the same species elsewhere may behave differently;
- infrastructure mitigation and habitat/coexistence measures can be more appropriate than combat.

Do not infer device damage, electrical drain rates, EMP effects or battle mechanics.

### Pokémon official Pokédex — Dustox

Source: https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokedex/dustox

Relevant high-level pattern: Dustox can be drawn toward bright city lights and feed on roadside vegetation. This directly connects Lightscape state to an urban wildlife response.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- lighting changes can alter wildlife distribution without changing the underlying regional population;
- a municipal lighting retrofit can become an ecological experiment with before/after observations;
- a vegetation-loss complaint may involve lighting, seasonality and feeding behavior together;
- an observed association does not grant a tactical light weakness.

### Pokémon official Pokédex — Trubbish

Source: https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokedex/trubbish

Relevant high-level pattern: Trubbish can establish itself around places where sanitation is poor. This connects Waste/Sanitation to habitat use without proving that every Trubbish observation means contamination or that Trubbish caused the waste problem.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- waste availability can be an attractant/habitat variable;
- sanitation-service changes can change urban Pokémon observations over time;
- residents may misread a visible Pokémon as the cause rather than a responder;
- cleanup should not erase the Pokémon's identity or history.

### Pokémon Tabletop official GM advice — first PTU session

Source: https://pokemontabletop.com/gm-advice-your-first-ptu-session/

Relevant high-level pattern: PTU encounter advice explicitly allows urban environments to matter and emphasizes that wild Pokémon do not necessarily fight until one side is completely knocked out. The page uses an abandoned urban building as an example of environmental interaction.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- urban-wildlife encounters can have withdrawal, territorial, retrieve-object or de-escalation endings;
- the physical city should matter to encounter framing;
- using environment as narrative context must not make Minecraft invent unsupported PTU hazards;
- low-stakes wild encounters should preserve reasons for the Pokémon to leave or stop.

### NPS — wildlife in cities / Nature Neighbor

Sources:

- https://www.nps.gov/samo/learn/education/classrooms/national-park-service-legacy-nature-neighbor-k-2-lesson.htm
- https://www.nps.gov/samo/learn/education/classrooms/national-park-service-legacy-nature-neighbor-3-4-lesson.htm
- https://www.nps.gov/subjects/watchingwildlife/animals.htm

Relevant high-level patterns:

- human food, pet food, unsecured trash and compost can become attractants;
- conflict reduction can often act on the attractant instead of directly on wildlife;
- food conditioning and habituation are related but distinct concepts;
- urban habitat can support wildlife even when animals still avoid or only selectively tolerate people;
- observation effort and conflict reports are not the same thing as population size.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

Create separate state for:

1. urban habitat use;
2. tolerance/habituation observations;
3. food-conditioning evidence;
4. attractant availability;
5. conflict events;
6. management/coexistence actions;
7. later observations of whether behavior changed.

Do not import real-world lethal-control policy, legal standards or disease assumptions into Ouros.

### NPS — urban nesting and habituation

Source: https://home.nps.gov/nebe/learn/management/compendium.htm

Relevant pattern: dense built environments can contain active nests on roofs, ledges, trees, gardens and signs; apparently abandoned young may still be under parental care. Habituation can change how wildlife responds to people.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- a Pokémon juvenile found alone in a city must not automatically become a rescue/capture candidate;
- roost/nest identity should persist separately from current occupant presence;
- building maintenance can conflict with a seasonal nesting window;
- absence of an adult at one observation does not prove abandonment.

### USDA APHIS — conflict management methods

Source: https://direct.aphis.usda.gov/wildlife-services/publications/nepa/methods-risk-assessments

Relevant high-level pattern: conflict management can include surveys, prevention, deterrence and habitat/landscape changes, with methods chosen for a specific context.

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- a coexistence response should be a recorded intervention with target, scope and follow-up;
- deterrence should not be a universal success button;
- different Pokémon/populations can respond differently;
- intervention outcome needs monitoring rather than assumption.

No real-world control technique is imported automatically into Ouros.

## Design synthesis for Ouros

The useful causal graph is:

`BUILT ENVIRONMENT + HUMAN ROUTINES + RESOURCE/ATTRACTANT STATE + LOCAL ECOLOGY -> URBAN POKÉMON OBSERVATIONS -> BEHAVIORAL HYPOTHESIS -> CONFLICT/COEXISTENCE RESPONSE -> FOLLOW-UP OBSERVATION`

Important separations:

- urban-adapted does not mean domesticated;
- habituated does not mean friendly;
- food-conditioned does not mean aggressive;
- nuisance report does not prove ecological harm;
- one conflict does not define the species;
- repeated presence does not imply ownership by residents;
- feeding does not establish partnership;
- visible abundance at an attractant does not prove population growth;
- juveniles near buildings are not automatically abandoned;
- a wild Pokémon using infrastructure does not grant it a job or institutional role.

## Mechanical guardrails

No source reviewed here proves a new PTU mechanic for urban coexistence.

Do not infer:

- city habitat -> Urban Terrain;
- flock -> Pack Mon;
- habituation -> lower Evasion or capture bonus;
- feeding -> Loyalty/Friendship;
- food conditioning -> aggression AI;
- roost -> defensive buff;
- Joltik near outlet -> electric hazard;
- Dustox near lights -> Accuracy penalty or mandatory movement;
- Trubbish near waste -> Poisoned zone;
- Pidove crowd -> swarm mechanics;
- resident complaints -> hostile encounter;
- deterrent -> status effect;
- successful battle -> conflict solved.

PTU/Caelo mechanics remain authoritative when their exact text is available. No reliable project-local Caelo source was recovered for this pass, so no urban-wildlife mechanical rule is asserted from Caelo.

## Candidate design direction

A dedicated `urban-wildlife-synanthropy-coexistence` layer is justified. It should own persistent urban population-use profiles, attractants, roost/nest sites, habituation/conditioning observations, conflict incidents, coexistence interventions and follow-up outcomes, while delegating physical structures, waste, lighting, roads, schedules and mechanical battle effects to their existing authorities.

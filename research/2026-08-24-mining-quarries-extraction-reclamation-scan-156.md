# Mining, Quarries, Extraction & Reclamation Research Scan — Pass 156

Status: RESEARCH / PROVENANCE ONLY. Not Ouros canon.
Date: 2026-08-24

## Why this pass

The repository already has owners for caves, geology-linked hazards, contaminated land, manufacturing, supply chains, worker associations, land tenure, groundwater, freshwater, archaeology, paleontology and remediation. However, `subterranean-karst-cave-ecology-layer.md` explicitly references Mining as an external authority, while no dedicated Mining layer exists in the current design inventory.

This pass therefore studies the operational life of mines and quarries: exploration, extraction, material movement, stockpiles/waste rock/tailings, processing handoff, care-and-maintenance, closure, progressive rehabilitation, post-closure monitoring and later reuse.

The intended Ouros layer must not take ownership away from:

- Subterranean Systems for natural cave identity and underground geometry;
- Geology for geologic interpretation and resource occurrence;
- Paleontology for fossil locality context;
- Manufacturing for repeatable production processes;
- Supply Chains for inventory and freight after material becomes stock;
- Worker Associations and Workplaces for labor organizations and staffing;
- Contaminated Land/Toxicology for contamination and exposure;
- Groundwater/Freshwater for hydrology;
- Land Tenure for access/use authority;
- Conservation for habitat outcomes;
- Architecture/Technology for structures and machines;
- PTU/Caelo/AutoPTU for battle mechanics.

## High-value Pokémon sources

### Oreburgh Mine / Roark — occupation, excavation, fossils and civic identity

Source: Pokémon.com, “Shapes of Things to Come”
https://www.pokemon.com/us/animation/seasons/10/episode-15-shapes-of-things-to-come

The official episode summary identifies Roark as both Oreburgh Gym Leader and mining foreman, actively excavating coal ore and fossils. A fossil discovery can then move into the museum/restoration system.

Reusable structure for Ouros:

mine operation -> unexpected scientific find -> documented custody/context -> specialist handoff -> public/museum consequences

Design lesson: a mine should be a workplace and persistent civic institution before it is a dungeon. Resource extraction, fossils, workers, transportation and competitive institutions can intersect without becoming one system.

Do not copy Roark, Oreburgh, Team Rocket or the fossil-restoration plot.

### Oreburgh after the find — the mine remains useful after the discovery

Source: Pokémon.com, “O’er the Rampardos We Watched”
https://www.pokemon.com/uk/animation/seasons/10/episode-18-oer-the-rampardos-we-watched

Ash later uses the mine as a training location while the museum has a separate fossil crisis. This is useful because the same industrial landscape supports multiple later functions.

Reusable structure:

persistent industrial site -> different actors reuse different parts -> previous events remain part of place history

For Ouros, a working mine, closed adit, reclaimed bench, haul road or former workshop can remain relevant to training, research, travel, public memory or habitat years later.

## Fangame / game-design patterns

### Mine as a reusable region rather than one-shot dungeon

Pokémon fangames repeatedly use mines/quarries as route connectors, industrial history, puzzle spaces and later revisitable locations. The reusable lesson is structural rather than plot-specific:

- working areas and abandoned areas can coexist;
- maintenance corridors create alternative navigation;
- extraction changes geometry over time;
- shutdowns can redirect travel/economy;
- reclaimed sectors can acquire ecological or community functions;
- old maps can remain historically correct while no longer matching the current workings.

Ouros should avoid copying named fangame mines, puzzle layouts, villain organizations or scripted bosses wholesale.

## Mining lifecycle / reclamation research

### Mine lifecycle includes exploration before operation and monitoring after closure

Source: Natural Resources Canada, “Mining and mineral resource development in Canada”
https://natural-resources.canada.ca/minerals-mining/canadian-mineral-exploration

The source describes exploration, development, operation, closure and possible post-closure measures as a long process. A resource discovery is not equivalent to a mine opening.

Reusable Ouros state machine:

regional geoscience -> exploration program -> candidate deposit -> feasibility/decision -> development -> active operation -> care & maintenance OR closure -> rehabilitation -> post-closure monitoring -> later land use

Do not import Canadian permitting, time estimates or legislation.

### Exploration does not guarantee mining

Source: NSW Resources, “What happens during exploration and rehabilitation on my land?”
https://www.resources.nsw.gov.au/mining-and-exploration/information-for-landholders/landholders-guide/exploration-and-rehab

Exploration gathers information about location, size and quality of mineral resources; discovery does not guarantee later extraction.

Ouros implication: prospecting and drilling can produce years of maps, samples and public expectations before a project is approved, rejected, deferred or abandoned.

### Progressive rehabilitation can happen while other parts still operate

Sources:
- British Columbia, “Mining life cycle and permitting”
  https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/industry/mineral-exploration-mining/permitting
- Northern Territory, “Closing and rehabilitating a mine site”
  https://nt.gov.au/industry/mining/applications-and-processes/closing-rehabilitating-mine

Both sources emphasize closure as part of the lifecycle and rehabilitation as something that can occur progressively. A site may also enter care-and-maintenance rather than permanent closure.

Reusable structure:

one mine site -> active pit/adit + reclaimed bench + maintenance-only facility + closed workings + monitoring zone at the same time

This is valuable for Minecraft because the whole site does not need a binary ACTIVE/CLOSED state.

### Closure criteria depend on intended post-mining use

Sources:
- NSW Planning, “Mine rehabilitation”
  https://www.planning.nsw.gov.au/the-planning-system/mining-and-resources/mine-rehabilitation
- South Australia, “Mine closure and progressive rehabilitation”
  https://www.energymining.sa.gov.au/industry/minerals-and-mining/mining/mine-closure-and-progressive-rehabilitation

Rehabilitation can include infrastructure removal, safe/stable landforms, revegetation and future use. Closure planning increasingly develops across the life of the project.

Ouros implication: “reclaimed” must be assessed against authored objectives. A former quarry converted to habitat, public recreation, industrial reuse or research access may need different completion evidence.

### Legacy mine problems can emerge or be reclassified years later

Sources:
- Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement, “Reclaiming Abandoned Mine Lands”
  https://www.osmre.gov/programs/reclaiming-abandoned-mine-lands
- OSMRE, Abandoned Mine Land Inventory System
  https://www.osmre.gov/programs/e-amlis

The public inventory model records abandoned-mine problems, newly identified features and reclaimed features over time.

Reusable Ouros structure:

legacy working -> inventory observation -> hazard/environmental assessment -> priority decision -> reclamation project -> follow-up -> possible new observation

Do not import U.S. program categories, fees or legal responsibility.

### Reclamation does not imply immediate environmental recovery

Source: USGS, “Effects of surface coal-mine reclamation on stream quality…”
https://www.usgs.gov/publications/effects-surface-coal-mine-reclamation-stream-quality-a-small-watershed-near

The study found limited chemical improvement during the short observation window after reclamation and explicitly notes that a longer monitoring period may be needed.

Ouros implication: earthworks complete -> physical stabilization observed -> hydrologic/chemical/ecological response measured later. These are distinct states.

### Historical mining impacts can move into water and sediment systems

Source: USGS, “Quality of water and sediment in streams affected by historical mining…”
https://www.usgs.gov/publications/quality-water-and-sediment-streams-affected-historical-mining-and-quality-mine

The study sampled streams, sediment and mine tailings to investigate impacts of historical mines.

Ouros implication: mine operation history can create hypotheses for Freshwater/Groundwater/Soil/Contaminated Land, but Mining does not itself decide contamination or exposure.

## Original design lessons extracted for Ouros

1. A mine is a long-lived institution and landscape, not only an underground dungeon.
2. Exploration, discovery, approval, extraction, processing, closure and reclamation require separate state.
3. The resource body belongs to Geology; the extraction project belongs to Mining.
4. A mine can be simultaneously active, closed and reclaimed in different sectors.
5. Care-and-maintenance is a first-class state; shutdown is not always abandonment.
6. Material identity should be preserved from extraction batch to stockpile/processor handoff where useful.
7. Waste rock, overburden and tailings are distinct from saleable ore/material and must not be treated as generic “junk blocks.”
8. Mining-induced contamination is a hypothesis until the environmental authority confirms it.
9. Wildlife occupancy can increase after closure without proving the site is safe for all uses.
10. Rehabilitation success must be assessed against explicit objectives and follow-up observations.
11. Old mine maps can be correct for their date while unsafe or incomplete today.
12. A collapse, water inflow or wild-Pokémon incident does not automatically prove operator negligence.
13. Workers can have high-value longitudinal observations without becoming omniscient experts.
14. Fossils discovered during extraction hand off to Paleontology; they are not mining loot by default.
15. Pokémon helping at a mine retain agency and individual qualification requirements.
16. Minecraft block removal cannot directly create authoritative production or reclamation records.
17. Closing a mine can create economic, social, transport and ecological stories that last longer than the operation itself.
18. Reuse can preserve industrial memory: trail, museum, research site, habitat, storage, workshop district or transport corridor.
19. Some years should be operationally normal; those baseline years make later change legible.
20. FULL tactical mine encounters must not outsource unsupported collapse, carts, conveyors, gas, falling, blasting, water, dust or forced-movement rules to Minecraft.

## Public source findings

### Darkness is functional habitat, not visual decoration

The U.S. National Park Service treats natural lightscape as an ecological resource. Natural light-dark patterns can cue navigation, concealment, foraging, reproduction and other behavior. Artificial light can alter these patterns and therefore change how animals use habitat.

Sources:

- National Park Service, `Protecting Natural Lightscape`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://home.nps.gov/articles/natural-lightscape.htm
- National Park Service, `Night Skies: A Natural Resource`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://home.nps.gov/subjects/nightskies/natural.htm
- National Park Service, `Animals Need the Dark`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://home.nps.gov/articles/nocturnal_earthnight.htm

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- night state can change what evidence is available without changing the underlying population instantly;
- artificial illumination can alter route choice, activity or signal visibility without proving injury or disappearance;
- mitigation can be spatial, directional or temporal rather than simply destroying infrastructure.

No real-world lux values, wavelength thresholds or biological injury thresholds are imported as Pokémon rules.

### Attraction and avoidance can coexist at different scales

USGS research on nocturnally migrating birds reports attraction toward bright areas at broad scale while showing reduced use very close to bright sources. Separate controlled work found that even low-rise downward-facing lights can alter nearby flight paths. This is useful because it prevents a simplistic `light attracts wildlife` rule.

Sources:

- U.S. Geological Survey, `Artificial light at night confounds broad-scale habitat use by migrating birds`, 2018: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/artificial-light-night-confounds-broad-scale-habitat-use-migrating-birds
- U.S. Geological Survey, `Potential effect of low-rise, downcast artificial lights on nocturnally migrating land birds`, 2021: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/potential-effect-low-rise-downcast-artificial-lights-nocturnally-migrating-land-birds

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- an illuminated structure can attract some movement while making the immediately adjacent habitat less usable;
- observation from only one distance can support the wrong interpretation;
- a quest should compare positions and times before assigning a causal story.

This does not authorize transferring bird-navigation mechanisms to Pokémon species without species-specific support.

### Artificial light can change detection as well as behavior

NPS material also notes glare, sky glow and reduced dark adaptation. That distinction matters for mystery design: an observer may fail to detect a faint biological visual signal because the background became brighter even if the Pokémon did not alter its behavior.

Source:

- National Park Service, `Light Pollution - Night Skies`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nightskies/lightpollution.htm

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- preserve raw observation separately from the ecological interpretation;
- `signal not detected` must not be equivalent to `signal not produced`;
- compare observations from shielded and illuminated viewpoints before concluding that behavior changed.

The official Pokémon Pokédex describes Illumise directing Volbeat into luminous aerial patterns whose meaning is studied by researchers. This supports a Pokémon-specific precedent for repeated visual signals that can be observed and interpreted without making all bioluminescent Pokémon equivalent.

Source:

- The Pokémon Company, official Pokédex, `Illumise`, accessed 2026-09-06: https://www.pokemon.com/it/pokedex/illumise

Reusable Ouros lesson:

- a visual pattern can be evidence with unknown meaning rather than an automatic translated message;
- signal visibility and signal production should remain separable;

This research does not place Illumise or Volbeat in Ouros and does not copy their exact signaling ecology into a new species.

## Reusable evidence model

A future nocturnal-observation system should keep these facts separate:

- physical presence evidence;
- authored ambient light state;
- biological or environmental signal production, when source-backed;
- observer position and observation window;
- detected signal;
- secondary evidence such as tracks, feeding traces or silhouettes;
- interpretation claim and confidence;
- infrastructure schedule or light-state change;
- provenance for every observation and conclusion.

The world simulator may know authored light state. An ordinary NPC should only know what they observed or were told.

## Proposed narrative structures

### The missing pattern

A repeated nocturnal visual pattern is no longer visible from a traditional observation ridge after new work lights are installed. The first question is whether the signal stopped, became harder to see, shifted location/time, or the population actually changed.

### Shielded viewpoint comparison

Two observation points share line of sight to the same habitat but have different exposure to glare. The player compares simultaneous or closely timed observations. A signal visible only from the shielded point supports a detection problem without proving that illumination has no ecological effect.

### Controlled lighting experiment

An operator can dim or shield a subset of lights for one agreed observation window. Reappearance of a signal strengthens a masking/detection explanation. A change in movement or activity may instead suggest a behavioral response. One trial should not become universal causal proof.

### False correlation

Lights and reduced activity begin on the same week, but another change also occurred: maintenance, weather, predator presence, construction traffic, vegetation loss or observer relocation. The scenario should retain concurrent explanations until evidence distinguishes them.
